Anchored on Better Place Inc. — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Present total lifetime funding
as "approximately $850 million" — never state one single precise, fully-reconciled round-by-round
total, since individually disclosed rounds only sum to roughly $686M against the commonly cited
$850M headline figure. Peak valuation ($2.25 billion, November 2011) is well-corroborated and safe
to state precisely. Total vehicles deployed is reported inconsistently ("fewer than 1,500" vs.
"950") — use the hedged range, never one precise figure. Keep the October 2012 "$490M lost since
founding" figure (at Shai Agassi's ouster) explicitly distinct from the May 2013 "lost more than
$800M of $850M raised" figure (at the bankruptcy filing) — different points in time, never conflate.
The swap-station cost overrun ($500K budgeted vs. ~$2M actual) is reasonably well-sourced and safe
to state. The 2012 "$6.9M revenue vs. $459M loss" figure is single-sourced to PrivCo, a private
financial-research firm reporting on obtained documents, not an audited public filing — attribute it
explicitly to PrivCo, never present it as independently confirmed. CRITICAL SEQUENCING: the July
2013 "Sunrise Group" deal for approximately $12 million NEVER CLOSED — it was canceled by the court
after the buyer failed to make its first payment. The real, final sale was in November 2013, to
Gnrgy Ltd., for $450,000. Never present the $12 million figure as the actual ending. No fraud or
criminal allegation exists anywhere in this story — never use "fraud/scam/lied" language; this is a
capital-intensity and single-automaker-dependency failure story.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 14–16 minutes (infrastructure/capital-intensity explainer plus a dated,
filing-level collapse timeline)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "In November 2011, this company was valued at $2.25 billion. In November 2013, it sold for
  $450,000. In between, it raised approximately $850 million, and it still wasn't enough."
- Visual: "NOV 2011 — $2.25B VALUATION" text, hard cut to "MAY 2013 — LIQUIDATION FILED," hard cut to
  "NOV 2013 — SOLD FOR $450,000," a thin timeline connecting all three
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This is not a fraud story. No one was
  ever criminally charged. It's still one of the biggest startup failures in Israeli tech history."

### Hook (0:15–0:55)
- VO: "This is Better Place — a company that tried to solve one of the real, structural problems with
  electric cars: what do you do about the battery? It raised approximately $850 million, built a
  network of robotic stations that could swap a depleted battery for a charged one in minutes, and
  reached a $2.25 billion valuation. Its entire business still depended on exactly one car, from
  exactly one automaker."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how the battery-swap model was
  supposed to work, why depending on a single exclusive partner became the company's core
  vulnerability, and how a $2.25 billion company ended up selling for less than the price of an
  apartment."

### Introduction (0:55–1:45)
- Method statement: reconstructed from company-funding coverage (TechCrunch, Fast Company, Greentech
  Media, WardsAuto), retrospective reporting (Quartz, MIT Technology Review, an academic
  ScienceDirect study), and Israeli bankruptcy/asset-sale coverage (Times of Israel, Global
  Venturing, Gas 2, Green Prophet) — sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this isn't a fraud story — no individual, including founder Shai Agassi, has
  been criminally charged with anything in this video's sourcing — it's a story about capital-
  intensive infrastructure and what happens when a company's entire model depends on one partner
  saying yes
- Direct cross-reference: "This channel has covered hardware and infrastructure moonshots before —
  Bird's physical scooter fleet, Virgin Orbit's rocket-launch bet. This one is different from both:
  it's a fixed physical NETWORK, built specifically around one car, from one manufacturer."

### Main Content — The Better Place Case (1:45–11:30)

- **Segment 1 — The Founder and the Idea (1:45–3:00):** founded in 2007 by Shai Agassi, a former SAP
  executive widely reported to have been in line for a future SAP CEO role before leaving to start
  this company. The idea: instead of plugging in an EV to charge — a slow process — a driver would
  pull into a robotic swap station and have a depleted battery automatically exchanged for a fully
  charged one in a few minutes, faster than filling a gas tank. Better Place would own the batteries,
  the single most expensive component of any EV, and sell "miles" to drivers on a subscription basis,
  similar to a mobile-phone plan — removing both the upfront battery cost and range anxiety as EV
  adoption barriers. Visual: founder graphic, a simple robotic-swap-station animation.

- **Segment 2 — The Raise and the Backers (3:00–4:30):** approximately $850 million raised over the
  company's lifetime — individual disclosed rounds included a $111 million Series A in 2007 (led by
  Lend Lease Ventures) plus a $25 million second tranche, a $350 million round in January 2010 (led
  by Morgan Stanley), and a $200 million Series C in November 2011. On-screen disclosure: these
  individually disclosed rounds only sum to roughly $686 million against the commonly cited $850
  million lifetime total — present as "approximately $850 million raised over its lifetime," never
  one single fully-reconciled figure. Key backers: Israel Corp (the largest single investor), HSBC,
  and General Electric. The November 2011 round pushed the company's valuation to a peak of $2.25
  billion. Visual: funding-round timeline graphic building toward "approximately $850M," with an
  explicit "not fully reconciled" callout; valuation climbing to $2.25B.

- **Segment 3 — The Single-Partner Dependency (4:30–6:00):** the entire swap-station network depended
  on exactly one compatible vehicle: the Renault Fluence Z.E., a car Renault built specifically with
  a battery pack designed to be removed and replaced by Better Place's automated machinery. On-screen
  disclosure, careful attribution: reporting (Quartz's retrospective) states the company was unable
  to secure a second automaker partner beyond Renault — present this as "Better Place could not
  secure a second automaker partner," not as this channel's own conclusion about why. Visual: a
  hub-and-spoke diagram — one car, one manufacturer, several other manufacturer icons greyed out and
  disconnected around it.

- **Segment 4 — Building the Network (6:00–7:30):** launched in Israel (chosen for its small
  geographic size, making a nationwide swap-station network logistically feasible) and Denmark
  (helped by EV tax incentives). First customer deliveries began in Israel in early 2012 (roughly 100
  units in January 2012). On-screen disclosure: total vehicles deployed across both markets is
  reported inconsistently — "fewer than 1,500 vehicles combined, with some sources citing as few as
  roughly 950" — present the hedged range, never one precise figure. Separately: each robotic
  swap station was originally budgeted at approximately $500,000 to build; actual stations ended up
  costing approximately $2 million each — a roughly 4x cost overrun on the company's core physical
  infrastructure. Visual: Israel/Denmark map graphic, swap-station cost-overrun bar comparison
  ($500K budgeted vs. $2M actual).

- **Segment 5 — The Real Numbers (7:30–8:45):** on-screen disclosure, single-sourced and attributed
  explicitly: financial research firm PrivCo reported that Better Place lost $459 million in 2012
  alone on just $6.9 million in sales that year, ending 2012 with only $34.4 million cash on hand
  against $915 million in total liabilities. State clearly on screen: "Reported by PrivCo, a private
  financial-research firm working from obtained documents — not independently verified against
  Better Place's own audited public filings in this video's sourcing." Visual: "$6.9M in sales / $459M
  lost" side-by-side graphic, clearly labeled "per PrivCo reporting."

- **Segment 6 — The Ouster (8:45–9:45):** October 2, 2012, Shai Agassi resigned/was replaced as
  worldwide CEO, replaced by Evan Thornley, previously CEO of Better Place's Australia unit. Agassi
  resigned from the company's board about a week later. At the time, the company was reported to have
  lost approximately $490 million cumulatively since its 2007 founding — explicitly distinct from the
  later, larger cumulative-loss figure at the bankruptcy filing seven months later; do not conflate
  the two. Within days of Thornley's appointment, the company asked its investors for an emergency
  funding round of approximately $100 million. Visual: leadership-change graphic, "$490M lost since
  founding — as of Oct 2012" callout, explicitly time-stamped to avoid confusion with the later figure.

- **Segment 7 — The Liquidation (9:45–10:45):** May 26, 2013, Better Place filed a motion for
  liquidation in an Israeli court — widely reported as one of the largest failures in Israeli tech
  history. On-screen disclosure: total cumulative loss at the time of filing is reported as either
  $812 million or $823 million of the roughly $850 million raised, across two different sources —
  present as "lost more than $800 million of the roughly $850 million it raised," never one precise
  figure. Reported root cause: the company would have needed an estimated additional $500 million and
  roughly four more years of operation to reach breakeven — investors were not willing to continue
  funding at that scale. Visual: Israeli court filing graphic, "$850M raised -> more than $800M lost"
  callout.

- **Segment 8 — Two Sales, Only One Real (10:45–11:30):** July 2013, an Israeli consortium referred
  to in reporting as the "Sunrise Group" initially agreed to buy Better Place's remaining assets for
  roughly $12 million total. State this explicitly and clearly: **this deal was subsequently canceled
  by the court after the Sunrise Group failed to make its first agreed payment — it never actually
  closed.** November 2013, the bankruptcy receivers instead sold the remaining assets — including
  approximately 1,800 charging stations across Israel and the company's patents — to a different
  Israeli energy startup, Gnrgy Ltd., led by Ran Eloya, for just $450,000. This is the real, final
  sale price. Visual: "DEAL 1 — $12M — CANCELED" graphic crossed out, followed by "DEAL 2 — $450,000 —
  CLOSED" as the actual ending.

- **Mid-video CTA (~11:00):** "If you haven't seen this channel's videos on Bird's scooter-fleet
  economics or Virgin Orbit's rocket-launch collapse, those are the closest companion pieces to this
  one — different mechanisms, same underlying lesson about capital-intensive physical bets. Subscribe
  for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson — Infrastructure Bets Need More Than One Partner (11:30–14:00)
- Return to the three-number arc one final time: approximately $850 million raised, a $2.25 billion
  peak valuation, a $450,000 final sale price. What actually happened in between: a genuinely real
  problem (EV range anxiety and battery cost) addressed with a genuinely working technology (the
  robotic swap stations worked), undermined by two structural risks — a single-automaker dependency
  that never widened to a second manufacturer, and infrastructure capital intensity (swap stations
  costing roughly 4x their original budget) that outpaced real-world adoption (fewer than 1,500
  vehicles deployed against a company built to support far more).
- Careful, sourced coda: battery swapping as a concept did not disappear from the EV industry after
  Better Place — the video should note this honestly but without overclaiming any specific successor
  company, since no successor-company research was verified in this package's sourcing pass; state
  simply that the broader concept was revisited elsewhere in the years since, in different forms, by
  different companies not covered in this video.
- The core, carefully-framed irony: Better Place solved the harder engineering problem — the
  robotic swap technology itself worked — and still failed on the more mundane, structural problem of
  needing more than one partner and more capital-efficient infrastructure than it ever achieved.
- Visual: three-number arc graphic ($850M -> $2.25B -> $450K) with each figure landing in sequence.

### Final CTA + Outro (14:00–16:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a genuinely working technology and a real problem to solve
  aren't enough on their own; an infrastructure bet this capital-intensive needed more than one
  partner and a faster path to real-world adoption than Better Place ever reached
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, including this channel's other hardware and
  infrastructure moonshots"
- Engagement prompt: "Had you heard of Better Place before this video? Do you think the idea was
  ahead of its time, or was the execution always going to fail? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent hardware/infrastructure autopsy (028/Bird or 031/Virgin Orbit) + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed three-number arc graphic ($850M raised -> $2.25B peak valuation ->
$450,000 final sale), then works backward into the founding, the single-automaker dependency, and
the infrastructure cost overrun in detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a genuine
diligence resource for anyone evaluating capital-intensive infrastructure or single-partner-
dependency business models.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the November 2011 Series C round and the $2.25 billion valuation in full — the legitimacy,
the "solving range anxiety" framing at the time — before revealing the single-car dependency, the
2012 real-numbers reveal, and the eventual two-stage ending (the canceled $12M deal, then the real
$450,000 sale). Slower pacing, holds the "what did it actually end up selling for" question as a
mini-mystery.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "3 things that killed Better Place, in order" — the single-automaker dependency, the
infrastructure cost overrun, and the capital-intensity/adoption-speed mismatch — rapid-fire through
each with the three-number arc as a recurring visual anchor. Strong Shorts-extraction potential,
especially the $2.25B-to-$450K numeral reveal and the canceled-deal-then-real-sale two-stage ending.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the funding-figure hedging in Segment 2 (the $686M-sums-to vs. $850M-cited gap)
  if it becomes a dry numbers lecture before the single-partner-dependency mechanism arrives
- **Fix:** keep the funding-hedging beat brief and visual (one clean on-screen callout), get to the
  Renault dependency and the real-numbers reveal as quickly as possible
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general EV-industry background if audience-retention data shows
  strong baseline familiarity with "why EVs had range anxiety" as a concept
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease "sold for $450,000" in the cold open (already done), hold
  the full canceled-deal-then-real-sale detail for Segment 8
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the three-number arc graphic visible in a corner throughout
  Segments 4 through 8, so the collapse thesis stays concrete across the whole video, not just its
  own dedicated segment
