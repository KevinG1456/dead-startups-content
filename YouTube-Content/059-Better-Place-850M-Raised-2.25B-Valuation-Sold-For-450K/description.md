## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Better
Place — founded in 2007 by Shai Agassi, a former SAP executive widely reported to have been in line
for a future SAP CEO role before leaving to start this company. Better Place set out to solve a real,
structural problem with electric cars: instead of plugging in to charge, a driver would pull into a
robotic station and have a depleted battery automatically swapped for a fully charged one in minutes.
Better Place would own the batteries and sell "miles" to drivers on a subscription basis, similar to
a mobile-phone plan.

The company raised approximately $850 million over its lifetime — backed by Israel Corp, HSBC,
General Electric, and Morgan Stanley among others — and reached a peak valuation of $2.25 billion in
November 2011. But its entire network depended on exactly one compatible vehicle: the Renault Fluence
Z.E., a car Renault built specifically for Better Place's swap stations. No second automaker ever
signed on.

Launched in Israel (chosen for its small geographic size) and Denmark, Better Place deployed fewer
than 1,500 vehicles combined — some sources cite as few as roughly 950 — against a company built to
support far more. Its robotic swap stations, meanwhile, cost roughly four times their original
budget: approximately $2 million each, against an initial $500,000 estimate. Financial-research firm
PrivCo later reported the company lost $459 million in 2012 alone on just $6.9 million in sales that
year.

Founder Shai Agassi was ousted as CEO on October 2, 2012, replaced by Evan Thornley. On May 26, 2013,
Better Place filed for liquidation in an Israeli court, having lost more than $800 million of what it
raised — widely reported as one of the largest failures in Israeli tech history. A July 2013 deal to
sell the company's remaining assets for approximately $12 million to a group referred to as the
"Sunrise Group" fell through when the buyer failed to make its first payment. The real, final sale,
in November 2013, was to a different Israeli startup, Gnrgy Ltd., for just $450,000 — less than two
years after the company's $2.25 billion valuation.

No fraud or criminal allegation exists anywhere in this story. This is a story about capital-
intensive infrastructure, single-partner dependency, and what happens when a genuinely working
technology still can't outrun its own cost structure and adoption speed.
