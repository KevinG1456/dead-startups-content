Sources Log — Package 059: Better Place
Filled in: 2026-08-16

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Better Place is this channel's first EV/battery-swap infrastructure moonshot — a hardware +
physical-network capital-intensity story, distinct from Bird (028)'s scooter-fleet story because
the core asset here is fixed infrastructure (robotic swap stations + a battery-owning subscription
model) tied to a single exclusive automaker partnership, not a mobile fleet. No fraud or criminal
allegations were found anywhere in this research pass — this is a pure capital-intensity /
single-partner-dependency / market-penetration failure story.

---

FOUNDING
- Founded 2007 by Shai Agassi, a former SAP executive (SAP board member, widely reported to have
  been in line for a future SAP CEO role before he left to start this company).
- Nominally headquartered in Palo Alto, California, but operations and top investors were centered
  in Israel (Agassi is Israeli; the largest backer, Israel Corp, is an Israeli conglomerate).
Source: Wikipedia "Better Place (company)"; Grokipedia "Shai Agassi"; multiple corroborating
company-history summaries.

BUSINESS MODEL
- Battery-swap network for electric vehicles: instead of plugging in to charge, a driver would pull
  into a robotic swap station and have a depleted battery pack automatically swapped for a fully
  charged one in a few minutes — faster than refueling a gas car.
- Better Place would own the batteries (the most expensive single EV component) and sell "miles" to
  drivers on a subscription basis, similar to a mobile-phone service plan — designed to remove both
  the upfront battery cost and "range anxiety" as EV adoption barriers.
Source: Wikipedia; IEEE Spectrum ("How Is This A Good Idea?: EV Battery Swapping"); Yale e360.

FUNDING — reconciled with a flagged gap
- Total lifetime funding most commonly cited: approximately $850 million. Individual rounds found:
  $111M Series A (2007, led by Lend Lease Ventures) + a $25M second Series A tranche; $350M round
  (Jan 2010, led by Morgan Stanley); $200M Series C (Nov 2011). *** [VERIFY]: these individual round
  figures sum to roughly $686M, not the full $850M commonly cited — the gap is not fully reconciled
  across sources found in this pass (likely additional smaller rounds/debt not individually broken
  out). Other figures found in secondary sources: "$800M+" (TechCrunch headline), "nearly $900M"
  (secondary blog), "$815 million lost" (Quartz). *** Use "approximately $850 million raised over
  its lifetime" as the safe, hedged headline figure — do not state a single fully-reconciled
  round-by-round total. ***
- Key investors: Israel Corp (the largest single backer, an Israeli conglomerate chaired by Idan
  Ofer), HSBC, General Electric, Morgan Stanley (led the 2010 round), Lend Lease Ventures (led the
  2007 Series A).
- Peak valuation: $2.25 billion, reached with the November 2011 Series C round (nearly double the
  valuation from the prior Jan 2010 financing).
Source: GlobalVenturing; WardsAuto (Nov 2011 Series C coverage); Greentech Media; The World/PRX
("Electric car company Better Place, once valued at $2.25B, to close"); Quartz.

THE RENAULT PARTNERSHIP — and the single-car-model dependency
- Better Place's swap-station network depended entirely on one compatible vehicle: the Renault
  Fluence Z.E., an all-electric sedan Renault built specifically with a battery pack designed to be
  removed and replaced by Better Place's automated swap-station machinery.
- *** [VERIFY, single/secondary-sourced]: reporting (Quartz's retrospective) states Agassi was
  unable to secure additional automaker partners beyond Renault, and characterizes this as Agassi
  having "alienated" potential partners like BMW and GM — this is a secondary retrospective
  characterization, not a direct on-record automaker statement found in this research pass. Present
  as "Better Place could not secure a second automaker partner beyond Renault" rather than adopting
  the sharper "alienated" framing as this channel's own conclusion. ***
Source: Quartz ("Why Better Place failed with swappable batteries"); Wikipedia "Renault Z.E."

MARKETS AND DEPLOYMENT NUMBERS
- Primary launch market: Israel — chosen for its small geographic size, which made a nationwide
  swap-station network logistically feasible. Second market: Denmark (EV tax incentives helped).
- First customer deliveries of the Fluence Z.E. began in Israel in early 2012 (roughly 100 units
  handed over in January 2012 as part of subscription packages).
- *** [VERIFY]: total vehicles deployed across both markets is reported inconsistently — one source
  states "fewer than 1,500" total (roughly 1,000 in Israel + ~400 in Denmark); another states the
  company had sold only "950" electric vehicles by the time of its collapse. Use "fewer than 1,500
  vehicles across both markets, some sources cite as few as roughly 950" — do not state one single
  precise figure. ***
Source: GreenCarReports; Quartz; TechCrunch (2013 bankruptcy coverage).

INFRASTRUCTURE COST OVERRUN
- Each robotic battery-swap station was initially budgeted at approximately $500,000 to build, but
  actual stations ended up costing approximately $2 million each — a roughly 4x cost overrun on the
  company's core physical infrastructure.
Source: Fast Company ("Better Place, by the Numbers"); GreenCarReports ("Better Place Battery
Swapping: The Technology Behind The Idea").

2012 FINANCIALS — real revenue vs. capital raised
- *** [VERIFY, single-sourced to PrivCo, a private financial-data research firm reporting on

  Place lost $459 million in 2012 alone on just $6.9 million in sales that year, ending 2012 with
  only $34.4 million cash on hand (down to $9.5 million by the time of the report) against $915
  million in total liabilities. This is a different metric from the ~$850M lifetime-funding figure
  (liabilities vs. capital raised) — do not conflate the two numbers; present the $6.9M 2012 revenue
  figure clearly labeled as PrivCo's reporting, not independently re-verified against Better Place's

Source: PrivCo, republished via Yahoo Finance ("Better Place Inc. Lost $459M in 2012 on Only $6.9M
in Sales").

THE OUSTER OF SHAI AGASSI
- October 2, 2012: Agassi resigned/was replaced as worldwide CEO of Better Place, replaced by Evan
  Thornley (previously CEO of Better Place's Australia unit). Agassi remained on the company's board
  briefly, then resigned from the board about a week later as well.
- At the time of the leadership change, the company was reported to have lost approximately $490
  million cumulatively since its 2007 founding — *** this is a distinct, earlier cumulative-loss
  figure from the later ~$812-823M lost by the May 2013 bankruptcy filing; do not conflate the two,
  they describe different points in time. ***
- Within days of Thornley's appointment, Better Place asked its investors for an emergency funding
  round of approximately $100 million.
Source: TechCrunch ("Better Place Board Quietly Removes CEO Shai Agassi, Places Evan Thornley At
Helm," Oct 2012); Forbes (Oct 2012); Green Prophet.

THE BANKRUPTCY — precisely dated
- May 26, 2013: Better Place filed a motion for liquidation in an Israeli court (widely reported as
  one of the largest failures in Israeli tech/startup history).
- *** [VERIFY]: total cumulative loss at the time of filing is reported as either $812 million or
  $823 million of the roughly $850 million raised, across two different sources — use "lost more
  than $800 million of the roughly $850 million it raised" as the safe, hedged figure. ***
- Reported root cause for the final collapse: the company would have needed an estimated additional
  $500 million and roughly four more years of operation to reach breakeven — investors were not
  willing to continue funding at that scale.
Source: Global Venturing ("Better Place found in bankruptcy"); Jewish Telegraphic Agency; TechCrunch
("Electric Car Tech Company Better Place Hits The Deadpool," May 2013); MIT Technology Review ("How
Better Place Came to a Bitter End").

THE ASSET SALES — a fire sale within a fire sale
- July 2013: an Israeli consortium referred to in reporting as the "Sunrise Group" (led by a
  businessman surnamed Abramowitz per CleanTechnica's headline) initially agreed to buy Better
  Place's remaining assets for roughly $12 million total — reported as at least NIS 18 million
  (~$4.9 million) for Better Place Israel's operating assets and NIS 25 million (~$6.9 million) for
  Better Place Switzerland's intellectual property.
- *** This deal was subsequently CANCELED by the court after the Sunrise Group failed to make its
  first agreed payment. *** State this explicitly — the $12 million deal never actually closed.
- November 2013: the bankruptcy receivers instead sold the remaining assets — including
  approximately 1,800 charging stations across Israel and the company's patents — to a different
  Israeli energy startup, Gnrgy Ltd. (led by Ran Eloya), for just $450,000.
- Framing note: less than two years before this final $450,000 sale, the company had carried a
  $2.25 billion valuation. This is the single sharpest, best-corroborated contrast in the whole
  package — use it as the headline arc.
Source: Autoblog ("Bankrupt Better Place sold to Sunrise group for about $12 million," Jul 2013);
CleanTechnica; Times of Israel ("Local energy startup buys remaining Better Place shares"); Gas 2
("Billion-Dollar Project Better Place Bought For $450,000," Nov 2013); Green Prophet ("Gnrgy Buys
Better Place for the Price of an Apartment in Tel Aviv").

NO FRAUD / NO CRIMINAL ALLEGATIONS
- No evidence of any fraud allegation, criminal charge, or regulatory action against Shai Agassi,
  Better Place, or any named executive was found in this research pass. This is a pure
  capital-intensity / single-automaker-dependency / market-penetration-shortfall failure story.
  Retrospectives (MIT Technology Review, ScienceDirect academic paper "Why Did Better Place Fail?")
  attribute the collapse to a combination of factors: high infrastructure capital intensity, spreading
  resources thin by pursuing pilots/expansion in multiple countries simultaneously, dependency on a
  single automaker's single car model, and real-world adoption far below the company's own
  projections. Do not use "fraud/scam/lied" language anywhere in this package.
