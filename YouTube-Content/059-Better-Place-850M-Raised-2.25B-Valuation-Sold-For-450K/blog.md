# Blog Article: This Company Raised $850 Million. It Sold For $450,000.

*All figures sourced to public funding coverage, retrospective reporting, and Israeli bankruptcy/
asset-sale press coverage — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Better Place's total
lifetime funding has no single reconciled figure across sources (individually disclosed rounds sum to
roughly $686M against a commonly cited $850M headline), so this article uses "approximately $850
million" throughout, never one all-inclusive precise total. The 2012 revenue/loss figures are
single-sourced to PrivCo, a private financial-research firm reporting on obtained documents, not an
audited public filing — attributed explicitly throughout. No fraud or criminal allegation exists
anywhere in this story.*

## Introduction
Most startup-failure stories on this channel involve a product that never found real demand, or
numbers that turned out not to be real. Better Place is different: it built genuinely working
technology to solve a genuinely real problem, and still failed. Founded in 2007 by Shai Agassi, a
former SAP executive widely reported to have been in line for a future SAP CEO role, Better Place set
out to fix one of the real structural barriers to electric-car adoption — the battery — with a
network of robotic stations that could swap a depleted battery for a charged one in minutes. The
company raised approximately $850 million and reached a peak valuation of $2.25 billion. By November
2013, it sold for $450,000. This is a story about capital intensity, single-partner dependency, and
what happens when working technology still isn't enough.

## The Founder and the Idea
Shai Agassi founded Better Place in 2007. The core idea: instead of plugging in an EV to charge — a
slow process that also required a large, expensive battery in every car — a driver would pull into a
robotic swap station and have a depleted battery automatically exchanged for a fully charged one in a
few minutes, faster than filling a gas tank. Better Place itself would own the batteries, the single
most expensive component of any EV, and sell "miles" to drivers on a subscription basis, similar to a
mobile-phone plan. This removed both the upfront battery cost and range anxiety as adoption barriers
— a genuinely clever structural solution to a real problem.

## The Raise and the Backers
Better Place's funding figures are reported inconsistently across sources. Individually disclosed
rounds included a $111 million Series A in 2007 (led by Lend Lease Ventures) plus a $25 million
second tranche, a $350 million round in January 2010 (led by Morgan Stanley), and a $200 million
Series C in November 2011. These individually disclosed rounds sum to roughly $686 million — short of
the approximately $850 million lifetime total most commonly cited across sources, a gap this article
does not attempt to fully reconcile. Key backers included Israel Corp (the largest single investor),
HSBC, and General Electric. The November 2011 Series C round pushed the company's valuation to a peak
of $2.25 billion.

## The Single-Partner Dependency
Better Place's entire swap-station network depended on exactly one compatible vehicle: the Renault
Fluence Z.E., a car Renault built specifically with a battery pack designed for Better Place's
automated swap machinery. Retrospective reporting (Quartz) states the company was unable to secure a
second automaker partner beyond Renault. This article presents that as a fact about the outcome —
Better Place never secured a second manufacturer — without adopting a sharper secondhand
characterization of why that happened, since no direct on-record automaker statement was found in
this research pass. Structurally, though, the effect is clear: an entire national infrastructure
network's viability rested on one manufacturer continuing to build one specific car.

## Building the Network
Better Place launched in Israel — chosen for its small geographic size, which made a nationwide
swap-station network logistically feasible — and Denmark, helped by EV tax incentives. First customer
deliveries began in Israel in early 2012, with roughly 100 units handed over that January. Total
vehicles deployed across both markets is reported inconsistently: some sources cite fewer than 1,500
combined, others cite as few as roughly 950. This article uses the hedged range rather than a single
precise figure. Separately, each robotic swap station was originally budgeted at approximately
$500,000 to build; actual stations ended up costing approximately $2 million each — a roughly 4x cost
overrun on the company's core physical infrastructure.

## The Real Numbers
Financial-research firm PrivCo later reported that Better Place lost $459 million in 2012 alone on
just $6.9 million in sales that year, ending 2012 with only $34.4 million cash on hand against $915
million in total liabilities. This needs to be stated precisely: this figure is reported by PrivCo, a
private research firm working from obtained documents, not independently verified against Better
Place's own audited public filings in this article's research pass. It is presented here attributed
specifically to PrivCo, not as independently confirmed.

## The Ouster
On October 2, 2012, Shai Agassi resigned as (or was replaced as) worldwide CEO, replaced by Evan
Thornley, previously CEO of Better Place's Australia unit. Agassi resigned from the company's board
about a week later. At the time, the company was reported to have lost approximately $490 million
cumulatively since its 2007 founding — a distinct, earlier figure from the much larger cumulative
loss reported at the company's bankruptcy filing seven months later; this article does not conflate
the two. Within days of Thornley's appointment, Better Place asked its investors for an emergency
funding round of approximately $100 million.

## The Liquidation
On May 26, 2013, Better Place filed a motion for liquidation in an Israeli court — widely reported as
one of the largest failures in Israeli tech history. Total cumulative loss at the time of filing is
reported as either $812 million or $823 million of the roughly $850 million raised, across two
different sources; this article uses "lost more than $800 million of the roughly $850 million it
raised." The reported root cause: the company would have needed an estimated additional $500 million
and roughly four more years of operation to reach breakeven, and investors were not willing to
continue funding at that scale.

## Two Sales, Only One Real
In July 2013, an Israeli consortium referred to in reporting as the "Sunrise Group" initially agreed
to buy Better Place's remaining assets for roughly $12 million total. This needs to be stated
clearly: this deal was subsequently canceled by the court after the Sunrise Group failed to make its
first agreed payment — it never actually closed. In November 2013, the bankruptcy receivers instead
sold the remaining assets — including approximately 1,800 charging stations across Israel and the
company's patents — to a different Israeli energy startup, Gnrgy Ltd., led by Ran Eloya, for just
$450,000. That is the real, final sale price — less than two years after the company's $2.25 billion
valuation.

## The Actual Lesson
Better Place solved the harder engineering problem: the robotic battery-swap technology genuinely
worked. It still failed on two more structural risks — a single-automaker dependency that never
widened to a second manufacturer, and infrastructure capital intensity (swap stations costing roughly
four times their original budget) that outpaced real-world adoption. Approximately $850 million
raised, a $2.25 billion peak valuation, and a $450,000 final sale price is one of the sharpest
raise-to-sale-price collapse ratios of any company this channel has covered — and it happened without
any fraud allegation anywhere in the story.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **Working technology is not the same thing as a de-risked business** — Better Place's core
  engineering worked; its business model still depended entirely on factors outside its own control
- **A single exclusive partner is a single point of failure** — an entire infrastructure network built
  around one manufacturer's one car model carries structural risk that no amount of capital fully
  offsets
- **Infrastructure cost overruns compound quietly** — a roughly 4x per-unit cost overrun on core
  physical infrastructure, multiplied across an entire national network, is a very different problem
  than a single missed software-development estimate
- **Watch the sequence of sale attempts, not just the first headline** — the widely-reportable $12
  million deal never actually closed; the real ending was a much smaller, later, less-covered sale
- **"Ahead of its time" and "well-executed" are different claims** — a real problem and real
  technology don't guarantee a viable path to profitability at the capital intensity this business
  required

## The Lesson
Better Place raised approximately $850 million, reached a $2.25 billion valuation, and built
genuinely working technology to solve a genuinely real problem — and still ended up selling for
$450,000, undone by a single-partner dependency and an infrastructure cost structure that outpaced
its own adoption curve.
