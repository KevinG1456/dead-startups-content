# 060 — Boo.com: $135 Million Burned in 18 Months, the Original Dot-Com-Bust Case Study

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built with extra care given how many inconsistent
retellings circulate for this story (funding totals, the fire-sale buyer, the exact founding year all
have real discrepancies across sources). Full citations and every flagged discrepancy in
`sources-log.txt`.

**The real case:** Boo.com was conceived in 1998 and formally founded in March 1999 by three Swedish
entrepreneurs — Ernst Malmsten (CEO), Kajsa Leander (former model, marketing), and Patrik Hedelin
(former investment banker, finance) — with a genuinely audacious pitch: a single global online
retailer for branded sportswear and designer fashion, built from day one for multiple languages and
currencies at once, targeting fashion-conscious 18-24 year olds. It raised roughly $135 million from
investors including Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, LVMH's internet-investment arm Europ@web (backed by
Bernard Arnault), and the Benetton family's investment arm. The site was supposed to launch May 1999.
It actually launched November 3, 1999 — six months late, burning cash the entire time — built on
Flash and JavaScript-heavy 3D "spin and try on" technology that took minutes to load on a dial-up
modem, didn't work on Macintosh computers, and let only about 25% of attempted purchases actually go
through. The company spent $25 million on advertising before launch alone — and still, by one
retrospective account, reached only about 13.2% brand awareness among internet users, a sharp contrast
to this channel's other 1999-2000 marketing-heavy collapse, 054/Pets.com, whose sock puppet became
near-universally recognized on a smaller budget. Boo.com was placed into receivership May 18, 2000,
having burned through its funding in roughly 18 months. Its remaining assets were split between two
separate buyers for a combined total reportedly under $2 million: Bright Station bought the
e-commerce technology for £250,000 (~$375,000, and mainly to recruit 50-70 of Boo's engineers);
Fashionmall.com separately bought the brand and domain for an undisclosed sum. Malmsten later
co-wrote "Boo Hoo: A Dot.Com Story from Concept to Catastrophe" (2001), which became one of the
earliest and most detailed insider accounts of a dot-com collapse — still used as a business-school
case study today.

**Framing requirement:** No fraud, no misconduct, no named individual behaving badly — this is a pure
overhype/execution/timing/technical-misstep story, the original template for nearly every dot-com-bust
narrative that followed it. Never state the commonly-repeated ~$390M peak-valuation figure on screen
(unconfirmed, dropped per `sources-log.txt`). Never conflate the two separate fire-sale buyers or
state a dollar figure for the Fashionmall.com brand purchase (undisclosed). Never commit to a single
founding year — Wikipedia's own article is internally inconsistent between 1998 and 1999.

- **Curiosity triggers:** how do you burn $135M in 18 months before most people even know your name;

  years too early" irony, schadenfreude at the fire-sale price

## 5. Improvement Suggestions
- Lead with the literal "this site is designed for 56K modems and above" detail — concrete, funny,
  and instantly explains the whole failure mode without narration
- Make the $25M-ad-spend-vs-13.2%-awareness contrast fully visual (a simple bar comparison against
  Pets.com's smaller spend and much higher recognition)
- Don't blur the two fire-sale buyers into one deal — the precision itself is a differentiator most
  existing coverage lacks
- Close on the "Boo Hoo" book as a genuine, lasting artifact — this company's failure directly shaped
  how a generation of founders and investors thought about premature scaling
