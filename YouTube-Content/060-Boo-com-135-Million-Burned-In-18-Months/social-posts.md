## X / Twitter Thread

1/ This company's own homepage warned you that you needed a faster modem just to load it.

It had raised $135 million. Here's what actually happened. 🧵

2/ This is Boo.com. Conceived 1998, formally founded March 1999, by three Swedish entrepreneurs:
Ernst Malmsten (CEO), Kajsa Leander (marketing), Patrik Hedelin (finance).

The pitch: one global online fashion retailer, built for every language and currency at once.

3/ ~$135M raised from Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, LVMH's investment arm Europ@web, and the Benetton
family's investment arm.

Planned launch: May 1999. Actual launch: November 3, 1999. Six months late, zero revenue the whole
time.

4/ The site ran on Flash-heavy 3D "spin and try on" tech that cost $6M+ to build. Homepage took
minutes to load on dial-up. Didn't work on Mac at all. The site literally said "designed for 56K
modems and above."

5/ Result: only about 25% of attempted purchases actually completed.

They'd already spent $25M on ads before launch. Reported brand awareness afterward: only ~13.2% of
internet users. (Compare that to Pets.com's sock puppet — smaller budget, way more recognition.)

6/ May 18, 2000: placed into receivership, having burned through roughly $135M in about 18 months.

Two SEPARATE companies split the wreckage: Bright Station bought the tech (~$375K, mainly for the
engineers). Fashionmall.com separately bought the brand (undisclosed price).

7/ Combined: reportedly under $2 million recovered. From $135 million raised.

Founder Ernst Malmsten later co-wrote "Boo Hoo" (2001) — one of the earliest, most detailed insider
dot-com-collapse memoirs ever published. Still taught in business schools.

8/ The lesson isn't "bad idea." Most of what Boo.com tried to build is completely normal e-commerce
today. The lesson: being right about the future doesn't save you if your actual customers are still
stuck in the present.

## LinkedIn Post

Everyone knows "the dot-com bubble popped." Almost nobody knows the specific mechanism behind its
most famous early casualty.

I just published a full breakdown of Boo.com: ~$135M raised (Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, LVMH's
Europ@web, the Benetton family), a launch six months behind schedule, a 3D product-visualization
platform that cost $6M+ to build and didn't work on dial-up or Macintosh — resulting in only about
25% of attempted purchases actually completing.

They'd spent $25M on advertising before launch. Reported brand awareness afterward: only around
13.2% — a sharp contrast to this channel's other 1999-2000 marketing story, Pets.com, whose sock
puppet reached near-universal recognition on a smaller budget.

May 2000: receivership. Two separate buyers split the wreckage — reportedly under $2M recovered
combined, against $135M raised.

The part most retellings skip: founder Ernst Malmsten turned the whole collapse into "Boo Hoo" (2001),
one of the earliest and most detailed startup-failure memoirs ever published — still assigned reading
in business schools today.

The lesson isn't "the idea was bad." Most of what they tried to build is standard e-commerce
infrastructure now. The lesson is that being early and being wrong can look identical from the inside,
right up until the money runs out.
