# Blog Article

## Title: This Website Warned You It Needed a Faster Modem to Even Load: The Boo.com Story

Long before Pets.com's sock puppet became the most recognizable face of the dot-com bust, there was
Boo.com — a company that raised roughly $135 million to build the future of online shopping, and
whose own homepage warned visitors they needed a faster modem just to load it.

This is the original textbook dot-com-collapse case study, the one business schools were teaching
before almost any other company like it. Here's what actually happened, and why the sources genuinely
disagree on some of it.

**A note on sourcing, up front.** Even the company's founding year is disputed — Wikipedia's own
article states both 1998 and 1999 in different places. This article uses "conceived in 1998, formally
founded March 1999" rather than picking one and pretending the disagreement doesn't exist. A
commonly-repeated ~$390 million peak-valuation figure also couldn't be independently confirmed during
research for this piece and is deliberately left out rather than restated as fact.

### An audacious pitch

Boo.com was founded by three Swedish entrepreneurs: Ernst Malmsten (CEO), Kajsa Leander (a former
model who led marketing), and Patrik Hedelin (a former investment banker who led finance). The pitch
wasn't just "sell fashion online" — it was a single global retailer, built from day one to operate in
multiple languages and multiple currencies simultaneously, targeting fashion-conscious 18-to-24-year-
olds worldwide. It was a genuinely ambitious idea, and investors agreed: roughly $135 million came in
from Goldman Sachs, J.P. Morgan, LVMH's internet-investment arm Europ@web (backed by Bernard Arnault),
and the Benetton family's own investment arm.

### Six months late, and still not ready

The site was supposed to launch in May 1999. It actually launched November 3, 1999 — six months late,
burning cash the entire time with no revenue coming in.

When it finally went live, the centerpiece was a 3D "spin and try on" product viewer, built heavily on
JavaScript and Flash, that reportedly cost more than $6 million to develop and roughly $500,000 a
month to maintain. The problem: the homepage alone was several hundred kilobytes, taking minutes to
load over a dial-up connection — the internet access most people actually had in 1999. The site
displayed its own warning that it was "designed for 56K modems and above." It didn't work properly on
Macintosh computers at all. Customers reportedly had to answer four or five configuration questions
before discovering an item wasn't even in stock. The result: only around 25% of attempted purchases
actually completed.

### The marketing spend that almost nobody noticed

Before it even launched, Boo.com had already spent $25 million on advertising and PR. According to one
retrospective account, the actual brand awareness that bought was only around 13.2% of internet users.

That's a genuinely useful contrast to this channel's other big 1999-2000 marketing story: Pets.com.
Pets.com spent $11.8 million in 1999 and produced a mascot that became near-universally recognized —
a Super Bowl ad, a Macy's parade balloon, magazine profiles. Boo.com spent roughly twice that and, by
this account, barely registered with most of the people it was targeting. Same era, similar ambition,
almost opposite outcomes on the one thing marketing spend is actually supposed to buy: being
remembered.

### The collapse

By the end, Boo.com was reportedly burning through more than $10 million a month while sales had
slowed to around $500,000 every two weeks. On May 18, 2000, the company was placed into receivership,
having spent roughly $135 million in about 18 months. (A separate figure — £125 million spent in six
months — likely describes specifically the live-operations window rather than the company's full
lifespan; sources don't fully reconcile the two numbers, so this piece states both rather than forcing
one.)

### Two buyers, not one

Here's a detail most retellings blur together: Boo.com's wreckage didn't go to a single buyer. Bright
Station, a UK internet services company, bought the actual e-commerce technology platform for £250,000
(reported at roughly $375,000) — mainly for the chance to recruit 50 to 70 of Boo.com's own software
engineers. Separately, Fashionmall.com, a New York-based fashion portal, bought the Boo.com brand name
and domain from the liquidator, KPMG, for an undisclosed sum. Combined, the total recovered across both
deals is commonly described as under $2 million — against $135 million raised.

### What happened after

Fashionmall.com relaunched Boo.com in October 2000 as a much smaller, streamlined fashion portal for
18-to-30-year-olds. The brand reportedly changed hands and purpose several more times over the
following decade before eventually fading out entirely.

The more lasting legacy came from Malmsten himself. In 2001, he co-wrote "Boo Hoo: A Dot.Com Story
from Concept to Catastrophe" with journalists Erik Portanger and Charles Drazin — one of the earliest
and most detailed insider accounts of a dot-com collapse ever published. It's still used as a
business-school case study today.

### The actual lesson

It would be easy to file Boo.com away as "another silly dot-com bubble company." That undersells what
actually happened. Most of what Boo.com tried to build — rich, interactive product visualization,
global multi-currency e-commerce, fast international shipping — is completely ordinary online retail
infrastructure today. The idea wasn't wrong.

What killed Boo.com was the gap between the internet its founders were building for and the internet
its actual customers had in front of them. Being right about where things are eventually headed
doesn't help you if the money runs out before "eventually" arrives.
