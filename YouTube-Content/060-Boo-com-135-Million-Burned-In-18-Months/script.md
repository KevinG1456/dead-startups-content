# Script Package

Anchored on Boo.com — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Never state the founding year as a single
confident number (conceived 1998, formally founded March 1999 — Wikipedia itself is inconsistent).
Never state the ~$390M peak valuation (unconfirmed, dropped). Never conflate the two fire-sale buyers
or state a dollar figure for the Fashionmall.com brand purchase (undisclosed). Present the "$135M/18
months" and "£125M/6 months" burn figures as two separate, differently-windowed numbers, not one
reconciled total. Tone: no fraud, no misconduct — pure overhype/execution/timing story, told with
genuine respect for how ambitious and how early the underlying idea actually was.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 13–15 minutes (founding, the ambition, the funding, the delayed launch, the
technical failure, the marketing-vs-awareness gap, the collapse, the fire sale, the book).

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This website's own homepage warned you that you needed a faster modem just to load it. It had
  raised a hundred and thirty-five million dollars."
- Visual: a stylized loading spinner frozen mid-spin, dissolving into a falling stock-style line
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to black, white text: "This isn't a story about a bad idea. It's a
  story about a good idea nobody's computer could actually run yet."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Boo.com — the original dot-com disaster, the one business schools were teaching before
  almost any other company on this channel even existed."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll know exactly why only one in four customers could
  actually complete a purchase, why two totally different companies split the wreckage between them,
  and why the founders' own book about this collapse became required reading for an entire generation
  of startup founders."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, Forbes' 2000 reporting on the fire sale, The Hustle's
  retrospective, and multiple contemporaneous trade-press articles — full citations in
  `sources-log.txt`
- Honesty note, stated plainly: "One thing up front — sources genuinely disagree on some of the exact
  numbers here, including the company's own founding year. Where they disagree, I'm telling you both
  versions instead of just picking the one that sounds cleaner."

### Main Content — The Ambition (1:40–4:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:20):** conceived in 1998, formally founded March 1999, by three
  Swedish entrepreneurs — Ernst Malmsten (CEO), Kajsa Leander (former model, marketing), Patrik
  Hedelin (former investment banker, finance). Visual: simple "1998/1999" title card.
- **Segment 2 — The Pitch (2:20–3:00):** not just an online fashion store — a single global retailer,
  built from day one for multiple languages and multiple currencies simultaneously, targeting
  fashion-conscious 18-24 year olds worldwide. Visual: rotating-globe graphic with fashion icons
  orbiting.
- **Segment 3 — The Money (3:00–4:00):** roughly $135 million raised from investors including Goldman
  Sachs, J.P. Morgan, LVMH's internet-investment arm Europ@web (backed by Bernard Arnault), and the
  Benetton family's investment arm. Visual: ascending funding graphic.

### Main Content — The Launch That Broke (4:00–7:30)
- **Segment 4 — Six Months Late (4:00–4:40):** planned launch May 1999; actual launch November 3,
  1999 — burning cash with zero revenue the entire time. Visual: calendar graphic, "MAY" crossed out,
  "NOVEMBER" landing.
- **Segment 5 — The Technology That Broke Everything (4:40–6:30):** the site ran on heavy JavaScript
  and Flash for a pseudo-3D "spin and try on" product viewer that cost over $6 million to build and a
  reported $500,000 a month to maintain. The homepage alone was several hundred kilobytes — minutes to
  load on a dial-up connection. The site displayed its own warning: "designed for 56K modems and
  above." It didn't work properly on Macintosh computers at all. On-screen framing card: "They built
  the future of the internet. Most of their customers were still on dial-up." Visual: dial-up
  loading-bar graphic frozen partway.
- **Segment 6 — The Broken Checkout (6:30–7:30):** customers reportedly had to answer four or five
  configuration questions before finding out an item wasn't even in stock. Reported result: only about
  25% of attempted purchases actually completed. On-screen framing card: "3 out of every 4 people who
  tried to buy something — couldn't." Visual: mostly-transparent shopping-cart icon.

### Main Content — The Marketing Gap (7:30–9:15)
- **Segment 7 — $25 Million Before Launch (7:30–8:15):** the company spent $25 million on advertising
  and PR before it even opened for business. Visual: ascending ad-spend graphic.
- **Segment 8 — The Number That Actually Explains the Failure (8:15–9:15):** despite that spend, one
  retrospective account puts total brand awareness at only about 13.2% of internet users — a sharp
  contrast to this channel's other 1999-2000 marketing story, Pets.com, whose sock puppet became
  near-universally recognized on a smaller ad budget. On-screen framing card: "Same era. Bigger ad
  budget. A fraction of the recognition." Visual: simple two-bar comparison graphic (labeled generically
  as "Company A / Company B" if direct naming risks confusion, or narrated verbally instead).

### Main Content — The Collapse (9:15–11:00)
- **Segment 9 — Burning Faster Than It Could Sell (9:15–10:00):** by the end, the company was
  reportedly burning through more than $10 million a month, while sales had slowed to around $500,000
  every two weeks. Visual: two diverging line graphs, one falling, one flat.
- **Segment 10 — Receivership (10:00–11:00):** May 18, 2000 — Boo.com placed into receivership, having
  spent roughly $135 million in about 18 months (a separately sourced figure also cites £125 million
  spent in the final six months of live operation specifically — both numbers are real, they're just
  measuring different windows of the company's life). Visual: "MAY 18, 2000" title card.

### Main Content — The Fire Sale (11:00–12:15)
- **Segment 11 — Two Buyers, Not One (11:00–12:15):** Bright Station, a UK internet company, bought
  Boo.com's actual technology platform for £250,000 (reported at roughly $375,000) — mainly to hire 50
  to 70 of Boo's own engineers. Separately, Fashionmall.com, a New York fashion portal, bought the
  brand name and domain from the liquidator for an undisclosed sum. On-screen framing card: "One
  hundred thirty-five million dollars raised. Total remaining assets: reportedly under two million."
  Visual: two separate shopping-bag icons on either side of a faded central icon.

### The Afterlife (12:15–13:00)
- Ernst Malmsten later co-wrote "Boo Hoo: A Dot.Com Story from Concept to Catastrophe" (2001) with
  journalists Erik Portanger and Charles Drazin — one of the earliest, most detailed insider accounts
  of a dot-com collapse ever published, still used as a business-school case study today. Mid-video
  CTA (~12:30): "This channel is basically a library of stories exactly like this one. Subscribe if
  you want the next one." Visual: generic open-book graphic.

### The Actual Lesson (13:00–14:00)
- Plain-language framing: Boo.com wasn't wrong that people would eventually shop globally online with
  rich, interactive product visualization — most of what it tried to build in 1999 is completely
  normal on any e-commerce site today. It was catastrophically wrong about the timeline: it built for
  the internet connections, computers, and browsers people would have a decade later, using the money
  and patience it had right now.
- The core lesson, stated directly: being right about the future doesn't help you if your actual
  customers are still stuck in the present — and the gap between "correct, eventually" and "solvent,
  right now" is where a lot of ambitious companies actually die.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding → funding → delayed launch → technical failure →
  marketing spend → awareness gap → burn rate → receivership → fire sale → the book.

### Final CTA + Outro (14:00–14:45)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — they weren't wrong, they were just early, and early is its own
  kind of wrong when the money runs out first
- "Subscribe — this channel is a full library of exactly these autopsies, and this one is basically
  the original template"
- Engagement prompt: "Had you heard of Boo.com before this? Which detail surprised you more — the
  modem warning or the two separate buyers? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Tech-History-Forward Style
Opens directly on the 3D technology and the "56K modems" warning as the narrative spine, treating the
funding and collapse as consequences of the technical overreach rather than a separate business story.
Strongest fit for a tech-history/developer-adjacent audience segment.

### Script Version B — Business-Case-Study Style
Opens on the funding rounds and the ad-spend-vs-awareness gap, treating the technical failure as
supporting evidence rather than the narrative spine. Denser on the specific dollar figures and directly
invites the Pets.com comparison early. Strongest fit for a founder/VC-audience-specific cut.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one company, six numbers": $135M raised, $25M spent on ads before launch, 6 months late,
25% checkout completion rate, 13.2% brand awareness, under $2M in total fire-sale value. Strong Shorts-
extraction potential; ends on the "Boo Hoo" book as the closing twist.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the funding/investor-names segment (Segment 3) if presented as a dry list of
  firm names without visual pacing
- **Fix:** keep the funding graphic simple and quick, spend the saved time on the "56K modem" detail
  instead — it's the single strongest retention hook in the script
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim the investor-name recitation if early-retention data shows drop-
  off before Segment 4 — most viewers care more about "what went wrong" than "who funded it"
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the two-separate-buyers fire-sale detail explicitly in the
  cold open if drop-off appears before Segment 11 — it's the freshest, least-covered beat in the story
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running dollar counter visible from Segment 3 onward
  ("$135M raised" → "$25M on ads" → "under $2M recovered") so the scale of the collapse stays visible
  throughout
