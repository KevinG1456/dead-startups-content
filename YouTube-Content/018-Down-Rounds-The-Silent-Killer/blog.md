# Blog Article: Down Rounds — The Silent Killer — 3 Cap Tables, Annotated

*The three cap tables below are illustrative teaching examples, not real leaked company data — labeled clearly throughout. The Klarna and Instacart figures are real, reported examples used for context; confirm current figures against primary sources before publishing.*

## Introduction
When a startup "raises a down round," headlines typically report only the new, lower valuation. That number badly understates the real impact. Through the mechanics of dilution, liquidation preference stacking, and provisions like pay-to-play, a down round can affect founders' and employees' actual equity value far more severely — and far more unevenly — than the headline valuation drop suggests. This article walks through three annotated, illustrative cap table examples to show exactly how.

## What Is a Down Round?
A down round is a funding round priced at a lower valuation than the company's previous round. This typically happens when a company's growth has slowed, market conditions have shifted, or investor sentiment toward the company or its sector has cooled.

## Cap Table 1: Dilution
`[Illustrative example]` — when a company raises a new round, it issues new shares to the incoming investors. In an up round, the increased valuation typically offsets much of the dilutive effect for existing shareholders. In a down round, existing shareholders are diluted by the new issuance without that valuation-increase offset — meaning the percentage ownership of founders and employees shrinks, while the per-share price they're diluted at is lower than what they might have expected based on the company's previous valuation trajectory.

## Cap Table 2: Liquidation Preference Stacking
`[Illustrative example]` — most venture financing rounds include a liquidation preference: in the event of an exit (acquisition or IPO), preferred stockholders (investors) are typically paid out before common stockholders (founders and employees) receive anything, often up to at least the amount they originally invested. When a company raises multiple rounds — especially when a down round is added on top of prior rounds — these preferences can stack. In an exit that doesn't generate enough proceeds to satisfy the full stack of preferences, common stockholders can receive little or nothing, even if the company's ownership table shows them holding a meaningful percentage.

## Cap Table 3: Pay-to-Play
`[Illustrative example]` — some down rounds include a pay-to-play provision, which requires existing investors to participate (invest additional capital) in the new round or face a penalty, commonly the conversion of their preferred shares into common stock (losing preferred rights like liquidation preference) or significant additional dilution. This mechanic is generally aimed at existing investors rather than employees, but it illustrates how down rounds often restructure the entire cap table's risk and reward distribution, not just the headline price per share.

## Real-World Scale: Klarna and Instacart
`[VERIFY all figures before publishing]` — Klarna, the fintech company, reportedly saw its valuation fall from around $45.6 billion in 2021 to roughly $6.7 billion in a 2022 funding round — an approximately 85% reduction, one of the most widely reported down-round examples in recent startup history. Instacart, meanwhile, saw several mutual funds that held its shares mark down their valuations of the company significantly during 2022 and 2023, ahead of its eventual 2023 IPO at a valuation well below its 2021 peak. These real examples illustrate the scale at which down rounds can occur, even at well-known, well-funded companies.

## What to Ask If Your Company Takes a Down Round
**If you're an employee:** ask how the round affects your option strike price and current 409A valuation, whether there's a plan for an option re-pricing or refresh, and what the updated liquidation preference stack looks like relative to your equity's position.

**If you're a founder:** communicate proactively and honestly with your team about what the round actually means for their equity — not just the new valuation — and work with your legal and finance advisors to understand the full implications of any preference stacking or pay-to-play terms before agreeing to them.

## The Lesson
A down round's headline valuation number is the least important part of the story for most people holding equity in the company. The real impact lives in the specific cap table mechanics — dilution, liquidation preference stacking, and provisions like pay-to-play — which determine who actually absorbs the loss and who remains protected. Understanding these mechanics, rather than just the reported valuation, is essential for founders, employees, and anyone evaluating equity compensation at a company that has gone through — or might go through — this process.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What is a down round?**
A funding round priced at a lower valuation than a company's previous round.

**2. Does a down round just mean the company is worth less now?**
That's part of it, but the real impact is more specific: dilution, liquidation preference stacking, and provisions like pay-to-play typically affect different shareholders very differently.

**3. Who is hurt most by a down round?**
Generally, common stockholders (founders and employees) tend to bear a disproportionate share of the impact compared to preferred stockholders (investors), due to liquidation preference protections investors typically hold.

**4. What is dilution?**
The reduction in existing shareholders' percentage ownership that occurs when a company issues new shares, such as in a new funding round.

**5. What is a liquidation preference?**
A term giving preferred stockholders (investors) the right to be paid out before common stockholders in an exit, often up to at least the amount they originally invested.

**6. What does "liquidation preference stacking" mean?**
When a company has raised multiple rounds, each with its own liquidation preference, these preferences can accumulate ("stack"), consuming more of an exit's proceeds before common stockholders receive anything.

**7. What is a pay-to-play provision?**
A term requiring existing investors to participate in a new financing round or face a penalty, commonly conversion of their preferred shares to common stock.

**13. What should founders do when negotiating a down round?**
Work closely with legal and finance advisors to fully understand preference stacking and pay-to-play implications, and communicate honestly with the team about the real impact on their equity.

**14. Can a down round trigger an option re-pricing?**
Sometimes — some companies choose to re-price or refresh employee options after a significant valuation drop, though this isn't automatic or guaranteed.

**15. What is a 409A valuation?**
An independent appraisal of a private company's common stock value, used to set the strike price for employee stock options in compliance with U.S. tax law.

**16. Does a down round always mean the company is failing?**
Not necessarily — it can reflect broader market conditions, sector-wide valuation resets, or a company resetting expectations, though it can also signal genuine business challenges.

**17. Can employees lose their options entirely in a down round?**
Not typically through the down round mechanics alone, though a severely stacked preference structure can reduce the practical value of those options significantly in a future exit.

**18. Is a down round the same as a company shutting down?**
No — a down round means raising additional capital, just at a lower valuation; it's a financing event, not a closure, though it can be a signal of broader difficulty.

**19. How common are down rounds?**
Their frequency varies significantly with the broader venture funding environment, becoming notably more common during funding downturns.

**20. Can a down round be avoided?**
Sometimes, through alternative financing structures (bridge rounds, venture debt, or delaying a raise), though these carry their own tradeoffs and risks.
