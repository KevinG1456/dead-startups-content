Sources Log — Package 040: Friendster
Filled in: 2026-08-16

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Friendster is the anchor for this channel's "Money Mechanics" Batch 2 title #20: "The founder who
wouldn't sell: the $200M offer, declined, and the $0 outcome." Pattern: valuation anchoring. This is
one of Silicon Valley's most famous real examples of this exact pattern — but the specific numbers in
the batch title don't match the real, sourced facts, and this package corrects them rather than forcing
a false "$200M" or literal "$0."

CRITICAL FRAMING CORRECTION — READ BEFORE WRITING ANY FILE IN THIS PACKAGE
The real offer was $30 million in pre-IPO Google stock (not $200M), made in 2003. The eventual outcome
was NOT literally $0: Friendster sold to MOL Global for $39 million in December 2009, and MOL separately
sold Friendster's patent portfolio to Facebook for $40 million in June 2010 — combined, more nominal
dollars than the original Google offer. The real, more dramatic and more accurate story is about
COUNTERFACTUAL value lost: the declined Google stock would have been worth approximately $30 billion
by March 2021, given Google's post-2004-IPO appreciation. This package's title and framing use that
real, sourced $30 billion figure rather than a fabricated "$200M offer, $0 outcome" that doesn't match
the facts.

FOUNDING — precisely sourced
- Friendster was founded in 2002 by Jonathan Abrams, with Kent Lindstrom as an early collaborator (the
  two later became partners together at a venture firm, 8-Bit Capital, decades later). Abrams worked at
  Friendster from 2002 to 2005.
- Business: an early social-networking site allowing users to connect with friends and friends-of-
  friends — widely regarded as one of the first mainstream social networks, predating both MySpace and
  Facebook.
Source: Wikipedia ("Jonathan Abrams (businessman)"); TechCrunch (2006, "The Friendster Tell-All
Story").

THE GOOGLE OFFER — 2003, PRECISELY SOURCED
- In 2003, after Friendster had been operating for about a year, Google made an acquisition offer of
  approximately $30 million, structured as roughly 200 million shares of pre-IPO Google stock (Google
  did not go public until August 2004).
- Jonathan Abrams and Friendster's board declined the offer. Several "high-powered and successful"
  early investors reportedly advised Abrams that they could help build Friendster into a multi-billion-
  dollar company independently, rather than selling early.
- *** [VERIFY]: no direct, on-record Abrams quote explaining his specific reasoning for declining was
  found in this research pass — present the investor-advice framing as the best-sourced available
  explanation, not as Abrams' own stated words, unless a firmer primary quote is found before
  recording. ***
- The declined Google stock, had it been retained, would have been worth approximately $30 billion as
  of March 2021, given Google's appreciation since its 2004 IPO — a real, calculable, frequently cited
  counterfactual figure.
Source: Wikipedia ("Jonathan Abrams (businessman)"); Netimperative (Jan 25, 2021, "Interview with
Jonathan Abrams: The man who rejected a $30m sale of Friendster to Google"); Inc. Magazine (Jun 2007,
"How to Kill a Great Idea!").

THE ALTERNATIVE PATH — PRECISELY SOURCED
- Instead of selling, Friendster raised a $13 million Series A round in October 2003, led by Kleiner
  Perkins Caufield & Byers and Benchmark Capital, at a $53 million post-money valuation. The company
  later also raised from Battery Ventures.
- At its peak, Friendster had more than 111 million registered users worldwide.
Source: Wikipedia; TechCrunch retrospective coverage.

THE FOUNDER'S OUSTER AND THE DECLINE — precisely sourced
- In 2004, Friendster's board of directors removed Jonathan Abrams as CEO. Tim Koogle — former CEO of
  Yahoo — was installed as interim CEO.
- Also in 2004, MySpace overtook Friendster in total user count, marking the beginning of Friendster's
  decline in the U.S. and broader Western social-networking market. Widely-cited contributing factors
  (beyond the declined Google offer) include persistent technical scaling and site-performance
  problems that frustrated users during Friendster's period of fastest growth.
- Friendster remained a dominant social network in parts of Southeast Asia (including the Philippines,
  Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia) for several more years even as it faded in Western markets.
Source: Wikipedia; Inc. Magazine (2007); FourWeekMBA retrospective.

THE SALE AND FULL SHUTDOWN — precisely dated
- December 2009: MOL Global, a Malaysian payments company, acquired 100% of Friendster for
  approximately $39 million.
- June 2010: MOL Global separately sold Friendster's patent portfolio — seven finalized patents and
  eleven pending patent applications — to Facebook for approximately $40 million.
- Following the acquisition, MOL Global pivoted Friendster's focus almost entirely toward in-browser
  social gaming, moving away from its original social-networking identity.
- In 2015, Friendster announced it was "taking a break"; the social-gaming pivot did not succeed, and
  in 2018, Friendster officially and fully shut down.
Source: Wikipedia; FourWeekMBA ("What happened to Friendster?"); multiple retrospective coverage
(TheRunway Ventures, Startupik, ProductMint).
