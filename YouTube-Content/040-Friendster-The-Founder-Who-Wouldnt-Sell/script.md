# Script Package

Anchored on Friendster — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS
SCRIPT: never state the offer was "$200 million" or the outcome was literally "$0" — the real, sourced
figures are a ~$30 million offer (2003) and a ~$30 billion counterfactual value by 2021. Present the
"investors advised independence" detail as the best-sourced available explanation, not a direct
Abrams quote — none was found. Present Abrams' 2004 removal as CEO as a neutral board decision, not
scandal.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 12–14 minutes (the founding, the Google offer, the decision's aftermath, the
decline, and the final outcome all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Google offered this founder $30 million in stock. He said no. That stock would be worth $30
  billion today."
- Visual: small glowing "$30M" numeral exploding into a massive "$30B" numeral
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a story about running out
  of money. It's a story about the money he turned down."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Friendster — founded in 2002 by Jonathan Abrams, one of the very first mainstream
  social networks, before MySpace and years before Facebook. In 2003, Google wanted to buy it."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly what was on the table in 2003, why
  it was declined, and exactly how far the real outcome — fifteen years later — fell short of what was
  turned down."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, TechCrunch, Inc. Magazine, and Netimperative —
  sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "One honesty note up front: this video uses the real,
  sourced numbers — a $30 million offer, not $200 million, and a real, dated sale years later, not a
  literal zero. The real numbers are more interesting anyway."

### Main Content — The Offer (1:40–5:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** Friendster founded 2002 by Jonathan Abrams, one of the
  first mainstream social networks — connect with friends and friends-of-friends online. Visual:
  generic early-2000s social-network node graphic.
- **Segment 2 — Google Comes Calling (2:15–3:15):** in 2003, after about a year of operation, Google
  offers to acquire Friendster for approximately $30 million in pre-IPO stock — roughly 200 million
  shares, more than a year before Google's own 2004 IPO. Visual: "$30 MILLION — GOOGLE'S OFFER, 2003"
  title card.
- **Segment 3 — The Decision (3:15–4:15):** Abrams and Friendster's board decline the offer. Reportedly,
  several high-powered early investors advised Abrams that they could help build Friendster into a
  multi-billion-dollar company independently. On-screen framing card: "No direct quote from Abrams
  explaining his own reasoning has been found — this is the best-sourced explanation available."
  Visual: neutral "DECLINED" stamp over the $30M card.
- **Segment 4 — Betting on Independence (4:15–5:00):** October 2003, instead of selling, Friendster
  raises a $13 million Series A from Kleiner Perkins Caufield & Byers and Benchmark Capital, at a $53
  million valuation. Visual: "$13M SERIES A — $53M VALUATION" title card.

### Main Content — The Cost (5:00–8:30)
- **Segment 5 — What the Stock Would Be Worth (5:00–6:00):** Google went public in August 2004. The
  declined ~200 million pre-IPO shares, held through Google's subsequent appreciation, would have been
  worth approximately $30 billion by March 2021. On-screen framing card: "This is a real, calculable
  figure, not hyperbole." Visual: "$30 BILLION — WHAT THE DECLINED STOCK WOULD BE WORTH TODAY" title
  card.
- **Segment 6 — The Board Turns on the Founder (6:00–7:00):** in 2004 — about a year after the Google
  decision — Friendster's board removed Jonathan Abrams as CEO, installing former Yahoo CEO Tim Koogle
  as interim CEO. Visual: "FOUNDER REMOVED — 2004" title card.
- **Segment 7 — Overtaken (7:00–8:00):** also in 2004, MySpace overtakes Friendster in total users,
  beginning Friendster's decline in Western markets — compounded by persistent technical scaling
  problems that frustrated users during its fastest-growth period. Friendster remains dominant in parts
  of Southeast Asia for several more years even as it fades elsewhere. Visual: user-count crossover
  graph, MySpace overtaking Friendster.
- **Segment 8 — Peak Users, Declining Value (8:00–8:30):** at its height, Friendster had more than 111
  million registered users worldwide — a real, massive audience that still wasn't enough to translate
  into lasting value. Visual: "111 MILLION USERS AT PEAK" title card.

### Main Content — The Ending (8:30–10:30)
- **Segment 9 — Sold (8:30–9:15):** December 2009, Malaysian payments company MOL Global acquires 100%
  of Friendster for approximately $39 million. Visual: "SOLD FOR $39 MILLION — DECEMBER 2009" title
  card.
- **Segment 10 — The Patents Alone (9:15–10:00):** June 2010, MOL separately sells Friendster's
  patent portfolio — seven finalized patents, eleven pending — to Facebook for approximately $40
  million, nearly recouping MOL's entire purchase price from the patents alone. On-screen framing card:
  "The patents sold for more than the whole company did, less than a year earlier." Visual: "$40
  MILLION — PATENTS ALONE, JUNE 2010" title card.
- **Segment 11 — The End (10:00–10:30):** MOL pivots Friendster to social gaming; the pivot doesn't
  work. In 2015, Friendster "takes a break." In 2018, it officially and fully shuts down. Visual:
  "SHUTDOWN — 2018" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:15): "This channel usually covers companies that ran out of money. This is the
  rare one that turned real money down first. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson (10:30–12:00)
- Plain-language comparison: the advice to stay independent wasn't unreasonable given what was known
  in 2003 — but the specific bet (that Friendster could out-build what Google was about to become) did
  not pay off, and the company lost both the deal and, eventually, control of its own direction anyway.
- The core lesson, stated directly: declining a real offer is a bet on your own future value — and the
  size of that bet is defined not by what you turn down, but by what the alternative actually turns out
  to be worth.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2002) → Google offer declined (2003) → Series A raised
  (Oct 2003) → Google IPO (Aug 2004) → founder removed (2004) → MySpace overtakes (2004) → peak 111M
  users → sold to MOL (Dec 2009) → patents sold to Facebook (Jun 2010) → shutdown (2018).

### Final CTA + Outro (12:00–13:15)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — turning down a real offer is a bet on your own future, and the
  cost of losing that bet isn't measured by the offer you declined, it's measured by what actually
  happens next
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this is the first one centered entirely on a
  declined offer rather than running out of money"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever use Friendster? And do you think the decision to say no to Google
  was defensible given what was known in 2003 — or was it always a mistake? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the $30M-to-$30B numeral graphic and the full-arc timeline, then works through the
founding, the offer, and the aftermath as supporting detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the
video as a genuine case study in valuation-anchoring and exit-timing risk.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the 2003 Google offer itself — the negotiation, the decision — before cutting hard to the 2018
shutdown, then rewinding to fill in everything in between. Slower pacing, holds the "$30 billion" reveal
until Segment 5, resolving with the patent sale and final shutdown as the closing beats. Strongest
tonal-irony arc of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one decision, fifteen years of consequences" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
Google offer, decline, Series A, IPO, ouster, overtaken, peak users, sale, patent sale, shutdown —
landing on the valuation-anchoring lesson as the final, clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction
potential, especially the $30M-to-$30B contrast and the "patents sold for more than the company"
detail.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the "betting on independence" segment (4:15–5:00) if the Series A detail feels
  like a dry funding-history footnote
- **Fix:** frame the Series A explicitly as "the company doubling down on the exact bet that would
  later look wrong," connecting it directly forward to the $30 billion reveal in the next segment
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general early-2000s social-networking context if audience
  familiarity with the period is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the $30 billion figure even more explicitly in the cold
  open if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 5 (5:00)
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "value of declined stock" corner counter visible
  from Segment 5 onward, ticking upward as the timeline advances toward 2021, landing on "$30 BILLION"
