# Blog Article: Google Offered $30 Million in Stock. He Said No. That Stock Would Be Worth $30 Billion Today.

*All figures sourced to Wikipedia, TechCrunch, Inc. Magazine, and Netimperative — see
`sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This article corrects the popular but inaccurate "$200
million offer, $0 outcome" version of this story to the real, sourced figures: a ~$30 million offer in
2003, and a real (not zero) eventual sale, framed around the actual, calculable counterfactual value of
what was declined.*

## Introduction
Startup lore is full of stories about founders who turned down a big offer and regretted it. Most of
those stories get retold with rounder, more dramatic numbers than the real ones. Friendster's story
doesn't need any exaggeration. The real numbers are already staggering enough.

## The Founding
Jonathan Abrams founded Friendster in 2002. It was one of the first mainstream social networks —
letting users connect with friends, and friends of friends, in a way that predated both MySpace and
Facebook by years.

## The Offer
In 2003, after Friendster had been operating for about a year, Google made an acquisition offer:
approximately $30 million, structured as roughly 200 million shares of pre-IPO Google stock. Google
itself wouldn't go public until August 2004 — meaning this offer was, in effect, a bet on Google's own
future, packaged as payment for Friendster.

Abrams and Friendster's board declined. The best-sourced explanation available: several high-powered,
successful early investors reportedly told Abrams they could help build Friendster into a multi-
billion-dollar company on its own, rather than selling this early. No direct, on-record quote from
Abrams explaining his own reasoning turned up in the research for this article — the investor-advice
framing is the most consistently cited account across multiple retrospectives.

## Betting on Independence
Instead of selling, Friendster raised a $13 million Series A round in October 2003, led by Kleiner
Perkins Caufield & Byers and Benchmark Capital, at a $53 million post-money valuation. The company was,
in effect, doubling down on the bet that independence would be worth more than Google's offer.

## What the Stock Would Be Worth
Here's the number that makes this story worth telling precisely: Google went public in August 2004.
Had Abrams and Friendster's other stakeholders kept the roughly 200 million shares Google had offered,
that stock would have been worth approximately $30 billion by March 2021, given Google's appreciation
in the years since its IPO. This is a real, calculable figure — not a rhetorical flourish.

## The Board Turns on the Founder
In 2004 — about a year after the Google decision — Friendster's own board of directors removed Jonathan
Abrams as CEO. Tim Koogle, formerly the CEO of Yahoo, was installed as interim CEO. The founder who had
turned down Google's money didn't get to keep steering the company that decision was supposed to
protect.

## Overtaken
That same year, 2004, MySpace overtook Friendster in total user count, beginning Friendster's decline
in Western markets. Persistent technical scaling and site-performance problems — widely cited across
retrospectives — compounded the competitive pressure, frustrating users during exactly the period
Friendster needed to be growing fastest. Friendster remained dominant in parts of Southeast Asia,
including the Philippines, Malaysia, Singapore, and Indonesia, for several more years even as it faded
in the U.S. At its peak, Friendster had more than 111 million registered users worldwide — a genuinely
massive audience that still wasn't enough to translate into lasting value.

## The Sale, and the Patents That Outsold It
In December 2009, Malaysian payments company MOL Global acquired 100% of Friendster for approximately
$39 million. Then, in June 2010 — less than a year later — MOL separately sold Friendster's patent
portfolio, seven finalized patents and eleven pending applications, to Facebook for approximately $40
million. The patents alone sold for more than the entire company had, less than a year earlier.

MOL pivoted Friendster's focus almost entirely to social gaming, moving away from its original identity
as a social network. The pivot didn't work. In 2015, Friendster announced it was "taking a break." In
2018, it officially and fully shut down.

## The Actual Lesson
The advice Abrams received in 2003 — that Friendster could be worth more as an independent company than
as a Google acquisition — was not obviously unreasonable at the time. Plenty of companies have declined
early offers and gone on to build something larger. The specific bet here simply didn't pay off: the
company that made the offer went on to become one of the most valuable companies in history, while the
company that declined it lost its founder within a year, lost its market position to a competitor
within that same year, and was fully gone within fifteen.

The lesson isn't that declining offers is always a mistake. It's that declining an offer is a bet on
your own future value, and the size of that bet isn't defined by what you turn down — it's defined by
what actually happens next, which nobody can fully control.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A declined offer's true cost is only knowable in hindsight** — the $30 billion figure only became
  calculable once Google's actual trajectory played out over the following two decades
- **Investor confidence isn't the same as investor certainty** — the advice Abrams received was
  sincere, but sincerity doesn't guarantee the underlying bet pays off
- **Turning down an offer doesn't guarantee you keep control of the company either** — Friendster's
  board removed its own founder about a year after the decision that was supposed to preserve his
  independence
- **A large user base is not the same as durable value** — 111 million users at peak still wasn't
  enough to prevent the company's eventual full shutdown
- **A company's assets can outlive and outvalue the company itself** — Friendster's patents sold for
  more than the whole company had, less than a year earlier

## The Lesson
Friendster's story is usually told as a cautionary tale about turning down Google. The more precise,
more useful version is about the actual mechanics of that kind of bet: what it looked like at the time,
what it cost in hindsight, and how little control the decision ultimately bought the person who made
it.
