THE COMPANY — precisely sourced, but limited
- Code Spaces (codespaces.com) was a source-code hosting and project-collaboration service —
  described as offering secure source-code hosting and project-management tools for development
  teams to collaborate, track project activity, and manage releases.
- Based in Coventry, United Kingdom (per Tracxn's company profile).
- *** [VERIFY]: no founder name, founding year, employee count, customer count, or funding history
  was found in this research pass. Multiple general web searches for these details returned no
  results specific to this company — pre-incident, Code Spaces appears to have had very little
  public profile; nearly everything published about it dates from after the June 2014 incident. ***
Source: Tracxn company profile (codespaces.com listing); general absence of pre-2014 coverage
across all searched outlets.

THE ATTACK — precisely dated and sourced
- June 17, 2014: Code Spaces was hit by a distributed denial-of-service (DDoS) attack against its
  servers.
- During the DDoS, the company discovered messages left by the attacker on its Amazon Web Services
  (AWS) EC2 control panel — meaning the attacker had gained unauthorized access to that panel, not
  just launched an external flood attack.
- The attacker left extortion demands, including a Hotmail contact address, demanding a large
  payment to stop the DDoS attack.
- Code Spaces did not pay. Instead, the company tried to regain control of its AWS account by
  changing passwords.
- The attacker had anticipated this: they had already created additional backup administrator
  logins into the AWS console. When the company's password change locked out the attacker's
  original access, the attacker used these backup logins to get back in — and began randomly
  deleting artifacts from the panel: EBS (Elastic Block Store) snapshots, S3 storage buckets, AMIs
  (Amazon Machine Images), some EBS instances, and multiple machine instances.
- *** [VERIFY]: exact chronology/duration language varies slightly by source — some describe the
  deletion as happening "randomly" as the company tried each recovery step; use "as Code Spaces
  attempted to regain control" rather than a precise minute-by-minute timeline, since none was
  independently published. ***
Source: HelpNetSecurity (Jun 19, 2014, "Code hosting Code Spaces destroyed by extortion hack
attack"); TheHackerNews (Jun 2014, "Cyber Attack On 'Code Spaces' Puts Hosting Service Out of
Business"); Threatpost ("Hacker Puts Hosting Service Code Spaces Out of Business"); Darknet.org.uk
(Jun 2014).

THE FATAL MISTAKE — precisely sourced, this is the story's central lesson
- Code Spaces kept its backups within the SAME AWS account as its production systems and data.
- Once the attacker had console-level access to that one account, they were able to delete
  production data AND backups in the same sweep — there was no separate, isolated copy to restore
  from.
- Company representatives publicly acknowledged, via the company's own Twitter account, that they
  had not implemented two-factor authentication (2FA) on the AWS account. The working theory,
  per multiple outlets, is that a compromised username/password was how the attacker initially
  gained access to the control panel — 2FA would very likely have prevented or complicated that
  initial entry.
- Later retrospective coverage (breaches.cloud, ManageEngine, CSO Online) consistently identifies
  two compounding failures, not one: (1) no MFA on the root/administrative AWS account, and (2)
  backups co-located with production in the same account rather than isolated (e.g., a separate
  account, offline, or with independently-gated access).
Source: Darknet.org.uk and HelpNetSecurity (2FA admission via Twitter); breaches.cloud ("Codespaces
(2014) - Public Cloud Security Breaches"); ManageEngine blog ("Code Spaces AWS Security Breach: A
Sad Reminder of the Importance of Cloud Environment Password Management"); CSO Online ("Code Spaces
forced to close its doors after security incident").

THE SHUTDOWN — precisely dated
- June 18, 2014 — the day after the attack began — Code Spaces posted a statement on its own website
  announcing it would cease operations. The statement read, in part: "Code Spaces will not be able
  to operate beyond this point, the cost of resolving this issue to date and the expected cost of
  refunding customers who have been left without the service they paid for will put Code Spaces in
  an irreversible position both financially and in terms of ongoing credibility."
- The company stated it would use its remaining resources to help customers export whatever data
  could still be recovered.
- *** [VERIFY]: several secondary/retrospective sources (e.g., a widely-repeated "12 hours" figure)
  compress the timeline to "hacked and out of business within about a day" — this is directionally
  accurate (attack began Jun 17, shutdown announced Jun 18) but no primary source gives a precise
  hour count. Use "within about a day" or "the next day," not a specific "12 hours" figure, unless a
  primary source confirming that exact figure is found before recording. ***
- Customer source code, project data, and configuration files were permanently lost. No successful
  data recovery was reported anywhere in this research pass.
Source: eSecurityPlanet ("Code Spaces Destroyed by Cyber Attack," quoting the company statement
directly); CSO Online; HelpNetSecurity; DataBreaches.Net (Jun 18, 2014, "Code Spaces destroyed in
wake of extortion demand").

THE ATTACKER — precisely sourced (what is and isn't known)
- No perpetrator was ever identified or charged, per every source found in this research pass.
- Code Spaces' own statement specified: "An unauthorized person who at this point is still unknown
  (all we can say is that we have no reason to think it's anyone who is or was employed with Code
  Spaces)" — i.e., the company explicitly ruled out an inside job, based on its own account.
Source: Threats.wiz.io ("Code Spaces breach"); company statement as quoted across multiple outlets.
