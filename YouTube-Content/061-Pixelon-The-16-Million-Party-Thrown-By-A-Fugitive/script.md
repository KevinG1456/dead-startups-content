# Script Package

Anchored on Pixelon and David Kim Stanley/"Michael Fenne" — full citations in `sources-log.txt`.
"Fraud" language IS appropriate throughout this script for the Pixelon-era conduct — well-sourced
across independent, largely contemporaneous outlets. Keep the 1989 conviction (Virginia/Tennessee,
~$1.25M from elderly victims, unrelated to Pixelon) and the Pixelon-era fraud (false identity, fake
technology, $30M+ raised) explicitly distinct every time either is mentioned — two frauds, same
person, a decade apart. Use $30.9M as the primary funding figure and $16M as the primary iBash '99
cost figure. Do not state or imply what happened to Stanley after his April 2000 surrender — not
confirmed in this research.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 14–17 minutes

### Cold Open (0:00–0:20)
- VO: "October 29, 1999. The MGM Grand in Las Vegas. KISS is on stage. So is Tony Bennett. So is a
  version of The Who that hadn't played together in years. It's the launch party for a tech company
  promising to change how the world watches video online. The technology didn't work. It had never
  worked."
- Visual: stage/crowd silhouette animating in, hard cut to text: "$16 MILLION. ONE NIGHT. A PRODUCT
  THAT DIDN'T EXIST."
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "The man who threw this party wasn't
  who he said he was. This is a real, sourced fraud case."

### Hook (0:20–1:00)
- VO: "This is Pixelon — a video-streaming startup that raised more than $30 million from real
  investors, built around a CEO calling himself Michael Fenne. Fenne wasn't real. His actual name was
  David Kim Stanley, and in 1996 he'd fled Virginia rather than finish paying back the victims of a
  fraud he'd already been convicted of once."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how a fugitive raised tens of millions of
  dollars under a false name, what actually happened on stage at the most expensive product demo in
  dot-com history, and how it all came apart in a matter of weeks."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from the Seattle Times' original April 2000 reporting, Pollstar
  News' contemporaneous trade coverage, The Register, Forbes, Wikipedia, and StreamingMedia.com —
  sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: "This channel is careful about the word 'fraud' — most founders we cover
  haven't been charged with anything. This one is different. Twice."

### Main Content — The Pixelon Case (1:40–12:30)

- **Segment 1 — The First Fraud, 1989 (1:40–2:50):** Before Pixelon existed, David Kim Stanley
  pleaded guilty to 55 fraud-related counts, taking roughly $1.25 million combined from elderly
  victims in Virginia and Tennessee. Sentenced to 36 years, with 28 suspended on the condition he
  repay his victims. On-screen note, held clearly: this event has nothing to do with Pixelon's
  technology or its investors — it's the reason "Michael Fenne" needed to exist at all. Visual:
  courthouse graphic, "1989" date stamp, "UNRELATED TO PIXELON" lower third held for the segment.

- **Segment 2 — The Escape and the New Name (2:50–3:40):** In 1996, Stanley fled before completing
  restitution and was placed on Virginia's "most wanted" list. He arrived in California reportedly
  living out of the back of his car before reinventing himself as "Michael Fenne." Visual: car
  silhouette, name-change graphic.

- **Segment 3 — The Pitch and the Raise (3:40–5:00):** In 1998, "Fenne" founded Pixelon in San Juan
  Capistrano, pitching a proprietary video-streaming technology that could deliver full-screen,
  TV-quality video over ordinary internet connections — a genuine breakthrough claim for 1999.
  Advanced Equities, a Chicago investment bank, raised $30.9 million from investors on that pitch.
  Visual: funding figure building toward "$30.9M," investor-pitch graphic.

- **Segment 4 — The Party (5:00–7:00):** October 29, 1999 — iBASH '99 at the MGM Grand, reported cost
  $16 million. Confirmed lineup: KISS, Tony Bennett, Sugar Ray, Faith Hill, the Dixie Chicks, Natalie
  Cole, LeAnn Rimes, the Brian Setzer Orchestra, and a one-off reunion of The Who. The stated purpose:
  a live global demonstration of Pixelon's streaming technology. Visual: lineup graphic (icons only,
  no real likenesses), "$16,000,000" figure held on screen.

- **Segment 5 — The Failure (7:00–8:30):** The live stream failed. Viewers who tried to watch it
  remotely saw error messages. Most people who did see any stream saw it through Microsoft's
  competing software — not Pixelon's own technology. On-screen note: this was, in effect, a public,
  unscripted demonstration that the company's central product claim wasn't true. Visual: buffering/
  error-icon graphic, hard cut to "MOST VIEWERS WATCHED IT ON A COMPETITOR'S SOFTWARE."

- **Segment 6 — The Exposure (8:30–10:00):** April 2000 — Stanley's real identity became public,
  reported first and most thoroughly by the Seattle Times and Pollstar News, with The Register also
  covering it within a day. Fearing he'd been tracked to California, Stanley returned to Virginia and
  turned himself in at the Wise County jail. On-screen note: what happened to him after this point —
  whether his suspended sentence was reinstated, how much time if any he served — was not confirmed
  in this research; state that plainly rather than guessing. Visual: newspaper-headline treatment,
  calendar graphic landing on April 2000, "UNCONFIRMED AFTER THIS POINT" lower third.

- **Segment 7 — The Collapse (10:00–11:15):** Following the exposure, Pixelon's management team was
  ousted, most employees were laid off, and the company filed for bankruptcy by June 2000 — about
  eight months after the party. Visual: layoff graphic, bankruptcy date stamp.

- **Segment 8 — Two Frauds, Not One (11:15–12:30):** On-screen recap, stated directly: this story
  involves two distinct frauds by the same person, a decade apart — the 1989 conviction, and the
  Pixelon-era fraud built on the identity that conviction forced him into. Visual: two-event
  comparison graphic, clearly separated and dated.

- Mid-video CTA (~10:30): "This channel has covered real, adjudicated fraud before — HeadSpin, Ozy
  Media, Theranos. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson (12:30–14:00)
- Plain-language comparison: unlike this channel's other real-fraud cases, Pixelon's fraud was
  exposed not by a whistleblower or a court filing, but by the product itself failing live, on stage,
  in front of the exact audience it was built to impress.
- The core point, stated directly: the most expensive, most convincing pitch in the world still has
  to survive contact with reality — and sometimes that contact happens in front of thousands of
  people at once.
- Visual: "1999 — THE DEMO FAILS" text card, holding on the irony.

### Final CTA + Outro (14:00–15:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a fugitive raised tens of millions under a false name, staged
  the most expensive product demo of the dot-com era to prove a technology that didn't exist, and was
  exposed within months
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these"
- Engagement prompt: "What's more unbelievable to you — that he raised $30 million under a fake name,
  or that he spent $16 million on a demo he knew would fail? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + real-fraud playlist + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed timeline graphic (1989 Conviction → 1996 Flight → 1998 Founding →
1999 Raise → 1999 Party → 2000 Exposure → 2000 Collapse), then works backward into each stage in
detail. Graphics-forward, treats the video as a documented case-study resource.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the party itself, reconstructed beat by beat — the lineup, the crowd, the broadcast
countdown — before revealing the stream failed and the CEO wasn't who he claimed to be. Slower
pacing, holds the identity reveal as a mid-video turn.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "5 dates that tell this entire story" — 1989 (the first conviction), 1996 (the
flight), 1999 (the party), April 2000 (the exposure), June 2000 (the collapse) — rapid-fire through
each with its single sourced fact. Strong Shorts-extraction potential.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** Segment 1 (The First Fraud, 1989) if it reads as background lecture before the
  more visually interesting party segment
- **Fix:** keep it under 70 seconds and lead directly into the escape/new-name segment, which has
  higher narrative momentum
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general 1999 dot-com-boom context if audience familiarity is
  already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the failed demo in the cold open framing ("the technology
  didn't work — it had never worked") without over-explaining why until Segment 5
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small persistent timeline strip visible in a corner during
  Segments 3 through 7, so viewers always know where in the sequence they are
