## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week:
Pixelon — a video-streaming startup founded in 1998 in San Juan Capistrano, California, by a man
calling himself Michael Fenne. Fenne was not his real name. He was David Kim Stanley, a fugitive who
had fled Virginia in 1996 rather than complete restitution on a 1989 conviction for 55 fraud-related
counts — roughly $1.25 million taken from elderly victims in Virginia and Tennessee. That earlier
fraud has nothing to do with Pixelon's technology or its investors; it's the reason "Michael Fenne"
needed to exist at all.

As "Fenne," Stanley raised $30.9 million from investors through Advanced Equities, a Chicago
investment bank, pitching a proprietary video-streaming technology that promised full-screen,
TV-quality video over ordinary internet connections. On October 29, 1999, Pixelon staged "iBASH '99"
at the MGM Grand in Las Vegas — a reported $16 million launch party featuring KISS, Tony Bennett,
Sugar Ray, Faith Hill, the Dixie Chicks, Natalie Cole, LeAnn Rimes, the Brian Setzer Orchestra, and a
one-off reunion of The Who — meant to demonstrate the technology live to a global audience.

The stream failed. Viewers who tried to watch remotely saw error messages; most who saw anything saw
it through Microsoft's competing streaming software, not Pixelon's own technology. In April 2000,
Stanley's real identity became public, reported first by the Seattle Times and Pollstar News.
Fearing he'd been tracked to California, he returned to Virginia and turned himself in at the Wise
County jail. What happened to him after that point was not confirmed in this research — this video
states that plainly rather than guessing. Pixelon's management was ousted, most employees were laid
off, and the company collapsed into bankruptcy by June 2000.

This is a real-fraud story, in the same category as this channel's other adjudicated cases — 029
(Ozy Media) and 052 (Theranos) — but with a distinct wrinkle: the founder's criminal history predates
the company entirely, and the fraud was exposed not by a whistleblower or a court filing, but by the
product itself failing live, on stage, in front of the exact audience it was built to impress.
