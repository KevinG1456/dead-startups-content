# Blog Article: He Booked The Who and KISS for His Company's Launch Party. He Was a Wanted Fugitive the Whole Time.

*All figures sourced to the Seattle Times' original April 2000 reporting, Pollstar News, The

## Introduction
Most retellings of Pixelon lead with the twist: the CEO wasn't who he said he was. That's true, but
it skips most of the actual story. This article follows the complete, documented sequence: the 1989
conviction that made a false identity necessary, the 1998 founding, the $30.9 million raise, the $16
million launch party, the technology's public failure, the exposure, and the collapse — each fact
sourced, and the one genuine gap in the record (what happened to Stanley after 2000) stated honestly
rather than filled in with a guess.

## The First Fraud, 1989
In 1989, David Kim Stanley pleaded guilty to 55 fraud-related counts, admitting to taking roughly
$750,000 from Virginia residents and roughly $500,000 from Tennessee victims — about $1.25 million
combined, reported as a scheme that primarily targeted the elderly. The sentencing judge at Wise
County Circuit Court in Virginia reportedly described him as "the man who fleeced his father's
flock." He was sentenced to 36 years, with 28 suspended on the condition he repay his victims — a
deferred arrangement contingent on restitution. This event has nothing to do with Pixelon's
technology or its later investors; it's the reason "Michael Fenne" needed to exist at all.

## The Escape and the New Name
In 1996, Stanley fled before completing restitution and was placed on Virginia's "most wanted" list.
He arrived in California — reportedly living out of the back of his car — before reinventing himself
as "Michael Fenne."

## The Pitch and the Raise
In 1998, "Fenne" founded Pixelon, Inc. in San Juan Capistrano, California, pitching a proprietary
video-streaming technology that promised full-screen, TV-quality video over ordinary internet
connections — a genuine breakthrough claim for 1999's internet infrastructure. Advanced Equities, a
Chicago investment-banking firm, raised $30.9 million from private investors on the strength of that
pitch. A looser, commonly repeated figure of "$35 million" also appears in some retrospective
sources; this article uses $30.9 million as the more precisely sourced number.

## The Party
On October 29, 1999, Pixelon staged "iBASH '99" at the MGM Grand in Las Vegas — a reported $16
million launch party. The confirmed lineup, corroborated across multiple sources: KISS, Tony
Bennett, Sugar Ray, Faith Hill, the Dixie Chicks, Natalie Cole, LeAnn Rimes, the Brian Setzer
Orchestra, and a one-off reunion of The Who. The stated purpose was a live global demonstration of
Pixelon's streaming technology.

## The Failure
The live stream failed. Viewers who tried to watch remotely were shown error messages. Most people
who did see any stream saw it through Microsoft's competing streaming software, not Pixelon's own
technology — a direct, public demonstration that the company's central product claim wasn't true,
delivered not by a whistleblower but by the product itself, live, in front of the exact audience it
was built to impress.

## The Exposure
In April 2000, Stanley's true identity became public — the Seattle Times (April 16) and Pollstar
News (April 18) are the two strongest contemporaneous sources for this window, with The Register also
covering it within a day. Fearing he had been tracked to California by Virginia State Police,
Stanley returned to Virginia and turned himself in at the Wise County jail, confessing his real
identity. What happened to him after this point — whether his suspended sentence was reinstated, how
much time if any he ultimately served, his subsequent whereabouts — was not confirmed in this
research. This article states that plainly rather than speculating.

## The Collapse
Following the exposure, Pixelon's management team was ousted, most employees were laid off, and the
company collapsed into bankruptcy by June 2000 — roughly eight months after iBash '99 and about two
months after Stanley's exposure and surrender.

## Two Frauds, Not One
This story involves two distinct frauds by the same person, a decade apart: the 1989 conviction
(unrelated to Pixelon, involving different victims and a different scheme entirely) and the
Pixelon-era fraud (a false identity used to raise investor money on the strength of technology that
did not work). Conflating them into a single event misrepresents both. This article, like the video
it accompanies, keeps them explicitly separate throughout.

## The Actual Lesson
Unlike this channel's other real-fraud cases — Ozy Media (package 029) and Theranos (package 052) —
Pixelon's fraud wasn't exposed by a whistleblower, a court filing, or an investigative reporter
working a beat. It was exposed by the product itself, failing live, in front of the audience it was
built to impress. The most expensive, most convincing pitch in the world still has to survive contact
with reality — and here, that contact happened in front of thousands of people simultaneously.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A live demo is real due diligence** — Pixelon's technology survived slide decks and investor
  meetings for over a year; it did not survive one live, public test
- **A confident founder is not a verified founder** — $30.9 million was raised from investors under
  a name that turned out not to be real; basic identity verification would have changed this story
  entirely
- **Two similar-sounding facts can be entirely separate events** — the 1989 conviction and the
  Pixelon-era fraud are a decade apart and involve different victims; treating them as one story
  flattens what actually happened
- **An honest gap in the record is better than a filled-in guess** — this article states plainly
  that Stanley's post-2000 outcome wasn't confirmed, rather than repeating a secondhand claim that
  can't be sourced

## The Lesson
A fugitive hiding a real, decade-old fraud conviction built a company on a technology that never
worked, raised tens of millions of dollars under a false name, and staged the most expensive product
demonstration of the dot-com era to prove it — live, in front of thousands of people, where it
failed. Both the scale of the spectacle and the fact that it was built on nothing are true, and both
are stated here plainly, sourced, and without filling in the one part of the story that isn't
confirmed.
