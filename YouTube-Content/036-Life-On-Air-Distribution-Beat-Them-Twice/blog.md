# Blog Article: This Team Built the Best-Loved App in Its Category. Twice. Distribution Beat Them Both Times.

*All figures sourced to CNN Money, TechCrunch, Variety, Washington Post, Axios, NoCamels, Sequoia
Capital, and NFX — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This article is honest about what
is and isn't a clean claim: Meerkat's specific product-quality edge over Periscope is genuinely
disputed in contemporaneous reporting, so this article presents Meerkat's advantage as cultural
momentum, not a confirmed product-quality win. Houseparty's acclaim is well-quantified and carries the
"best product" half of this story's claim.*

## Introduction
Most people who remember Meerkat and most people who remember Houseparty don't know they're the same
story. Both apps were built by the same small team — Life On Air, founded in Israel in 2012 by Ben
Rubin and Itai Danino — in two completely different video categories, five years apart. Both apps
achieved real, documented acclaim. Both lost to competitors whose actual advantage was distribution,
not necessarily a better product. This is the connected version of that story.

## Round One: Meerkat
Meerkat launched in February 2015 and became the breakout star of that year's SXSW festival — CNN
Money's own headline at the time put it plainly: "Meerkat stole the show at SXSW. Sorry, Twitter." The
app rode that momentum to a $12 million round from Greylock Partners in March 2015.

The same day Twitter confirmed it was acquiring a directly competing live-streaming app, Periscope,
Twitter cut off Meerkat's access to its follower-graph API — the feature that let new Meerkat users
find people to follow using their existing Twitter social connections. Meerkat's own community
director said the team received only about two hours of advance notice. Periscope, as a Twitter-owned
product, kept its native integration.

**Here's the part worth stating honestly, because it changes the shape of the lesson:** some
contemporaneous reporting describes Periscope as the more polished product at the time — a cleaner UI,
and critically, the ability for users to replay past streams, a feature Meerkat's live-only format
didn't have. Meerkat won the cultural moment at SXSW. It did not clearly win a head-to-head product
comparison. What it lost, decisively, was the distribution channel Periscope kept and it didn't.
Meerkat shut down October 4, 2016, about nineteen months after its breakout.

*This channel has covered Meerkat's collapse before, in package 008, through the lens of platform API
dependency risk specifically. This article covers the same event more briefly and continues into the
part that package doesn't: what the same team built next.*

## The Pivot
In early 2015, around the same period Meerkat was losing its footing, Ben Rubin told investors Meerkat
"was not going to work." The team began developing a new idea: a group-video-chat app built around
casually "dropping in" on friends who were already online, rather than scheduling a call — eventually
named Houseparty.

## Round Two: Houseparty
Houseparty raised significant funding as it grew — a $52 million round for Life On Air was reported in
December 2016, tied specifically to Houseparty's momentum. In June 2019, Epic Games, the maker of
Fortnite, acquired Houseparty in a deal reported at approximately $35 million.

Then, in March 2020, as COVID-19 lockdowns spread, Houseparty's downloads spiked from about 533,000 in
February to roughly 17.2 million in March — growth of over 2,900% — briefly making it one of the most
downloaded apps in the world. And unlike Meerkat, Houseparty's acclaim during this period is
well-quantified: as of August 2020, it held a 4.9-out-of-5-star rating from more than 500,000 App
Store reviews. Contemporaneous coverage repeatedly and specifically praised its playful, socially
organic design against Zoom's more utilitarian, work-oriented interface — one widely-cited framing at
the time was "Zoom by day, Houseparty by night."

This is the clean version of "best product in the category." It's quantified. It's documented. And it
still wasn't enough.

Zoom's status as the default tool for a massive, sudden wave of remote work; Apple's built-in Group
FaceTime, requiring no download at all for iPhone users; and Facebook's Messenger Rooms, launched in
April 2020 inside an app already on most phones — all three had a structural distribution advantage
Houseparty, as a standalone app people had to separately choose to download, couldn't match. In the
first eight months of 2021, Houseparty logged roughly 8 million installs — down 83% year-over-year
from approximately 47 million installs in the same period of 2020.

On September 9, 2021, Epic Games announced it would shut down Houseparty, fully discontinuing the app
that October. Epic's own stated reasoning, from its blog post: the team was "working on creating new
ways to have meaningful and authentic social interactions at metaverse scale across the Epic Games
family" — its own account of a strategic reallocation, not a claim that Houseparty's product itself
had failed.

## The Actual Lesson
Put the two rounds side by side and the pattern is exact. Meerkat lost to a rival that could switch
off its distribution channel with two hours' notice. Houseparty lost to three rivals that didn't
require users to download anything separate at all. In neither case was the product the thing that
actually decided the outcome — and in Houseparty's case, the product was demonstrably, quantifiably
loved, and it still wasn't enough.

The lesson isn't that product quality doesn't matter. It's that product quality alone doesn't
neutralize a genuine distribution disadvantage — and a team can understand that lesson completely,
having lived through it once already, and still find themselves on the losing side of it again in a
different category five years later.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **A distribution disadvantage can survive genuine product acclaim** — Houseparty's 4.9-star rating
  from 500,000+ reviews didn't change the outcome once its competitors didn't require a separate
  download
- **A platform-owned competitor carries a switch that can be flipped in hours, not months** —
  Meerkat's two-hour API cutoff notice is the sharpest version of this risk
- **"Default" and "already installed" are themselves product features, whether or not they're
  designed ones** — Zoom, FaceTime, and Messenger Rooms won partly because they required zero
  additional decision from the user
- **Learning a lesson once doesn't guarantee avoiding it the second time** — the same team, having
  lived through Meerkat's collapse, still built a second product with the same fundamental
  distribution vulnerability
- **Acclaim and survival are different metrics, and only one of them is optional** — a product can be
  loved and still lose, if the thing it's competing against doesn't need to be chosen at all

## The Lesson
Meerkat and Houseparty are usually told as two separate nostalgia stories — one about a 2015 SXSW
sensation, one about a 2020 lockdown obsession. Told together, as one team's repeated experience, they
become something more useful: real, dated, quantified proof that a genuinely great product is not, on
its own, a defense against a competitor that simply doesn't ask users to choose it.
