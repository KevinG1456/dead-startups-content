# Blog Article: Yik Yak Hit a $400 Million Valuation. The Feature That Made It Popular Also Killed It.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, Forbes, The Washington Post, CBS News, and Fortune's
contemporaneous reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This article presents
the abuse/threat material factually and briefly, naming only the three independently-verified
individuals documented in the sources log. No fraud or misconduct framing is applied to founders
Tyler Droll or Brooks Buffington.*

## Introduction
Most product failures come from building the wrong thing. Yik Yak's failure is stranger: it built
exactly the right thing for a moment, grew explosively on the strength of one specific feature, and
then had to remove that exact feature to survive — only to discover the company couldn't survive
without it either.

## The Idea
Yik Yak was founded by Tyler Droll and Brooks Buffington, both connected to Furman University in
Greenville, South Carolina. The app launched publicly in November 2013: post anything, see only
what's posted nearby, with no name or profile attached. The hyperlocal, fully anonymous format spread
fast on college campuses, where it became a genuine cultural phenomenon.

## The Money
A November 2014 funding round valued Yik Yak at $400.5 million, on a total of roughly $73.4-73.5
million raised from investors including Sequoia Capital, DCM Ventures, and Azure Capital Partners.
The company had grown from a dorm-room idea to a company valued at nearly half a billion dollars in
about one year.

## A Founding Dispute
Before Yik Yak, the app was reportedly built under a three-way partnership called Locus Engineering,
split evenly between Droll, Buffington, and a fraternity brother, Douglas Warstler. Droll and
Buffington dissolved that partnership in early 2013 and founded Yik Yak LLC without Warstler, who sued
in November 2014 alleging he'd been unfairly cut out. The case settled in April 2016 on undisclosed
terms — an ordinary business dispute, not a fraud allegation.

## What Anonymity Enabled
The same feature that made Yik Yak popular also made it a tool for real threats and harassment.
Multiple documented arrests followed at colleges nationwide. In October 2015, a student at Emory
University's Oxford College was arrested for a Yik Yak post threatening to "open fire" on campus. In
November 2015, a Missouri University of Science and Technology student was arrested for a Yik Yak
threat targeting Black students and faculty at the University of Missouri, during that campus's
Concerned Student 1950 protests. A Fresno State football player was arrested within about two hours of
a threatening post. National reporting at the time described more than a dozen campus threat
investigations tied to the app in a single academic year.

## The Fix
In August 2016, Yik Yak rolled out a major pivot: requiring users to add a handle and photo, aimed
directly at curbing anonymous abuse. The problem was structural — the fix required removing the exact
feature that had made the app popular in the first place. One user at the time described it as
"contrary to the whole idea of Yik Yak."

## The Backlash
Usage collapsed almost immediately. Some local communities reportedly saw post volume fall to as few
as 5 posts a day.

## The Apology
In November 2016, Droll and Buffington released a public video admitting "we messed this one up" and
reversed the change, making handles optional again. The reversal came too late to restore engagement.
By the end of 2016, downloads were down 76% year over year compared to the same period in 2015, and
the company laid off most of its staff.

## The Sale
In April 2017, payments company Square agreed to hire a number of Yik Yak's engineers and license some
of its intellectual property for roughly $1 million. The remaining app shut down days later — a
company that had been worth $400.5 million twenty-nine months earlier.

## Not the Same Company
A completely different, unrelated team purchased the Yik Yak brand rights in February 2021 and
relaunched the app that August, with new safety and moderation features. That revived app was itself
acquired by a rival anonymous campus app, Sidechat, in March 2023 — an acquisition, not a second
shutdown — and continues operating today. This is a different company with different owners; it
carries no continuity with Droll and Buffington's original decisions.

## The Actual Lesson
When your entire product is built on one feature, you generally can't safely remove that feature to
fix its downside — you either solve the downside from within the feature itself, or the product stops
being the product that anyone wanted. Yik Yak tried to keep the app while removing what made it the
app, and lost its user base doing it.

This is a different mechanism from this channel's other platform-dependency stories (package 008,
Meerkat/Viddy; package 036, Life On Air/Houseparty; package 039, Twitterrific) — those companies were
cut off by an external platform decision. Yik Yak's core feature turned into its own liability, and
the fix for that liability is what actually ended the company.

## What Founders and Product Teams Should Take From This
- **A feature that drives your growth can also be your single point of failure** — the mechanism that
  makes a product popular and the mechanism that makes it dangerous can be the exact same mechanism
- **There may be no safe way to "fix" a core feature's downside without removing the feature itself**
  — Yik Yak's attempt to curb abuse required eliminating anonymity, which was the product
- **A public apology and a quick reversal don't guarantee recovered trust or engagement** — Droll and
  Buffington's November 2016 apology video was genuine and fast, and it still wasn't enough
- **A company's brand can outlive the company itself under entirely different ownership** — the "Yik
  Yak" name today belongs to a separate team with no connection to the original decisions covered here
- **Real, sourced facts about a content-moderation failure are enough to tell this story** — no fraud
  or misconduct framing is needed or appropriate

## The Lesson
Yik Yak's story is a reminder that a product's greatest strength and its greatest liability can be the
exact same design decision. A $400.5 million company tried to solve that tension by removing the
decision that built it — and discovered there was no version of the product left that users actually
wanted.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What was Yik Yak?**
An anonymous, hyperlocal social app that let users post to a nearby feed with no name or profile
attached, launched in November 2013.

**2. Who founded Yik Yak?**
Tyler Droll and Brooks Buffington, both connected to Furman University in South Carolina.

**3. How much funding did Yik Yak raise?**
Roughly $73.4-73.5 million, from investors including Sequoia Capital, DCM Ventures, and Azure Capital
Partners.

**4. What was Yik Yak's peak valuation?**
$400.5 million, set in a November 2014 funding round.

**5. Was there a founding dispute?**
Yes — a third early collaborator, Douglas Warstler, sued in November 2014 claiming he'd been cut out
of the company; the case settled confidentially in April 2016.

**6. What made Yik Yak popular?**
Total anonymity combined with a hyperlocal, GPS-based feed — no profile, no followers, just posts from
people nearby.

**7. What went wrong with the anonymity model?**
It also enabled sustained threats and harassment, leading to real arrests at multiple college
campuses.

**8. Can you name any of the real incidents?**
Yes — three independently verified, named cases: a 2015 threat arrest at Emory University, a 2015
threat arrest at Missouri University of Science and Technology tied to threats against Black students
and faculty at Mizzou, and a threat arrest involving a Fresno State student.

**9. How did Yik Yak try to fix the abuse problem?**
In August 2016, it required users to add a handle and photo — removing the anonymity that was the
core of the product.

**10. How did users react to the handles requirement?**
Very negatively — usage collapsed almost immediately, with some local feeds seeing as few as 5 posts a
day.

**11. Did the founders acknowledge the mistake?**
Yes — in November 2016, Droll and Buffington released a public video admitting "we messed this one
up" and reversed the change.

**12. Did reversing the change fix the problem?**
No — engagement never recovered. By the end of 2016, downloads were down 76% year over year.

**13. How did Yik Yak's original run end?**
In April 2017, Square paid roughly $1 million for a handful of engineers and an IP license; the app
shut down days later.

**14. How much did Yik Yak's value fall from peak to sale?**
From a $400.5 million valuation (Nov 2014) to roughly $1 million (Apr 2017) — about 29 months later.

**15. Is Yik Yak still around today?**
A version of the app is — but it's a completely different company. A new team bought the brand rights
in February 2021 and relaunched it in August 2021.

**16. Did the 2021 revival shut down too?**
No — it was acquired by a rival app, Sidechat, in March 2023, and continues operating today. It was
not a second shutdown.

**17. Are the 2013-2017 founders connected to the current app?**
No — the 2021 relaunch team is unrelated to Tyler Droll and Brooks Buffington's original company.

**18. Was this a fraud story?**
No — this is a content-moderation and product-pivot failure story, not a financial-misconduct one.

**19. Were Droll or Buffington charged with anything?**
No — neither founder faced any criminal charges or convictions in connection with this story.

**20. What is Locus Engineering?**
The original three-way partnership (Droll, Buffington, Warstler) that reportedly built the app before

**21. What happened with the Warstler lawsuit?**
He sued in November 2014 alleging he was unfairly cut out of the company; it settled on undisclosed

**22. Who was Yik Yak's buyer in 2017?**
Square, the payments company (later renamed Block, Inc. in December 2021, after this deal).

**24. Has this channel covered a similar story before?**

**25. How is Yik Yak's story different from those?**
Yik Yak wasn't cut off by an outside platform — its own core feature became a liability, and the fix

**26. What is the core risk this story illustrates?**
That a product's defining feature and its greatest liability can be the exact same design decision,

**27. Could Yik Yak have avoided this outcome?**
Not within its existing model — anonymity was both the product and the risk; the lesson is structural,
