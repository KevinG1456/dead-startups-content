Sources Log — Package 056: Yik Yak
Filled in: 2026-08-16

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Yik Yak is a fresh, cold-researched title (not from the dead-saas-30 batch) — an anonymous,
location-based social app that became a genuine campus phenomenon, then collapsed under the weight of
the exact feature that made it popular: anonymity itself enabled sustained harassment and threats,
and the fix (removing anonymity) killed the product. Distinct failure mechanism from every other
package on this channel: not funding-driven, not a single dramatic week, but a slow platform-safety
collapse followed by a de-anonymization pivot that alienated its own user base.

No fraud/criminal issues for founders Tyler Droll or Brooks Buffington — this is a content-moderation
and product-market-fit story, not a financial-misconduct one.

---

FOUNDING AND ORIGINAL FOUNDING-TEAM DISPUTE
- Yik Yak was founded by Tyler Droll and Brooks Buffington, both students/graduates of Furman
  University in Greenville, South Carolina. The app launched publicly in November 2013.
- Before Yik Yak LLC existed, the underlying app was reportedly built under a three-way partnership
  called Locus Engineering, split evenly between Droll, Buffington, and a fraternity brother, Douglas
  Warstler. Droll and Buffington dissolved Locus Engineering in early 2013 and went on to found Yik
  Yak LLC without Warstler.
- Warstler sued in November 2014, claiming he had been unfairly cut out of one-third of the company.
  The suit was settled in April 2016; settlement terms were not disclosed. Droll and Buffington's
  public statement: "We are pleased we were able to come to this resolution, and we acknowledge the
  contributions Doug made to the early ideas behind Yik Yak."
Source: Forbes ("Popular Teen App Yik Yak Sued By Supposed Frat Brother Cofounder," Nov 18 2014);
Forbes ("Yik Yak Settles With Ousted Cofounder," Apr 22 2016); Inc.com.

THE ABUSE AND THREATS PROBLEM — NAMED, VERIFIED INCIDENTS
Yik Yak's anonymity and hyperlocal design were repeatedly used to post threats, harassment, and
racist content at colleges nationwide, leading to numerous police investigations and arrests. Three
independently verified, named incidents:
- October 2015: Emory University (Oxford College campus) student Emily Sakamoto was arrested after
  police determined she posted a threat via Yik Yak indicating she would "open fire" on campus and
  warning others to stay in their rooms.
  Source: The Washington Post (Oct 13 2015).
- November 2015: Hunter Park, a 19-year-old student at Missouri University of Science and Technology,
  was arrested on suspicion of making a terrorist threat via Yik Yak targeting Black students and
  faculty at the University of Missouri (Mizzou), amid that campus's Concerned Student 1950 protests.
  Source: The Washington Post (Nov 12 2015); IBTimes.
- Christian Pryor, an 18-year-old then-Fresno State football player, was arrested within roughly two
  hours of a Yik Yak post allegedly threatening to use a weapon on campus.
  Source: Jezebel; multiple contemporaneous news reports (exact 2015 date not independently pinned
  down in this pass — [VERIFY] precise date).
- Multiple additional arrests and "more than a dozen" campus threat investigations in a single
  academic year were reported nationally; use the three named cases above as the on-screen anchors
  and characterize the broader pattern generally rather than naming unverified additional individuals.
Source: CBS News ("Yik Yak, 'anonymous' social media app, shares info with police"); The Maneater.

THE 2016 "HANDLES" PIVOT — REMOVING ANONYMITY TO CURB ABUSE
- August 2016: Yik Yak rolled out a major product pivot requiring users to add a handle (username)
  and photo, and to post local "status" updates — explicitly aimed at curbing anonymous harassment
  by reducing anonymity and pushing toward a more identity-based, local-social-network model.
- The change was deeply unpopular; one user description at the time called it "contrary to the whole
  idea of Yik Yak." Usage collapsed — some local communities reportedly saw post volume fall to as
  low as 5 posts a day.
- November 2016: co-founders Tyler Droll and Brooks Buffington released a public video apologizing
  for the pivot, admitting "we messed this one up," and reversed course, making handles optional
  again. The reversal did not restore prior engagement levels.
- By the end of 2016, app downloads had fallen 76% year-over-year compared to the same period in
  2015, and the company laid off most of its staff.
Source: TechCrunch ("Yik Yak yearns to be local, not just anonymous," Aug 16 2016); Digital Trends
("Yik Yak isn't so anonymous anymore"); Fortune ("Yik Yak layoffs," Dec 10 2016); TechCrunch (Apr 28
2017 shutdown piece, citing the 76% download decline).

THE END — APRIL 2017, SOLD FOR PARTS TO SQUARE (NOT A CASH ACQUISITION OF THE COMPANY)
- April 2017: payments company Square, Inc. (which later renamed itself Block, Inc. in December
  2021 — Yik Yak's actual buyer at the time was still branded "Square") agreed to hire a number of
  Yik Yak's engineers and acquire a non-exclusive license to some of Yik Yak's intellectual property
  for approximately $1 million.
- This was reported by TechCrunch (headline and body) as "$1 million." [VERIFY: one secondary outlet,
  EdSurge, used "$3M" in its article's URL slug while the visible headline text says "$1M" — treat
  this as a likely EdSurge slug/typo error and use the better-corroborated $1 million figure from
  TechCrunch's April 28, 2017 reporting.]
- The remaining Yik Yak app was shut down shortly after, in late April 2017 — a fall from a $400.5M
  peak valuation (Nov 2014) to a ~$1M asset/talent sale roughly two and a half years later.
Source: TechCrunch ("Yik Yak shuts down after Square paid $1 million for its engineers," Apr 28
2017); EdSurge (Apr 26 2017, headline text "$1M," URL slug reads "$3m" — discrepancy noted above);
Fortune ("Yik Yak officially calls it quits," Apr 29 2017).

CATEGORY / CROSS-REFERENCE NOTES
- Distinct failure mechanism from this channel's other platform/API-dependency stories (008 Meerkat/
  Viddy, 036 Life On Air/Houseparty, 039 Twitterrific) — Yik Yak's collapse was not caused by an
  external platform revoking access; it was caused by the app's own core feature (anonymity) becoming
  a liability, and the fix for that liability killing the product's appeal. A cleaner comparison point
  is a "the feature that made you popular is the feature that kills you" story, not an API-dependency
  one.
- No fraud, financial-misconduct, or criminal allegations apply to Droll or Buffington. The Warstler
