# 017 — Convoy: How a Secured Lender Ended Up Owning What Was Left of a $3.8B Startup

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built around Convoy Inc., a real, extensively-reported case (CNBC, TechCrunch, GeekWire, FreightWaves, Wikipedia — full citations in `sources-log.txt`). This is a mechanism-explainer anchored by a real company, not a company autopsy first: the goal is to teach how venture debt's secured-lien structure gives a lender leverage that equity holders — and even a company's own leadership — don't have once distress hits.

**The real case:** Convoy — a Seattle-based digital freight brokerage (an "Uber for trucking," matching shippers with independent truckers), founded in 2015 by ex-Amazon executives Dan Lewis and Grant Goodale — raised $260M in April 2022: $160M in Series E equity (led by Baillie Gifford and T. Rowe Price) plus $100M in secured venture debt from Hercules Capital, alongside a separate new $150M line of credit from J.P. Morgan. The round valued Convoy at $3.8B. Eighteen months later, a historic freight-market downturn combined with what CEO Dan Lewis called "dramatic monetary tightening" crushed the business and killed its best acquisition prospects at the same time. Convoy shut down on October 19, 2023, after more than four months of exhausted strategic options. Its heavy secured-debt load meant that when the company failed, Hercules Capital — the venture debt lender — was positioned to foreclose on Convoy's assets (technology, IP, accounts receivable) and take effective control of the wind-down, rather than Convoy's own board running a standard, company-led sale process. Convoy's technology stack was ultimately acquired by Flexport in November 2023 — reported by some sources at roughly $16M `[VERIFY: other outlets describe the price as undisclosed — confirm the exact figure before citing it on screen]` — a steep comedown from a $3.8B valuation just 18 months earlier.

**Important honesty note:** public reporting on Convoy documents Hercules Capital's foreclosure on Convoy's assets and its resulting "effective control" of the wind-down, and separately documents Convoy's heavy secured-debt structure ($100M Hercules venture debt + $150M JPMorgan line of credit). It does **not** document a specific numeric covenant that was breached, a specific breach date, or a specific cash balance Convoy held at the moment Hercules acted. Do not state a "$X in the bank" figure as Convoy's confirmed balance on the day of the foreclosure — none is sourced, and this package deliberately does not invent one (see the original `[VERIFY: $8M]` warning this package shipped with). The video's teaching core — how minimum-cash covenants, Material Adverse Change (MAC) clauses, and blanket liens generally work, and how they let a secured lender act fast in a distress scenario — is accurate to general venture debt industry practice, sourced separately from Convoy's own case (see `sources-log.txt`), and should be presented as the generalized mechanism that Convoy's story illustrates in outcome (a secured lender ending up in control), not as a beat-by-beat account of Convoy hitting a specific numeric trigger.

**Framing requirement:** Convoy's story doesn't need an invented figure to land — the real numbers already carry it. A company that raised $260M (including $100M in secured venture debt) at a $3.8B valuation had its core technology sold off for a small reported fraction of that within 18 months, in a process substantially controlled by its senior secured lender rather than its own board or equity holders. Lead with the real figures. Where the video needs a concrete "cash covenant" example to teach the mechanism, use a clearly-labeled generic/illustrative figure (see script.md) — never presented as Convoy's own balance.
