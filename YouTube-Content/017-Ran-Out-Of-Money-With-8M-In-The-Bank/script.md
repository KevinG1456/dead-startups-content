## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 12–15 minutes

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company raised $260 million at a $3.8 billion valuation. Eighteen months later, its lender owned what was left."
- Visual: "$3.8B" graphic animates in, a padlock and chain wrap around it, a heavy SFX hit, the number shrinks
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This actually happened. And it was completely legal."

### Hook (0:15–0:50)
- VO: "This is what happened at Convoy — a Seattle freight-marketplace startup backed by Jeff Bezos, Bill Gates, and some of the biggest names in venture capital. It raised hundreds of millions of dollars. It still ended up with its lender, not its own board, deciding what happened to it."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how venture debt covenants work, why a secured lender can end up with more control over a distressed company than its own equity holders, and what specifically happened to Convoy."

### Introduction (0:50–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from public company statements, the CEO's own shutdown memo, and contemporaneous press coverage (CNBC, TechCrunch, GeekWire, FreightWaves) — sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this is a mechanism explainer as much as a company story — the general lesson about secured venture debt applies to any founder considering debt financing, regardless of industry

### What Is Venture Debt (1:40–4:15)
- Plain-language explanation: venture debt as a loan (not equity), typically layered alongside a funding round, usually secured by a lien on company assets — which can include cash, receivables, and IP, not just equipment
- Introduce covenants generally: minimum cash balance requirements, revenue/growth thresholds, Material Adverse Change (MAC) clauses — sourced to general venture-debt lending practice (see `sources-log.txt`), not to Convoy specifically
- Explicitly flag on screen: "the general mechanism below is accurate to how venture debt typically works — we don't have a confirmed, public record of the exact covenant Convoy's agreement contained"
- Visual: simple diagram of a venture debt structure, covenant types

### Main Content — The Convoy Case (4:15–10:30)
- Segment 1 — The Raise (4:15–6:00): April 2022, Convoy raises $260M at a $3.8B valuation — $160M in Series E equity led by Baillie Gifford and T. Rowe Price, plus $100M in secured venture debt from Hercules Capital, alongside a separate new $150M line of credit from J.P. Morgan. Total lifetime funding reportedly over $1B across investors including Jeff Bezos, Bill Gates, Marc Benioff, Greylock, Generation Investment Management, and CapitalG. Visual: funding-round timeline graphic building to the $3.8B peak.
- Segment 2 — The Collapse (6:00–8:00): 2022–2023 brings a historic freight-market downturn — falling freight rates and overcapacity crush margins for a company whose entire business is brokering trucking capacity. Layoffs begin; headcount falls from a peak of roughly 1,500 to roughly 500. CEO Dan Lewis's team spends more than four months searching for a buyer or a path forward. Visual: headcount graph declining, freight-rate index declining in parallel.
- Segment 3 — Who Actually Controls the Wind-Down (8:00–10:30): explain the mechanism plainly — because $100M of Convoy's capital structure was *secured* venture debt (not equity), Hercules Capital held a contractual claim on Convoy's assets that ranked ahead of equity holders. When Convoy couldn't find a buyer and shut down on October 19, 2023, Hercules foreclosed on Convoy's assets — its technology, IP, and accounts receivable — and took effective control of the wind-down, subsequently marketing Convoy's tech stack to buyers rather than Convoy's own board running a standard sale process. Convoy's technology was ultimately bought by Flexport in November 2023 — reported at roughly $16M by some outlets `[VERIFY: confirm exact figure/sourcing before stating on screen]`. Visual: the "who's actually in control" diagram — equity holders at the bottom of the stack, secured lender at the top.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:00): "If you're considering venture debt right now, the next part is exactly what you need. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### Story — The Fall (10:30–12:30)
- CEO Dan Lewis's memo to employees: "We hoped this day would never come." His own framing — "an unprecedented freight market collapse" combined with "dramatic monetary tightening" that "crushed our progress at the same time that it was crushing our logical strategic acquirer... it was the perfect storm."
- The aftermath: roughly 50 of Convoy's former employees, including Lewis himself, joined Flexport as part of the tech-stack deal; the rest of what had been a 1,500-person company did not. Convoy's equity holders — the people who owned the $3.8B valuation on paper — were left with a company whose core asset had just been sold off in a lender-driven process.
- Slow the pacing deliberately here — this is the human cost beat, not another data segment

### Final CTA + Outro (12:30–14:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — venture debt is a legitimate and often valuable financing tool, but a secured lender's claim can outrank everyone else's — including the company's own board — the moment things go wrong, and that's a fundamentally different risk than simply running out of cash
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you use or ship freight through Convoy? Or have you dealt with venture debt covenants yourself? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: two related autopsies (Down Rounds, Acquired for $1) + subscribe
