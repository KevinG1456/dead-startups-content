# Blog Article: Convoy Raised $260M at a $3.8B Valuation. 18 Months Later, Its Lender Took What Was Left.

*All figures sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Where a specific figure is still unconfirmed (the exact Flexport sale price), it's flagged inline.*

## Introduction
In April 2022, Convoy — a Seattle-based digital freight brokerage founded by two ex-Amazon executives — raised $260 million at a $3.8 billion valuation, backed by investors including Jeff Bezos, Bill Gates, and some of the largest names in venture capital and growth equity. Eighteen months later, in October 2023, Convoy shut down. What happened next is the more interesting part of the story: because a large portion of that $260 million was *secured* venture debt rather than equity, Convoy's lender — not its board, not its founders, not its equity holders — ended up controlling what happened to the company's remaining assets. This article explains how that mechanism works, what specifically happened to Convoy, and what founders should scrutinize before taking on venture debt of their own.

## The Raise
Convoy was founded in 2015 by Dan Lewis and Grant Goodale, both former Amazon executives, building an "Uber for trucking" marketplace that matched independent truckers with shippers needing capacity. Early backers reportedly included Jeff Bezos personally, Bill Gates, Marc Benioff, and Greylock. The company raised through a string of major rounds: a $185 million Series C in September 2018 (valuing it at roughly $1 billion), a $400 million Series D in November 2019 co-led by Generation Investment Management and T. Rowe Price (valuing it at roughly $2.75 billion), and finally, in April 2022, a $260 million raise — $160 million in Series E equity led by Baillie Gifford and T. Rowe Price, plus $100 million in secured venture debt from Hercules Capital — alongside a separate new $150 million line of credit from J.P. Morgan. This final round valued Convoy at $3.8 billion. Convoy's lifetime funding is reported at over $1 billion across its history.

## The Collapse
Freight brokerage is a cyclical, margin-sensitive business, and 2022–2023 brought one of the sharpest downturns the industry had seen in years: falling freight rates and persistent overcapacity squeezed margins across the sector. Convoy's headcount fell from a peak of roughly 1,500 employees to roughly 500 through a series of restructurings, including a round of cuts in February 2023. CEO Dan Lewis later said the company spent more than four months exhausting every viable strategic option — additional fundraising, a sale, a merger — before concluding none of them would work.

## Who Actually Controls a Company in Distress
This is the part of the story most coverage of Convoy's shutdown treated as a footnote, but it's the part with the broader lesson: venture debt is a loan, not equity, and it is typically *secured* — meaning the lender has a contractual claim on specific company assets (which can include cash, receivables, and intellectual property, not just equipment) that ranks ahead of equity holders' claims. Because $100 million of Convoy's final funding round was secured venture debt from Hercules Capital, Hercules held a claim on Convoy's assets that outranked the company's own shareholders when Convoy's business failed.

When Convoy shut down on October 19, 2023, Hercules Capital foreclosed on Convoy's assets — its technology, intellectual property, and accounts receivable — and took effective control of the wind-down. Rather than Convoy's own board running a standard, company-led sale process, it was Convoy's senior secured lender that ended up marketing the company's technology to potential buyers. Convoy's tech stack was ultimately acquired by Flexport in November 2023, with some reporting placing the price at roughly $16 million `[VERIFY: other outlets describe the price as undisclosed — confirm before citing a specific figure]`. Flexport explicitly did not acquire Convoy the company or its liabilities — just the technology and a small group of employees, including CEO Dan Lewis himself, who joined Flexport before later moving to Microsoft in 2025.

**Important distinction:** public reporting confirms Hercules foreclosed on Convoy's assets and took effective control — it does not document a specific numeric covenant that was breached, a specific breach date, or Convoy's exact cash balance at the time. This article does not assert those unconfirmed details as fact.

## How Venture Debt Covenants Generally Work
Separately from Convoy's specific case, it's worth understanding the general mechanics that make this kind of outcome possible for any venture-debt-backed company:
- **Minimum cash balance covenants** — many venture debt agreements (including publicly-filed Hercules Technology Growth Capital loan templates) require the borrower to maintain "unrestricted cash" equal to a percentage of the outstanding loan principal, commonly cited in the range of roughly 35% after certain milestones, rising to 50–70% if those milestones aren't met.
- **Material Adverse Change (MAC) clauses** — broad provisions letting the lender act if the company's overall financial condition deteriorates significantly, even without breaching a specific numeric threshold.
- **Blanket liens and tri-party control agreements** — because venture debt is frequently secured by essentially all of a company's assets, lenders often negotiate a tri-party agreement with the company's bank that gives the lender the contractual right to take control of deposit accounts if a default occurs.
- **Acceleration and foreclosure rights** — on default, a lender can typically demand immediate repayment of the full loan balance and, because the loan is secured, move to seize or foreclose on collateral to recover its position.

## What Founders Should Scrutinize Before Signing
- **The specific covenants**, not just the interest rate and loan amount — understand exactly what triggers a default
- **What "cure periods" exist** — how much time the company has to fix a breach before the lender can act
- **What the lender's rights actually are upon default** — specifically what collateral is covered by the security interest, and how quickly the lender could move to take control of it
- **How much of the company's total capital structure is secured debt versus equity** — the higher that ratio, the more control shifts toward the lender if things go wrong, as Convoy's case illustrates

## The Lesson
Venture debt is a legitimate and often valuable financing tool — it's less dilutive than equity and can meaningfully extend runway. But it carries a distinct risk that pure equity financing does not: a secured lender's contractual claim can outrank everyone else's, including the company's own founders and board, the moment the business gets into serious trouble. Convoy's story — a $3.8 billion valuation, followed eighteen months later by its own lender controlling the fire sale of what was left — is a real, well-documented illustration of exactly how that can happen. Founders considering venture debt should give the same careful scrutiny to covenant and security terms that they would to any other major legal commitment, not just the headline interest rate and loan amount.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What is venture debt?**
A loan product for venture-backed startups, typically layered alongside an equity round, that must be repaid with interest and is usually secured by company assets.

**2. What is a loan covenant?**
A condition written into a loan agreement that the borrower must maintain throughout the life of the loan, such as a minimum cash balance or revenue threshold.

**3. What happened to Convoy?**
Convoy, a Seattle freight-brokerage startup, raised $260 million at a $3.8 billion valuation in April 2022 (including $100 million in secured venture debt from Hercules Capital) and shut down in October 2023 after a historic freight-market downturn. Its lender, Hercules Capital, foreclosed on its assets and took effective control of the wind-down; its technology was later sold to Flexport.

**4. Why did Convoy shut down?**
CEO Dan Lewis cited an "unprecedented freight market collapse" combined with "dramatic monetary tightening," which he said crushed both Convoy's own progress and the prospects of its most likely acquirer at the same time.

**5. Who is Hercules Capital?**
A specialty venture debt lender that provided Convoy with $100 million in secured venture debt as part of its April 2022 funding round.

**6. What does it mean that Hercules "foreclosed" on Convoy's assets?**
Because Hercules's loan was secured by a claim on Convoy's assets, when Convoy failed, Hercules was able to exercise its rights as a secured creditor to take control of those assets (technology, IP, accounts receivable) rather than Convoy's own board managing the outcome.

**7. Who bought Convoy's technology?**
Flexport acquired Convoy's technology stack in November 2023, along with a small group of employees including former CEO Dan Lewis. Flexport did not acquire Convoy the company or its liabilities.

**10. What specific covenant did Convoy breach?**

**11. How is a cash sweep different from a foreclosure?**
A cash sweep specifically refers to a lender taking control of a company's bank accounts upon default; foreclosure is the broader term for a secured lender seizing/taking control of any collateral, which can include cash accounts, receivables, and other assets.

**12. Is this legal?**
Yes — a secured lender exercising rights it negotiated as part of a loan agreement is a standard, legally enforceable outcome under the terms the borrower agreed to.

**13. Why would a lender have rights over a company's technology or receivables, not just cash?**
Because venture debt is typically secured by a blanket lien covering most or all company assets, not just cash — giving the lender a legal claim across the business to protect its position if the loan is in default.

**14. What happened to Convoy's employees?**
Headcount fell from a peak of roughly 1,500 to roughly 500 through 2022–2023 restructurings; roughly 50 employees, including Dan Lewis, joined Flexport as part of the technology deal. The rest did not.

**15. What happened to Convoy's equity holders?**
The company's equity — including the value implied by its $3.8 billion peak valuation — was left with essentially nothing once the secured lender's claim was satisfied through the asset foreclosure and sale.

**16. Is venture debt a bad idea for startups?**
Not inherently — it can be a useful, less dilutive financing tool, but it carries risks that equity financing doesn't, and covenant/security terms deserve careful scrutiny.

**17. What should founders look for before signing a venture debt agreement?**
The specific covenants and their thresholds, available cure periods, exactly what collateral is covered by the security interest, and exactly what rights the lender has upon default.

**18. What is a "cure period"?**
A window of time, specified in the loan agreement, during which a borrower can fix a covenant breach before the lender is entitled to take action.

**19. Who typically provides venture debt?**
Specialty lenders and certain banks that focus on venture-backed companies, such as Hercules Capital, TriplePoint, Trinity Capital, and historically Silicon Valley Bank, among others.

**20. Is venture debt common among startups?**
Yes — it's a fairly common financing tool, often used to extend runway between equity rounds without additional dilution.

**21. How is venture debt different from a traditional bank loan?**
Venture debt lenders typically underwrite based on a company's venture-backing and growth trajectory rather than traditional profitability metrics, and often include equity kickers (warrants) as part of the deal.

**22. What are warrants in a venture debt context?**
The right for the lender to purchase equity in the company at a set price, typically included as additional compensation for the lender's risk in venture debt deals.

**23. Can covenant terms be negotiated?**
Yes — like most loan terms, covenants are generally negotiable, particularly for companies with strong leverage or advised by experienced counsel.

**24. Did the broader 2023 freight downturn affect other companies besides Convoy?**
Yes — the 2022–2023 freight-rate downturn and overcapacity affected the trucking and logistics industry broadly; Convoy's collapse was one of the highest-profile venture-backed casualties of that cycle.
