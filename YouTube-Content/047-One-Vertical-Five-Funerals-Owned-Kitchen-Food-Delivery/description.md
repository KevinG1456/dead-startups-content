## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week is five
companies instead of one — all in the exact same narrow category, on-demand meal delivery where the
startup cooks the food itself, rather than running a marketplace of restaurants.

**SpoonRocket** (founded 2013 by Steven Hsiao and Anson Tsui, Berkeley CA) raised $13.5 million and was
the first of these five to die — March 2016. Its own shutdown statement named competitor Sprig
directly, unusually specific for this kind of announcement. Its assets were later acquired by
Brazil-based iFood.

**Sprig** (founded 2013 by Gagan Biyani, San Francisco) raised roughly $56-58 million, hit a
founder-confirmed run rate of "$21-22 million" while losing about $850,000 a month, and shut down May
26, 2017. Full episode on this channel: package 001.

**Maple** (founded 2014 by Caleb Merkl and Akshay Navle, backed by celebrity chef David Chang) raised
roughly $50 million+ and shut down in May 2017 — except it wasn't announced as a shutdown at all. Maple
called it "joining" Deliveroo, which acquired its technology and team.

**Munchery** (founded 2011 by Tri Tran and Conrad Chu, San Francisco) raised over $120 million — more
than the other three combined — and lasted the longest, until January 2019. It still failed the exact
same way. Its Chapter 11 filing named the causes on the record, in writing: "increased competition,
over-funding, aggressive expansion efforts, and Blue Apron's failed IPO." Full episode on this channel:
package 023.

**Josephine** (founded 2014 by Charley Wang and Tal Safran) raised just over $2 million — by far the
smallest of the five — and is the deliberate twist in this video. It wasn't an owned-kitchen company at
all, and it wasn't killed by unit economics. It was shut down, market by market, by health-department
cease-and-desist orders, and finally wound down in early 2018 after concluding it couldn't afford to
keep fighting regulators while also running the business.

Four companies, one shared mechanism. One company, a completely different cause of death — proof that
even inside one narrow vertical, the lesson isn't always the same.
