# Script Package

Anchored on five companies — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR
THIS SCRIPT: four companies (Sprig, Munchery, SpoonRocket, Maple) share the SAME core failure mechanism
— an owned-kitchen cost structure that never produced sub-restaurant-margin meals at delivery-app
prices and scale. Josephine is the DELIBERATE outlier — smallest funding, a marketplace (not
owned-kitchen) model, killed by regulatory/legal pressure, not unit economics. State this contrast
explicitly on screen as the video's twist, not as a mismatched inclusion. Reuse Sprig's and Munchery's
figures exactly as sourced in packages 001 and 023 — do not restate them with different numbers. No
fraud or criminal allegations apply to anyone across all five companies — keep the tone analytical.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 17–20 minutes (pattern setup, five company segments, and the shared/broken lesson,
all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Five companies. One narrow category — on-demand meal delivery, where the startup cooks the food
  itself. All five are gone within a few years of each other. Four of them died the exact same way.
  One of them didn't."
- Visual: five small company-logo icons on a single timeline, four fading to gray in sequence, the
  fifth fading differently (a different color/pattern)
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't five unrelated failures.
  It's one repeatable mistake — and one exception. Watch for which one breaks the pattern."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This video covers five real companies — Sprig, Munchery, SpoonRocket, Maple, and Josephine —
  that each built a version of the same idea: cook the food yourselves, control the whole experience,
  deliver it fast. Four of them ran into the exact same structural problem. The fifth ran into
  something completely different."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll see exactly why owning the kitchen sounded like an
  advantage and turned out to be a trap for four of these five companies — and you'll know exactly
  which one didn't fall into that trap, and what got it instead."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Bloomberg, TechCrunch, Inc.com, Berkeleyside, Recode, Crain's
  New York Business, Fast Company, and this channel's own prior deep-dive packages on Sprig (001) and
  Munchery (023) — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "Two of these five companies — Sprig and Munchery — have
  already gotten their own full episodes on this channel. Today they're side by side with the three
  companies that died around them, in the exact same category."

### Company 1 — SpoonRocket (1:40–4:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Rise (1:40–2:30):** founded 2013, Berkeley CA, by Steven Hsiao and Anson Tsui.
  Raised $13.5 million total — a 2013 Y Combinator seed plus angels, and a 2014 Series A from
  Foundation Capital, Base Ventures, and Sherpa Capital. Business: pre-made meals, cars stocked and
  ready, aiming for sub-10-minute delivery. Visual: "$13.5M RAISED" title card.
- **Segment 2 — The Collapse (2:30–3:30):** the company said it had actually reached positive
  contribution margin — selling meals for more than it cost to cook, package, and deliver them — but
  a colder fundraising climate on top of other operating costs meant it couldn't raise what it needed
  to keep going. March 15, 2016, SpoonRocket shuts down. On-screen framing card: "Its own CEO named a
  competitor directly in the shutdown statement — unusually specific for this kind of announcement."
  Visual: "SHUTDOWN — MARCH 15, 2016" title card, with a small "CEO NAMED SPRIG DIRECTLY" callout.
- **Segment 3 — The Twist Ending (3:30–4:00):** after shutting down U.S. operations, SpoonRocket's
  assets were acquired by Brazil-based delivery platform iFood, which used the technology to expand
  into Latin America. Visual: "ACQUIRED BY iFOOD — BRAZIL" title card.

### Company 2 — Sprig (4:00–7:00)
- **Segment 4 — The Rise (4:00–4:45):** founded 2013 by Gagan Biyani, San Francisco. Raised roughly
  $56-58 million total (a $10M Series A in 2014, a $45M round in 2015). Visual: "$56-58M RAISED" title
  card.
- **Segment 5 — The Mechanism (4:45–6:00):** Sprig owned and operated its own kitchens, bearing the
  full cost of food production directly. Hit a founder-confirmed run rate of "roughly $21-22 million"
  (Biyani's own words) while losing approximately $850,000 a month. Uber Eats' major relaunch around
  February 2016 was the precisely dated trigger — Biyani: "instead of going up by 2%, we were going
  down by 2%." On-screen framing card: "Full episode on this channel: package 001." Visual: "$21-22M
  RUN RATE — $850K/MONTH LOST" title card.
- **Segment 6 — The Shutdown (6:00–7:00):** May 26, 2017, Sprig shuts down. Roughly $8 million returned
  to investors out of the ~$56-58 million raised. Biyani's own hindsight, on record: "I would have shut
  it down within three months" — once the negative trend was clear. Visual: "SHUTDOWN — MAY 26, 2017"
  title card.

### Company 3 — Maple (7:00–10:00)
- **Segment 7 — The Rise (7:00–8:00):** founded 2014, New York City, by Caleb Merkl and Akshay Navle,
  with CTO Dan Cowgill — notably backed by celebrity chef David Chang of Momofuku, lending the concept
  major press attention and culinary credibility. March 2015: a $25-26 million Series A led by
  Greenoaks Capital, valuing Maple at roughly $115 million; total lifetime funding around $50 million+.
  Visual: "DAVID CHANG-BACKED — $50M+ RAISED" title card.
- **Segment 8 — The Same Mechanism, Premium Version (8:00–9:00):** Maple cooked its own food in its own
  kitchens and delivered directly — the same owned-kitchen structure as Sprig and SpoonRocket, just
  positioned as a premium, chef-driven product. On-screen framing card: "Not even a celebrity chef's
  backing changed the underlying math." Visual: side-by-side comparison graphic, Maple/Sprig/SpoonRocket
  cost structures shown as identical.
- **Segment 9 — Not a Shutdown, a "Joining" (9:00–10:00):** May 8-9, 2017, after fewer than three years
  in business, Maple's technology and team were acquired by London-based Deliveroo — undisclosed
  terms, all three co-founders joined Deliveroo directly. Maple's own announcement framed this as
  "joining" Deliveroo, never using the words "shut down." Visual: "ACQUIRED BY DELIVEROO — MAY 2017"
  title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:45): "Three companies down, all owned-kitchen, all the exact same underlying
  problem. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week — and stay for the fourth, which raised and outlasted
  all three of these combined."

### Company 4 — Munchery (10:00–14:00)
- **Segment 10 — The Rise (10:00–11:00):** founded 2010 (idea stage), launched May 2011, San
  Francisco, by Tri Tran and Conrad Chu. Raised over $120 million total across five rounds — more
  capital than Sprig, SpoonRocket, and Maple combined. A major 2015 round valued Munchery at roughly
  $300 million. Visual: "$120M+ RAISED — MORE THAN THE OTHER THREE COMBINED" title card.
- **Segment 11 — The Same Trap, More Time (11:00–12:30):** the exact same owned-kitchen cost structure
  as the first three companies — industrial kitchens, in-house chefs, Munchery's own delivery drivers.
  Bloomberg reported Munchery losing up to $5 million a month and wasting roughly 16% of the food it
  prepared. November 2016: Tri Tran steps down to Chief Strategy Officer, James Beriker installed as
  CEO. January 2017: roughly 30% of HQ staff laid off. May 2018: retreats from LA, New York, and
  Seattle to San Francisco only. On-screen framing card: "Full episode on this channel: package 023."
  Visual: "$5M/MONTH LOST" title card.
- **Segment 12 — The Shutdown (12:30–13:30):** January 21, 2019, Munchery shuts down abruptly,
  notifying customers the same day. Vendors reportedly got no notice. The company had been actively
  marketing gift cards the previous month. Visual: "SHUTDOWN — JANUARY 21, 2019" title card.
- **Segment 13 — The Court-Filed Cause (13:30–14:00):** February 28, 2019, Chapter 11 filed. CEO James
  Beriker named the causes ON THE RECORD, in the bankruptcy filing itself: "increased competition,
  over-funding, aggressive expansion efforts, and Blue Apron's failed IPO." On-screen framing card:
  "The most capital, the most time, and still the exact same underlying trap." Visual: "CAUSE OF DEATH:
  NAMED IN THE COURT FILING ITSELF" title card.

### Company 5 — Josephine, the Twist (14:00–17:00)
- **Segment 14 — The Rise (14:00–14:45):** founded 2014 by Charley Wang and Tal Safran — a marketplace
  connecting home cooks directly with neighbors buying home-cooked meals. NOT an owned-kitchen model
  like the first four. Raised just over $2 million from Kapor Capital and angels — by far the smallest
  amount of the five companies in this video. Visual: "$2M+ RAISED — SMALLEST OF THE FIVE" title card.
- **Segment 15 — A Different Kind of Death (14:45–16:00):** May 2016, Josephine is served a
  cease-and-desist order by Alameda County health regulators for illegal food sales, forced to stop
  operations there. Further cease-and-desist letters follow from the City of Berkeley and Alameda
  County, citing "aiding and abetting illegal food sales." On-screen framing card: "This company wasn't
  losing money on every meal like the first four. It was being shut down, city by city, by regulators."
  Visual: "CEASE-AND-DESIST — ALAMEDA COUNTY, 2016" title card.
- **Segment 16 — Fighting the Wrong Battle to Win (16:00–17:00):** Josephine shifts its own focus
  toward pursuing legislative change (cottage-food-law reform) rather than pure growth — concluding
  "legislation needed to be our focus" — but doesn't have the funding to fight regulators market by
  market while also running the business. Winds down in early 2018. On-screen framing card: "Four
  companies died of bad math. This one died of a fight it couldn't afford to keep having." Visual:
  "WOUND DOWN — EARLY 2018" title card.

### The Actual Lesson (17:00–18:30)
- Plain-language comparison: five companies, five different funding scales ($2M to $120M+), four of
  them sharing the exact same owned-kitchen cost structure and the exact same ending — more capital
  (Munchery) bought more time, but never solved the underlying math. The fifth (Josephine) proves the
  category itself has more than one way to die.
- The core lesson, stated directly: when every company in a category makes the same structural bet,
  watching them fail in the same order — smallest-funded first, largest-funded last — isn't a
  coincidence, it's the math playing out at different speeds. And the one company that broke the
  pattern is proof that "the vertical is doomed" and "the model is doomed" are two different claims —
  Josephine's model wasn't what killed it.
- Visual: five-column side-by-side timeline graphic, each company's rise-to-ending sequence shown in
  parallel, four columns visually matching and the fifth column breaking the pattern.

### Final CTA + Outro (18:30–20:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — four companies proved owning the kitchen doesn't scale into
  delivery-app economics, no matter how much capital or time you throw at it, and the fifth company
  proves that even inside one narrow vertical, not every death has the same cause
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want the full individual treatments of
  Sprig and Munchery, check out packages 001 and 023 on this channel"
- Engagement prompt: "Which of these five surprised you most — SpoonRocket naming its own competitor,
  Maple's 'joining' language, Munchery's court-filed cause, or Josephine's regulatory ending? And do you
  know another owned-kitchen startup that fits this same pattern? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 001 (Sprig) + package 023 (Munchery) + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the five-column side-by-side timeline graphic from The Actual Lesson, then works
through each company in funding-size order (Josephine → SpoonRocket → Sprig → Maple → Munchery),
using the graphic as a recurring visual anchor after each segment. Measured, graphics-forward, treats
the video as a genuine category-level case study.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Munchery's court-filed bankruptcy cause as the most concretely sourced single moment, then
works backward chronologically through all five companies by death date (SpoonRocket 2016 → Sprig 2017
→ Maple 2017 → Josephine 2018 → Munchery 2019), closing with the shared-pattern-and-exception reveal.
Strongest tonal build of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "five companies, five funerals, one pattern and one exception" with rapid-fire beats per
company (rise → mechanism named → ending), landing on the shared/broken lesson as the final,
clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential — each company segment can stand alone as its own
Short, and the Josephine twist works especially well as a standalone hook.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the transition into the fourth company (Munchery) if viewers feel they've already
  seen the pattern three times and don't yet know a fifth, different company is coming
- **Fix:** explicitly tease in the cold open and again at the mid-video CTA that one of the five
  companies breaks the pattern — this converts "here we go again" fatigue into anticipation for the
  twist
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general owned-kitchen business-model explanation after the second
  company (Sprig) if audience is clearly already tracking the shared mechanism
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** in the cold open, visually differentiate the fifth company's icon
  from the first four immediately, so viewers are actively watching for which one it is and why
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "1 of 5 / 2 of 5..." counter visible in a corner
  graphic throughout, switching color/style only when Josephine's segment begins
