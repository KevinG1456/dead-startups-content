# Blog Article: One Vertical, Five Funerals — Every Owned-Kitchen Food Delivery Startup, Same Few Years

*All figures for SpoonRocket, Maple, and Josephine sourced to Bloomberg, TechCrunch, Inc.com,
Berkeleyside, Recode, Crain's New York Business, and Fast Company — see `sources-log.txt` for the full
citation map. Sprig and Munchery figures are reused directly from this channel's own packages 001 and
023, not re-derived. No individual across any of the five companies was charged with fraud or any
crime.*

## Introduction
Most of this channel's videos follow one company from rise to collapse. This one follows five — all in
the same narrow category: on-demand meal delivery, where the startup cooks the food itself rather than
running a marketplace of restaurants. Four of them died from the exact same structural problem. The
fifth one didn't.

## SpoonRocket: The First to Fall
SpoonRocket was founded in 2013 in Berkeley by Steven Hsiao and Anson Tsui, raising $13.5 million
across a Y Combinator seed round and a 2014 Series A. The idea was pre-made meals in cars stocked and
ready, aiming for sub-10-minute delivery. The company said it had actually reached positive
contribution margin — selling meals for more than they cost to make — but a colder fundraising
environment on top of other costs meant it couldn't raise what it needed. On March 15, 2016, SpoonRocket
shut down — the first of this video's five companies to go. Its own shutdown statement named competitor
Sprig directly, unusually specific for this kind of announcement. Afterward, its assets were acquired by
Brazil-based delivery platform iFood, which used the technology to expand into Latin America.

## Sprig: The Founder Who Explained It Himself
Sprig was founded in 2013 by Gagan Biyani in San Francisco, raising roughly $56-58 million. Like
SpoonRocket, it owned and operated its own kitchens, bearing the full cost of food production directly.
Biyani later told Mixergy the company had hit "a run rate of $22 million, so or 21, or something like
that" while losing approximately $850,000 a month. Uber Eats' major relaunch around February 2016 was
the precisely dated trigger — in Biyani's words, "instead of going up by 2%, we were going down by 2%."
Sprig shut down on May 26, 2017. Full detail on Sprig's story is available in this channel's own package
001.

## Maple: A Celebrity Chef Couldn't Save It Either
Maple was founded in 2014 in New York City by Caleb Merkl and Akshay Navle, with CTO Dan Cowgill —
notably backed by celebrity chef David Chang of Momofuku, which brought the company outsized press
attention. A March 2015 Series A raised $25-26 million, valuing Maple at roughly $115 million; total
lifetime funding is reported around $50 million or more. Maple ran the same owned-kitchen model as
SpoonRocket and Sprig, just positioned as a premium, chef-driven product. On May 8-9, 2017, after fewer
than three years in business, Maple's technology and team were acquired by London-based Deliveroo —
undisclosed terms, all three co-founders joining Deliveroo directly. Maple's own announcement framed
this as "joining" Deliveroo, never using the words "shut down."

## Munchery: More Capital, More Time, Same Ending
Munchery was founded in 2010 (idea stage), launching publicly in May 2011 in San Francisco under Tri
Tran and Conrad Chu. It raised over $120 million — more than SpoonRocket, Sprig, and Maple combined — a
major 2015 round valuing it at roughly $300 million. The same owned-kitchen cost structure applied:
industrial kitchens, in-house chefs, its own delivery drivers. Bloomberg reported Munchery losing up to
$5 million a month and wasting roughly 16% of the food it prepared. Tri Tran stepped down to Chief
Strategy Officer in November 2016; James Beriker took over as CEO. Roughly 30% of headquarters staff
were laid off in January 2017, and the company retreated from Los Angeles, New York, and Seattle to San
Francisco only by May 2018. On January 21, 2019, Munchery shut down abruptly, having been actively
marketing gift cards the month before. Its February 28, 2019 Chapter 11 filing did something rare: CEO
James Beriker named the causes on the record, in writing — "increased competition, over-funding,
aggressive expansion efforts, and Blue Apron's failed IPO." Full detail on Munchery's story is available
in this channel's own package 023.

## Josephine: The Twist
Josephine was founded in 2014 by Charley Wang and Tal Safran — but it wasn't an owned-kitchen company at
all. It was a marketplace connecting home cooks directly with neighbors buying home-cooked meals,
raising just over $2 million from Kapor Capital and angels — by far the smallest amount of any company
in this video. Its failure had nothing to do with unit economics. In May 2016, Alameda County health
regulators served Josephine a cease-and-desist order for illegal food sales, forcing it to stop
operating there. Further cease-and-desist letters followed from the City of Berkeley and Alameda County,
citing "aiding and abetting illegal food sales." Josephine shifted its own focus toward pursuing
legislative change — cottage-food-law reform — concluding "legislation needed to be our focus," but
didn't have the funding to fight regulators market by market while also running the underlying
business. It wound down in early 2018.

## The Actual Lesson
Four companies, one shared mechanism: an owned-kitchen cost structure that never produced sub-
restaurant-margin meals at delivery-app prices and scale, no matter how much capital or time was thrown
at it. Munchery raised the most and lasted the longest, and it still ended the same way as SpoonRocket,
which raised roughly a tenth as much and died three years earlier.

Josephine breaks that pattern entirely — not because its business model was flawless, but because its
death came from an entirely different direction. It proves something worth remembering about category-
level failure patterns: "this vertical is doomed" and "this specific model is doomed" are two different
claims, and mistaking one for the other means missing the real cause of death when it's actually
something else — in this case, a regulatory environment the company couldn't afford to fight.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **Owning the kitchen sounds like control. It's actually a fixed-cost bet against a variable-margin
  business** — Sprig, Munchery, SpoonRocket, and Maple all bore the full cost of food production
  directly, with no restaurant partner absorbing any of that risk
- **More capital buys time, not a different outcome, if the underlying structure is unchanged** —
  Munchery outraised and outlasted the other three by years, and still hit the same wall
- **A celebrity name attached to a company doesn't change its unit economics** — Maple's backing from
  David Chang generated press attention, not a different cost structure
- **Not every failure in a narrow category has the same cause** — Josephine operated in the exact same
  broad space as the other four and still died for a completely unrelated reason
- **Regulatory risk can end a company faster than bad unit economics** — Josephine didn't get the years
  that Sprig, Munchery, and Maple got; a market-by-market legal fight it couldn't afford to keep having
  ended it in under four years

## The Lesson
When five companies in one narrow category all fail within a few years of each other, it's tempting to
assume they all failed for the same reason. Four of these five did. The fifth is proof that a category
can be genuinely unforgiving in more than one way at once — and that the specific cause of death still
matters, even when the broader pattern looks identical from a distance.
