# Script Package

Anchored on Toysmart.com — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Never state the Disney investment as
a single precise "$45 million" without noting one source splits it as $20M investment + $25M marketing.
Never state the customer data was ultimately sold — it was NOT; Disney's Buena Vista subsidiary paid
$50,000 specifically to have it destroyed instead. Never call Toysmart "VC-funded" — it was a spin-off
of an existing retailer (Holt Company) later majority-invested in by Disney. No fraud/criminal framing
anywhere — this is a civil FTC Section 5 regulatory-precedent story.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11–13 minutes (the founding, the Disney investment, the collapse, the attempted
data sale, the FTC fight, and the resolution all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Disney put $45 million into this company. Eighteen months later, its last asset for sale in
  bankruptcy was your kid's data."
- Visual: a privacy-policy document with the line "we will never share your information" highlighted,
  then stamped "FOR SALE"
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a story about running out
  of money. It's a story about what a promise is actually worth once you do."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Toysmart.com — an online toy retailer that Disney bought a majority stake in back in
  1999. Less than a year later, it went bankrupt. And what happened next became the first case of its
  kind in FTC history."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly what was at stake, why 41 states got
  involved, and how this 25-year-old case still decides what happens to your data today."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Forbes, Computerworld, FTC.gov, the New York Attorney General's
  office, and Duke Law's Duke Law & Technology Review — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "One honesty note up front: this channel already covered
  a similar story — RadioShack's 2015 bankruptcy and its own customer-data fight. This is a different,
  earlier company. In fact, this is the ORIGINAL case. Everything RadioShack's case had to work within
  fifteen years later, this case decided first."

### Main Content — The Promise (1:40–4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** Toysmart.com began as Holt Educational Outlet in October
  1997, a spin-off of an existing educational-toy retailer, formally becoming Toysmart.com, Inc. in
  December 1998 — "good toys that nurture children's imaginations": building blocks, puzzles, arts and
  crafts. Headquartered in Waltham, Massachusetts. Visual: generic late-1990s e-commerce storefront
  graphic.
- **Segment 2 — Disney Buys In (2:15–3:15):** August 1999, The Walt Disney Company's Buena Vista
  Internet Group takes a roughly 60% majority stake in Toysmart.com for approximately $45 million.
  On-screen framing card: "One source breaks this down as $20M investment plus $25M in marketing
  support — presented here as approximately $45 million combined." Visual: "~$45 MILLION — DISNEY'S
  INVESTMENT, AUGUST 1999" title card.
- **Segment 3 — The Promise (3:15–4:30):** Toysmart's published privacy policy makes an explicit
  promise to every customer: their information — names, ages, family details, much of it about
  children — would "never be shared with third parties." Visual: privacy-policy document graphic,
  the "never" highlighted.

### Main Content — The Collapse (4:30–6:30)
- **Segment 4 — Burning Through the Money (4:30–5:15):** Toysmart burns through most of Disney's
  investment in under 18 months, begins seeking additional funding in early 2000, and a month of
  reorganization negotiations fails. Visual: declining cash-runway graphic.
- **Segment 5 — Shutdown, Then Bankruptcy (5:15–6:30):** Toysmart.com ceases operations at midnight,
  May 19, 2000. On June 26, 2000, creditors force the company into Chapter 11 bankruptcy. Among the
  assets listed for sale: the customer database. On-screen framing card: "The same data the privacy
  policy promised would never be shared with third parties was now listed as a company asset for
  sale." Visual: "CHAPTER 11 — JUNE 26, 2000" title card, transitioning into a bankruptcy-filing
  asset-list graphic with "CUSTOMER DATABASE" highlighted.

### Main Content — The Fight (6:30–10:00)
- **Segment 6 — The FTC Steps In (6:30–7:30):** July 2000, the FTC sues, alleging the proposed sale
  would violate Section 5 of the FTC Act — the first-ever FTC enforcement action of this specific
  kind. On-screen framing card: "This was the first time in FTC history a bankrupt company's attempt
  to sell customer data became a federal case." Visual: FTC seal with "FIRST-EVER CASE" stamp.
- **Segment 7 — The Settlement That Wasn't Quite a Block (7:30–8:30):** the FTC's own initially
  proposed settlement doesn't ban the sale outright — it would allow the data to be sold, but only to
  a "qualified buyer" in a related business, bound by the same original privacy terms. Visual:
  "QUALIFIED BUYER" title card with a document/handshake icon.
- **Segment 8 — 41 States Say No (8:30–9:30):** attorneys general from 41 states file formal
  objections to both the proposed sale and the FTC's own settlement, arguing it doesn't go far enough
  to protect the people — many of them children — whose data is at stake. The bankruptcy judge
  signals she's unlikely to approve it. Visual: "41 STATES OBJECT" title card, a map graphic filling
  in state by state.
- **Segment 9 — The $50,000 Payment (9:30–10:00):** January 2001, Buena Vista Internet Group —
  Disney's subsidiary and Toysmart's own majority owner — agrees to pay $50,000 to Toysmart's
  creditors, specifically for the right to have the customer database destroyed instead of sold to
  anyone. The judge approves, on the condition the list be destroyed only once all creditor claims are
  satisfied. On-screen framing card: "No third party ever received this data." Visual: "$50,000 — TO
  DESTROY WHAT $45 MILLION BUILT" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:45): "This channel usually covers companies whose money disappeared. This is the
  rare one where the fight was over what was left behind. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson (10:00–12:00)
- Plain-language comparison: Toysmart's privacy promise wasn't broken by bad intent — it was tested by
  bankruptcy, the one scenario a privacy policy almost never accounts for. The system held, but only
  because 41 states actively fought to make it hold — it wasn't automatic.
- The core lesson, stated directly: a privacy promise is a commitment made by a company, not a law of
  physics — once that company runs out of money, the promise only survives if someone with power
  fights to enforce it. This case is the reason that fight now has a legal template to follow.
- Explicit forward connection: "Fifteen years later, RadioShack went bankrupt and faced almost the
  exact same question — what happens to customer data in a bankruptcy sale. If you want that story,
  it's already on this channel." Visual: split-screen "TOYSMART 2000 → RADIOSHACK 2015" timeline
  graphic.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (1997) → Disney investment (Aug 1999) → shutdown (May
  2000) → bankruptcy filing (Jun 2000) → FTC lawsuit (Jul 2000) → 41-state objection → resolution
  (Jan 2001).

### Final CTA + Outro (12:00–13:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a privacy promise is only as strong as someone's willingness to
  enforce it once the company behind it is gone, and this is the case that first proved that fight is
  winnable
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this is the one about what happens after the
  money's gone, not just how it disappeared"
- Engagement prompt: "Have you ever actually read a privacy policy before agreeing to it? And did you
  know this case is basically the reason your data usually gets protected instead of sold when a
  company you use goes under? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the $45M-to-$50,000 numeral contrast and the full-arc timeline, then works through
the founding, the promise, and the fight as supporting detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the
video as a genuine case study in privacy-policy enforceability and bankruptcy-asset law.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the bankruptcy filing itself — the moment the customer database appears on an asset list —
before cutting hard to the January 2001 resolution, then rewinding to fill in everything in between.
Slower pacing, holds the "41 states" reveal until Segment 8, resolving with the $50,000 payment as the
closing irony.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one promise, one bankruptcy, one fight" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding, Disney
investment, the promise, the collapse, the bankruptcy filing, the FTC lawsuit, the qualified-buyer
settlement, the 41-state objection, the $50,000 resolution — landing on the precedent-setting lesson
as the final, clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $45M-to-$50,000
contrast and the "first-ever case" framing.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the "qualified buyer" settlement segment (7:30–8:30) if it reads as a dry legal
  technicality rather than genuine dramatic tension (the FTC almost allowed a version of the sale)
- **Fix:** frame the qualified-buyer proposal explicitly as "the FTC's first move wasn't actually a
  full win for privacy — it took 41 more states pushing back to get the real outcome," turning it into
  a stakes-raising beat rather than a footnote
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general late-1990s e-commerce context if audience familiarity
  with the dot-com era is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the $50,000 figure even more explicitly in the cold open
  if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 9 (9:30)
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "WAS THE DATA SOLD?" status indicator visible
  from Segment 6 onward, flipping from an ambiguous "?" to a clear "NO" only at Segment 9
