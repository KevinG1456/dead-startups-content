# 048 — Disney Put in $45 Million. Then a Bankrupt Toy Company Tried to Sell Your Kid's Data.

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built around Toysmart.com's fully documented public
history (Forbes, Computerworld, FTC.gov, New York Attorney General's office, Duke Law's DLTR — full
citations in `sources-log.txt`). Fills dead-saas-30 batch title #25 ("Your data after the funeral:
tracing where a dead app's user data actually went") — one of the 6 remaining untouched titles from
the batch's second half (#21-30) after #12/#16/#18/#19 were permanently retired and #21/#24/#27/#28
were filled by packages 044-047. This is the fourth of six titles researched in this pass (#22 and
#23 were found unsourceable and skipped; #26, #29, #30 became packages 049-051).

**Why this doesn't duplicate package 020:** this channel already has package 020 ("Where Dead
Startups' Code, Data, and Customers Actually Go"), covering Sidecar, Cambridge Analytica, and
RadioShack's data-after-death stories. Toysmart is a DIFFERENT, EARLIER company — and it is the
ORIGINAL case. Toysmart's July 2000 FTC action is the first-ever enforcement of its kind and the
direct legal ancestor of RadioShack's 2015 case already covered in 020: same core legal mechanism
(can a bankrupt company sell customer data despite a "never share" privacy promise?), fifteen years
apart, Toysmart came first and set the precedent everything after it — including RadioShack — had to
work within. This lineage is the package's core differentiator and should be stated explicitly on
screen, not glossed over.

**The real case:** Toysmart.com began life as Holt Educational Outlet (Oct 1997), a spin-off of an
existing educational-toy retailer, formally becoming Toysmart.com, Inc. in Dec 1998. In August 1999,
The Walt Disney Company's Buena Vista Internet Group took a ~60% majority stake for approximately $45
million (combining direct investment and marketing commitments). Toysmart burned through most of that
funding in under 18 months, and ceased operations at midnight, May 19, 2000. Creditors forced it into
Chapter 11 bankruptcy on June 26, 2000. Among the assets listed for sale: its customer database —
including children's names, ages, and family profiles — despite Toysmart's own privacy policy
explicitly promising that data would "never be shared with third parties." In July 2000, the FTC sued,
alleging this would violate Section 5 of the FTC Act. The FTC's own proposed settlement wasn't a
flat block — it would have allowed a sale to a "qualified buyer" bound by the same privacy terms.
Attorneys general from 41 states formally objected to that plan. The bankruptcy judge signaled she
likely wouldn't approve it. In January 2001, Disney's Buena Vista subsidiary — Toysmart's own majority
owner — agreed to pay $50,000 to Toysmart's creditors specifically for the "privilege" of having the
customer list destroyed instead of sold to anyone. The judge approved it, on the condition the data
be destroyed only once all creditor claims were satisfied. No third party ever received the data.

**Framing requirement:** Never assert a single precise "$45 million" figure without the sourced
caveat that one account splits it as $20M investment + $25M marketing. Never state the data was
ultimately sold — the ending is that it explicitly was NOT, at a cost of $50,000 to the company's own
majority owner. Never call Toysmart "VC-funded" — it was a retailer spin-off later majority-invested
in by Disney, a different funding shape than this channel's typical subject. No fraud/criminal framing
— this is a regulatory-precedent story about a civil FTC Section 5 action, not personal wrongdoing.
