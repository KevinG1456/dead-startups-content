# Blog Article

## Title
Disney Put in $45 Million. Then This Company Tried to Sell Your Kid's Data — The Full Toysmart.com
Story

## Article

In August 1999, The Walt Disney Company's Buena Vista Internet Group took a roughly 60% majority
stake in an online toy retailer called Toysmart.com, investing approximately $45 million. Less than
18 months later, that company was bankrupt — and the fight over what happened next became the
first-ever FTC case testing whether a company can sell customer data despite promising never to.

### The Company

Toysmart.com began as Holt Educational Outlet, launched in October 1997 as a spin-off of an existing
educational-toy retailer. It formally became Toysmart.com, Inc. in December 1998, selling "good toys
that nurture children's imaginations" — building blocks, puzzles, arts and crafts — from a
50,000-square-foot headquarters in Waltham, Massachusetts, under CEO David Lord.

Disney's August 1999 investment gave the company real backing and real credibility. And, like most of
Toysmart's customers would have read in the company's own privacy policy, real assurance: their
information — names, ages, family and purchasing details, much of it about children — would "never be
shared with third parties."

### The Collapse

Toysmart burned through the bulk of Disney's investment in under 18 months. Early 2000 negotiations to
raise more money or reorganize the business failed. The company ceased operations at midnight on May
19, 2000. On June 26, 2000, creditors forced Toysmart.com into Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Massachusetts.

Among the assets listed for sale in that bankruptcy filing: the company's customer database — the
exact information its own privacy policy had promised would never leave the company.

### The Fight

In July 2000, the FTC sued, alleging the proposed sale would violate Section 5 of the FTC Act's
prohibition on unfair or deceptive trade practices. It was the first FTC enforcement action of its
specific kind.

What's less commonly remembered: the FTC's own initial proposed settlement didn't ban the sale
outright. It would have permitted the data to be sold — just to a "qualified buyer" in a related
business, bound by the same privacy terms Toysmart itself had promised.

Attorneys general from 41 states formally objected, arguing this didn't go far enough to protect the
people — many of them children — whose data was on the line. The presiding bankruptcy judge, Carol
Kenner, signaled she was unlikely to approve the settlement given the scale of the pushback.

### The Resolution

In January 2001, Buena Vista Internet Group — Disney's subsidiary and Toysmart's own majority
shareholder — agreed to pay $50,000 to Toysmart's creditors. Not to acquire the customer data. To
destroy it.

Judge Kenner approved the plan, with one condition: Toysmart's attorneys would hold the list and
destroy it only once all creditor claims against the estate were fully satisfied.

No third party ever received Toysmart's customer data.

### Why This Case Still Matters

Toysmart's story predates — and directly shaped — nearly every later fight over what happens to
customer data when a company dies. This channel has already covered RadioShack's 2015 bankruptcy,
which faced almost the identical question fifteen years later, working within the legal framework
Toysmart's case helped establish.

A privacy policy is a promise made by a company. Toysmart's story is the reminder — and the
precedent — that the promise only survives a company's death if someone is willing to fight to
enforce it. In 2000, 41 states did.
