customer data despite promising never to?), fifteen years apart, Toysmart first.

FOUNDING AND DISNEY INVESTMENT — precisely sourced
- Toysmart.com began as "Holt Educational Outlet," launched October 1997, a spin-off of the Holt
  Company, an existing retailer of educational supplies. Toysmart.com, Inc. was formally spun off in
  December 1998. The company sold "good toys that nurture children's imaginations" — building blocks,
  puzzles, arts and crafts — and claimed to be the first online toy retailer. Headquartered in a
  50,000-square-foot office in Waltham, Massachusetts.
- CEO: David Lord.
- August 1999: The Walt Disney Company, through its Buena Vista Internet Group subsidiary, took a
  majority (~60%) stake in Toysmart.com with a $45 million investment. *** [VERIFY]: one contemporary
  account breaks this down differently — a reported $20 million direct investment plus $25 million in
  marketing support, rather than a flat $45 million cash figure. Present as "approximately $45 million,
  combining investment and marketing commitments" rather than asserting one precise clean number. ***
Source: Forbes (May 23, 2000, "Toysmart Shuts Down"), E-Commerce Times ("Disney's Buena Vista Acquires
Stake in Toysmart.com").

THE PRIVACY PROMISE — precisely sourced
- Toysmart.com's published privacy policy explicitly promised that customer information — including
  names, addresses, ages, and family/purchasing profiles, much of it involving children — would "never
  be shared with third parties."
Source: FTC press release (Jul 2000); FTC v. Toysmart.com case page (ftc.gov); IT Law Wiki summary.

THE COLLAPSE — precisely dated
- Toysmart burned through the bulk of Disney's funding within about 18 months and began seeking
  additional funding in early 2000. Month-long reorganization negotiations failed.
- The company ceased operations at midnight, May 19, 2000 — under 18 months after the Disney
  investment closed.
- June 26, 2000: creditors forced Toysmart.com into Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Massachusetts.
- Among the assets listed for sale in the bankruptcy filing: the company's customer list and database
  of customer information — directly contradicting the "never share with third parties" promise made
  to every one of those customers.
Source: Forbes (May 23, 2000); Computerworld ("Toysmart goes out of business"); Computerworld
("Bankruptcy judge puts off decision on Toysmart data sale").

THE FTC ACTION AND STATE PUSHBACK — precisely sourced, this is the package's core sequence
- July 2000: the FTC sued, alleging the proposed data sale would violate Section 5 of the FTC Act
  (unfair or deceptive trade practices) by breaking the company's own stated privacy promise. This is
  the FIRST-EVER FTC enforcement action of this specific kind — a bankrupt company's attempt to sell
  customer data as a distressed asset despite a no-sharing privacy promise.
- The FTC's initially PROPOSED settlement did not block the sale outright — it would have allowed the
  data to be sold, but only to a "qualified buyer": a company in a business related to Toysmart's own
  market, bound by the same original privacy-policy terms.
- Attorneys general from 41 states filed formal objections to BOTH the proposed data sale itself and
  the FTC's proposed "qualified buyer" settlement, arguing it did not adequately protect consumers
  (especially children) whose data was at stake.
- U.S. Bankruptcy Court Judge Carol Kenner signaled she was unlikely to approve the qualified-buyer
  sale, in significant part because of the volume and weight of the state AG objections.
Source: FTC press release (Jul 2000); ABC News ("FTC, Toysmart.com Settle"); Computerworld (bankruptcy
judge coverage); Duke Law's DLTR ("FTC vs. Toysmart," Mar 2001 retrospective).

THE RESOLUTION — precisely dated
- January 2001: Buena Vista Internet Group (the Disney subsidiary, and Toysmart's 60% majority
  shareholder) agreed to pay $50,000 to Toysmart's creditors — not to buy and use the customer data,
  but specifically for the "privilege" of having it destroyed instead of sold to anyone.
- Judge Kenner approved this plan, with one added stipulation: Toysmart's attorneys were to retain
  physical/digital custody of the list and destroy it only once all creditor claims against the
  bankruptcy estate were fully satisfied — not immediately.
- Net effect: no third party ever received Toysmart's customer database. The company that once
  promised never to share the data ended its life with its own majority owner paying a small sum
  specifically to make sure that promise held, after the market (a sale) had already tried and failed
  to break it.
Source: New York Attorney General press release ("Toysmart Bankruptcy Settlement Ensures Consumer
Privacy Protection"); Computerworld; ABI Journal ("Texas Attorney General: Privacy Is Not for Sale").

CATEGORY / CROSS-REFERENCE NOTES
- This package explicitly distinguishes itself from package 020 (Sidecar/Cambridge Analytica/RadioShack)
  by being a DIFFERENT and EARLIER company (2000 vs. 020's cases, which range from the mid-2010s
  onward). State this lineage explicitly on screen: Toysmart's 2000 case is the direct ancestor of
  RadioShack's 2015 case in package 020 — same core legal mechanism (bankruptcy-sale-vs-privacy-promise
  under FTC Section 5 authority), fifteen years apart, Toysmart came first and set the precedent that
  every later case (including RadioShack's) had to work within.
- No fraud or criminal charges apply to anyone at Toysmart.com or Disney/Buena Vista in this story —
  this is a regulatory/policy precedent story, not a fraud story. Toysmart's leadership is not accused
  of deliberate deception in available sourcing beyond the underlying FTC Section 5 "unfair/deceptive
  practice" civil framing, which concerns the company's ACT (attempting the sale), not an individual's
  criminal intent. Present neutrally.
- Toysmart itself does not appear to have raised independent venture capital before the Disney deal —
  it was a corporate spin-off of an existing retailer (Holt Company), not a VC-backed startup in the
  conventional sense. State this accurately; do not describe it as "VC-funded."
