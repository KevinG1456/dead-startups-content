# 035 — A Bank Called In One Loan. 35,000 Small Businesses Lost Access to Their Own Books Overnight.

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built around Bench Accounting's fully documented public
history (BetaKit, Wikipedia, GeekWire, CBC News, Banking Dive — full citations in `sources-log.txt`).
Fifth title on the channel sourced from the "dead-saas-30-Content Titles new-video-ideas.md" batch
(Cluster 1, idea #7: "The startup that got margin-called: venture debt's ugliest quarter"). Titles #5
and #6 from the same batch were skipped after exhaustive searches found no real, named companies
matching those patterns (see `project_channel_overview.md` memory) — #7 is the first of the remaining
titles with strong, real sourcing.

**The real case:** Bench Accounting, a bookkeeping-as-a-service company founded 2012 in Vancouver by
Ian Crosby and Adam Saint, raised over $100 million, including a 2021 Series C ($60M — $37M equity
plus $23M debt) that valued the company at $232 million. Days after that round closed, the board
removed Crosby as CEO. Over the following three years, Bench reportedly closed two additional bridge
financings while struggling with scalability and burning cash. On December 27, 2024, Bench's platform
went dark with zero warning, locking out an estimated 35,000+ small-business customers from their own
financial records, and the company laid off 450 employees the same day. Per BetaKit's reporting, the
shutdown was forced by a bank calling in Bench's venture debt loan — the specific lender and the exact
covenant trigger were never publicly confirmed; Bench declined to disclose them. On January 7, 2025,
Bench filed for bankruptcy in Canada, listing $2.8 million in cash against $65.4 million in
liabilities. Three days after the shutdown, HR-tech company Employer.com announced it was acquiring
Bench, closing the deal January 1, 2025, and rehiring more than half the laid-off staff.

**Framing requirement:** This channel already has a venture-debt-lender package (017, Convoy) — keep
the two mechanisms explicitly distinct: Convoy's named lender (Hercules Capital) took control and
wound the company down gradually; Bench's unnamed lender calling in the loan caused an immediate,
overnight shutdown with zero customer warning. Never name a specific lender or covenant term for
Bench — those details are genuinely unconfirmed in public reporting, not just omitted for pacing.

## 2. Viewer Psychology
- **Curiosity triggers:** how a real, operating company with tens of thousands of customers can vanish
  overnight with zero warning; what actually forced the shutdown; how the company came back within days
- **Emotional triggers:** shock at the abruptness, sympathy for affected small businesses, disbelief at
  the whiplash sequence (shutdown → bankruptcy filing → acquisition, all within about a week)
- **Viewer objections:** "startups shut down all the time" — pre-empt with the specific mechanism (a
  loan being called) and the concrete human impact (35,000+ businesses locked out of their own records)
- **Motivation to watch:** small-business owners who use or used similar bookkeeping services; founders/
  operators evaluating venture debt; general audience drawn by the dramatic real-time sequence
- **Expected comments:** affected former Bench customers sharing their own experience, questions about
  whether other bookkeeping services carry similar risk, debate over whether the sudden shutdown was
  handled responsibly

## 3. Competitor Analysis
- **Similar creators:** covered extensively as a business/small-business-news story (GeekWire, CBC,
  Banking Dive) and an accounting-industry story — almost no coverage names the specific venture-debt
  mechanism as clearly as the core cause, or connects it to the broader "venture debt risk" pattern
- **Content gap:** existing coverage treats this as "a startup collapsed and got bought," not as a
  direct, teachable example of what a lender calling in a loan actually does to a real company in real
  time
- **How to outperform:** a clean, dated hour-by-hour/day-by-day timeline graphic (Dec 27 shutdown → Dec
  30 acquisition announced → Jan 1 deal closes → Jan 7 bankruptcy filing) showing just how fast this
  entire sequence happened
