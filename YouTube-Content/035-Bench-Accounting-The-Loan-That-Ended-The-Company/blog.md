# Blog Article: A Bank Called In One Loan. 35,000 Small Businesses Lost Access to Their Own Books Overnight.

*All figures sourced to BetaKit, Wikipedia, GeekWire, CBC News, and Banking Dive — see
`sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This article is deliberately precise about what is and
isn't confirmed: the specific lender behind Bench's collapse, and the exact covenant they invoked,
were never publicly disclosed. This article states the sourced mechanism — a bank called in Bench's
venture debt loan — without naming a lender or covenant term that no source actually confirms.*

## Introduction
Most startup collapses on this channel unfold over months — a bad quarter, a failed raise, a slow
decline. Bench Accounting's ending took about a day. This is the story of what happens when a venture
debt lender decides to call in a loan: not a metaphor, not a slow-motion warning sign, but a real
company with real customers going dark with no notice at all.

## The Founding and the Climb
Bench was founded in 2012 as "10sheet Inc." by Ian Crosby and Adam Saint, through the TechStars NYC
accelerator. The company rebranded to "Bench" in 2013 after relocating to Vancouver, British Columbia,
building a bookkeeping-as-a-service product: real human bookkeepers, supported by proprietary
software, for small and medium-sized businesses too small to justify a full-time accountant.

Over the following years, Bench raised a $2 million seed round (2013), $1 million more (2014), a $7
million Series A (2015), a $16 million Series B (2016), and an $18 million Series B-1 (2018). In 2021,
a $60 million Series C — $37 million in equity, $23 million in debt — valued the company at $232
million.

## The Ouster
Days after that Series C closed, Bench's board removed co-founder Ian Crosby from the CEO role. Per
Crosby's own account, the board told him a "professional CEO" was needed to take the company to its
next stage, and he says he had turned down a lucrative acquisition offer shortly before his removal —
his own claim, stated here as such, since this research found no independent corroboration of that
specific detail.

Over the following three years, Bench reportedly closed two additional bridge financing rounds while
struggling with scalability and burning cash toward insolvency, per BetaKit's sourcing.

## December 27, 2024
Then, without warning, Bench's platform simply stopped working. An estimated 35,000-plus small-
business customers — real people running real companies — lost access to their own bookkeeping and tax
records with no advance notice. The same day, Bench laid off 450 employees.

## The Mechanism
Here is what actually caused it, stated as precisely as the sourcing allows: per BetaKit's reporting,
the shutdown was forced by a bank calling in Bench's venture debt loan — the lender exercising its
contractual right to demand immediate repayment. This is what a venture debt "margin call" looks like
in practice: not a warning, not a renegotiation period, but an immediate demand that a company without
the cash to meet it simply cannot survive operating through.

**What this article will not do is name a specific lender or covenant.** No source found in this
research confirms either detail. Bench itself declined to confirm them when asked by reporters. The
honest version of this story is: a bank called in a loan, and the company went dark the same day. That
is fully sourced. Anything more specific than that is not.

## The Bankruptcy Filing
On January 7, 2025, Bench filed for bankruptcy protection in Canada, disclosing $2.8 million in cash
against $65.4 million in total liabilities — the stark numerical snapshot of a company that had, just
three years earlier, been valued at $232 million.

## The Rescue
Three days after the shutdown, on December 30, 2024, Employer.com — an HR-technology company with no
prior specialization in accounting — announced it would acquire Bench. Employer.com's own CEO, Jesse
Tinsley, said he learned of the shutdown from news reports, not from Bench directly. The deal closed
January 1, 2025; Bench resumed operating under its own brand as an Employer.com subsidiary, rehiring
more than half of the 450 employees laid off days earlier.

Put together: shutdown (December 27) → acquisition announced (December 30) → deal closed (January 1)
→ bankruptcy filing disclosed (January 7). About eleven days, start to finish.

## The Actual Lesson
This channel has told a venture-debt story before — package 017, Convoy, where a lender took control
of the company and wound it down gradually, marketing its technology to buyers over an extended
period. Bench's version of the same underlying risk moved at a completely different speed: one lender
decision, and the company's operations ended the same day, with zero built-in warning for the tens of
thousands of small businesses depending on it.

Venture debt is often framed as the "safe," non-dilutive alternative to raising more equity. It is
non-dilutive — right up until the lender decides it's done waiting, at which point it can be faster
and more absolute than almost any other way a company can fail.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **Venture debt carries a structural risk equity doesn't: a single lender decision can end operations
  immediately** — there's no board vote, no shareholder process, just a contractual right being
  exercised
- **A company's customers have zero visibility into this risk until it happens to them** — Bench's
  35,000+ small-business customers had no way to know their bookkeeping provider carried this
  exposure
- **Leadership instability early on can compound risk later** — Crosby's ouster days after the Series
  C closed is a visible early signal, even though this research can't draw a direct causal line from
  that event to the 2024 collapse
- **A distressed company's fastest path back to operating isn't always the cleanest one** — Employer.com
  learned about the acquisition opportunity from the same news coverage everyone else did, and moved
  fast anyway
- **Public reporting on a story like this can be thorough and still leave real gaps** — the lender's
  identity here may never be publicly known, and that's a reasonable thing for a video (or an article)
  to say plainly rather than paper over

## The Lesson
Bench Accounting's ending is a real, dated, human-scale illustration of a risk that's usually discussed
only in the abstract: venture debt can end a company's operations in a single day, without warning,
for reasons that may never be made fully public. The company came back within days under new ownership
— but for the businesses locked out of their own financial records on December 27, 2024, that
overnight silence already happened.
