# Blog Article: $185 Million. 400 Products. Can You Name One?

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, Forbes, GE.com press releases, Fortune, and Entrepreneur.com —
see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This isn't a fraud story or a villain story — it's
a focus story.*

## Introduction
Most startups on this channel fail because they picked the wrong idea, ran out of money, or got
undercut by a competitor. Quirky is different: it never had a shortage of ideas. It had over a
million people submitting them every week, real hit products in real stores, and a partnership with
General Electric. It still went bankrupt in six years. The reason is almost the opposite of most
failure stories here — it never learned to say no.

## The Founding
Quirky was founded in June 2009 by Ben Kaufman, then 22 years old, who had already sold his first
company, an iPod-accessory maker called mophie, in 2007. Quirky's model was genuinely novel: the
community submitted product ideas, voted and commented on them, Quirky's in-house team designed and
manufactured the winners, and everyone who contributed — inventors and community "influencers"
alike — shared in the sales revenue. It worked. Quirky built a community of over a million engaged
users and shipped real, well-reviewed products into major retailers.

## The Funding
Quirky raised roughly $185 million across four venture rounds: a $6 million Series A led by RRE
Ventures in 2010, a Series B led by Norwest Venture Partners, a $68 million Series C led by
Andreessen Horowitz in 2012, and a $79 million Series D in November 2013. That Series C round is
the pivot point of this story — it's what funded Quirky's decision to accelerate.

## Then They Sped Up
Kaufman's original stated goal at launch was one new consumer product every week. After the 2012
Series C, Quirky tripled that pace: three new product launches a week, selected from more than
1,000 community submissions every single week. This is the moment the company's core mechanism
stopped being able to slow down, even as the underlying strategy problem it created kept compounding.

## General Electric Gets Involved
Starting in 2011, Quirky partnered with General Electric to co-develop connected smart-home
products built on GE's own patents. By November 2013, GE had expanded that relationship into a $30
million equity investment, folded into Quirky's $79 million Series D. GE named Wink — Quirky's
in-progress smart-home software — its preferred connected-home platform, and Home Depot signed on
to carry Wink-connected devices.

## Wink Is Born
Wink was spun off from Quirky as its own standalone company in mid-2014, built specifically to
control the growing category of connected-home devices from a single app. On paper, this looked
like exactly the kind of focused, defensible product line Quirky needed.

## 400 Products, No Focus
It wasn't enough to offset the rest of the business. Over roughly six years of operation, Quirky
shipped around 400 distinct products across nearly every consumer category imaginable — kitchen
gadgets, office supplies, smart-home hardware — with thin retail margins and, per multiple
retrospectives, little iteration once a product actually launched. There was no version of this
company that wasn't trying to build everything at once.

## The Slide
Layoffs began in late 2014 as external funding got harder to raise. On July 31, 2015, Ben Kaufman
stepped down as CEO alongside a 111-person layoff.

## Bankruptcy
On September 22, 2015, Quirky filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, explicitly to facilitate an asset
sale. Wink went up for auction as part of that process; Flextronics — an existing hardware/firmware
supplier to Wink — won it with a $15 million bid, approved by the bankruptcy court that December.

## What GE's $30 Million Actually Bought
It's worth sitting with this for a second: a Fortune 500 company's capital, its own patents, and a
national retail partner in Home Depot still weren't enough to save Quirky from a strategy problem.
Money and credibility couldn't substitute for focus.

## Wink Didn't Die, Exactly
Here's the part most retrospectives skip. In July 2017, Flex (renamed from Flextronics) resold Wink
to i.am+, the technology company founded by musician will.i.am, reportedly for $59 million — a real
jump from the $15 million Flex had paid less than two years earlier.

## But It's Not a Happy Ending
This channel has covered a company whose product outlived it and genuinely thrived — Parse, Inc.,
whose open-sourced Parse Server has more active users today than the company ever had while it
existed (see our earlier video on that story). Wink's afterlife is a messier, more honest version
of the same question. In May 2020, Wink imposed a mandatory $4.99/month subscription on existing
hardware owners — people who'd already bought the devices — restricting basic functionality behind
a paywall and sparking real backlash. The platform has a documented history of outages continuing
into 2024 and 2025. It's technically still running today. It's not a clean win.

## The Actual Lesson
The lesson here isn't "never partner with a big company" or "never scale your product pace" as
universal rules — both of those things can work fine in the right context. The real lesson is
narrower: a business that can build almost anything still has to decide, deliberately and often,
what NOT to build. Quirky's engine for turning ideas into products never broke. What broke was the
absence of any mechanism to say no to most of them.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **A working idea-generation engine is not the same as a working business strategy** — Quirky's
  crowdsourcing model genuinely worked at generating and validating ideas; it never developed an
  equally strong mechanism for saying no to most of them
- **Big-name partnerships don't substitute for focus** — GE's money, patents, and retail
  relationships were real and substantial, and still weren't enough to offset a scale-without-
  focus strategy problem
- **Accelerating output after a funding round isn't automatically progress** — tripling the launch
  pace after the 2012 Series C compounded the underlying focus problem rather than solving it
- **"The product outlived the company" isn't always a clean, happy story** — Wink's three-owner,
  backlash-and-outage-riddled afterlife is a more realistic outcome than a triumphant one
- **A company that tries to do everything usually ends up known for nothing** — despite shipping
  around 400 products, most people can't name a single one

## The Lesson
Most of the videos in this series are about a single wrong decision or a single external shock.
This one is about the absence of a decision — the discipline to stop saying yes — and what happens
to a genuinely good idea-generation machine when nothing ever filters its output.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What was Quirky?**
A crowdsourced invention platform founded in 2009, where the community submitted and voted on
product ideas, and the company designed, manufactured, and sold the winners.

**2. Who founded Quirky?**
Ben Kaufman, who publicly launched the company in June 2009 at age 22, after previously founding
and selling mophie in 2007.

**3. How much did Quirky raise in total?**
Roughly $185 million across four venture rounds.

**4. Who invested in Quirky?**
RRE Ventures (Series A), Norwest Venture Partners (Series B), Andreessen Horowitz (Series C), and
General Electric among others (Series D).

**5. What was Quirky's relationship with General Electric?**
GE partnered with Quirky starting in 2011 on connected-home products, then invested $30 million
directly into the company in November 2013 as part of a $79 million Series D round.

**6. What was Wink?**
A smart-home hub and app platform that grew out of the Quirky-GE partnership, spun off as a
standalone company in mid-2014.

**7. How many products did Quirky make?**
Approximately 400 distinct products over roughly six years of operation.

**8. Why did Quirky fail?**
It scaled its product output (from one to three new launches a week after its 2012 Series C)
without ever developing enough category focus — a genuinely working invention engine spread too
thin across too many unrelated products.

**9. When did Quirky's CEO step down?**
Ben Kaufman stepped down as CEO on July 31, 2015, alongside a 111-person layoff.

**10. When did Quirky file for bankruptcy?**
September 22, 2015 — Chapter 11, explicitly to facilitate an asset sale.

**11. What happened to Wink when Quirky went bankrupt?**
It was auctioned off as part of the bankruptcy process; Flextronics won it with a $15 million bid,
approved by the court in December 2015.

**12. Did Wink survive after that?**
Yes — Flex resold Wink to i.am+ (will.i.am's company) for a reported $59 million in 2017.

**13. Is Wink still around today?**
As of this research (2026), yes, technically — though with a documented history of outages and a
real user backlash over a 2020 mandatory subscription requirement.

**14. What happened with Wink's 2020 subscription controversy?**
Wink began requiring a $4.99/month subscription to access previously-free basic features on
hardware customers already owned, which sparked significant backlash.

**15. Is this the same kind of story as this channel's Parse video?**
It's a related question — "did the product outlive the company" — but a different, more ambiguous
answer. Parse Server (package 049) is a clean, thriving, independently-maintained afterlife. Wink's
afterlife is real but troubled — three owners, user backlash, ongoing reliability issues.

**16. Was Quirky's failure a fraud or scandal?**
No — this is presented as a strategy/focus failure, not fraud or misconduct.

**17. What is Quirky's inventor royalty model?**
Inventors and community contributors earned a share of sales revenue. Sources conflict on the
exact percentage structure, so this video doesn't state a specific disputed number.

**18. What happened to Ben Kaufman after Quirky?**
He joined BuzzFeed as Chief Commerce Officer in 2016, became CMO in January 2019, stepped down that
August, and founded CAMP, a family-experience retail chain, in 2018, which he still leads.

**19. Did Ben Kaufman face any fraud or legal allegations?**
No — no such allegations were found in this research; his post-Quirky career is a normal executive
trajectory.

**20. How much did GE invest in Quirky?**
$30 million, announced November 13, 2013, as part of a larger $79 million Series D round.

**21. Why did GE partner with a crowdsourced gadget startup?**
To co-develop connected-home products using GE's own patents and to establish Wink as its preferred
connected-home software platform.

**22. What was Quirky's original product launch goal?**
One new consumer product per week, later tripled to three per week after the 2012 Series C.

**23. What's the core lesson of this video?**
That having a working idea-generation engine isn't the same as having a business strategy — a
company that can build almost anything still has to decide what NOT to build.

**24. How does this compare to this channel's Parse video?**
Both ask whether a product can survive its company's death. Parse Server's survival is clean and
thriving; Wink's survival is real but messy, marked by ownership changes and user backlash.

**25. Did Quirky's products sell well?**
Some individual products were genuine hits; the broader portfolio suffered from thin retail margins
and little iteration once products launched.

**26. How big did Quirky get?**
Over 400 employees and over a million engaged community members at its peak.

**27. Was Home Depot involved with Quirky?**
Yes — Home Depot agreed to carry and integrate Wink-connected smart-home devices as part of the
broader GE/Wink partnership.

**28. What can other founders learn from Quirky's collapse?**
That accelerating output after a funding round isn't automatically progress, and that big-name
partnerships and capital don't substitute for a clear sense of what a company should say no to.

A working idea-generation engine and a working business strategy are two different things — and a

partnerships behind it.
