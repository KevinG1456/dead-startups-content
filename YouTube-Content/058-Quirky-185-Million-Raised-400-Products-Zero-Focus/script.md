# Script Package

Anchored on Quirky — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Tonal note: this is a "strategy failure,"
not a "fraud" or "villain" story — Ben Kaufman built a genuinely working invention engine that
scaled itself to death. Keep the tone curious and a little wry, not accusatory. The Wink-afterlife
ending is deliberately NOT triumphant — hold it as an honest, ambiguous close, not a tidy bow.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11-13 minutes (founding, scale-up, GE partnership, collapse, and the Wink
afterlife all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00-0:15)
- VO: "This startup raised $185 million and shipped 400 products. Name one."
- Visual: a fast, rapid-fire montage of generic gadget silhouettes flashing past — dozens in a few
  seconds — before hard-cutting to black
- Pattern Interrupt: white text on black: "Most people can't name a single one. That's the whole
  story."

### Hook (0:15-1:00)
- VO: "This is Quirky — a startup that built a genuinely working machine for turning ordinary
  people's ideas into real, manufactured products. It had real funding, a real partnership with
  General Electric, and a real community of over a million people submitting ideas every week."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly why having too many good ideas
  can be just as dangerous as having none — and what actually happened to the one part of this
  company that's technically still alive today."

### Introduction (1:00-1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from TechCrunch, Forbes, GE's own press releases, Fortune, and
  bankruptcy-court reporting — sourced on screen throughout
- Framing: "This isn't a fraud story or a villain story. This is a focus story."

### Main Content — The Machine (1:40-4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40-2:20):** Ben Kaufman, 22, publicly launched Quirky in June
  2009 — after already selling his first company, mophie, in 2007. The model: the community
  submits and votes on product ideas, Quirky's team designs and manufactures the winners,
  everyone who contributed shares in the sales revenue. Visual: "IDEA → VOTE → BUILD → SELL →
  SHARE" flow graphic.
- **Segment 2 — It Actually Worked (2:20-2:50):** by the early 2010s, Quirky had over a million
  engaged community members and real hit products in real stores. Visual: generic "1M+ community"
  stat card.
- **Segment 3 — The Funding (2:50-3:40):** $6M Series A (RRE Ventures, 2010), a Series B led by
  Norwest, then a $68M Series C in 2012 led by Andreessen Horowitz — ~$185M raised in total across
  the company's life. Visual: funding-rounds ladder graphic, "$185M TOTAL RAISED" title card.
- **Segment 4 — Then They Sped Up (3:40-4:30):** the original goal was one new product a week.
  After the 2012 Series C, Quirky tripled that pace: three new products every week, chosen from
  over 1,000 weekly community submissions. On-screen framing card: "This is the moment the machine
  stopped being able to slow down." Visual: "1 PRODUCT/WEEK → 3 PRODUCTS/WEEK" title card.

### Main Content — GE and Wink (4:30-7:00)
- **Segment 5 — General Electric Gets Involved (4:30-5:30):** starting in 2011, Quirky partnered
  with GE to co-develop connected smart-home products using GE's own patents. In November 2013, GE
  expanded the deal with a $30 million equity investment, folded into a $79 million Series D.
  Visual: "$30 MILLION — GE'S INVESTMENT, NOVEMBER 2013" title card.
- **Segment 6 — Wink Is Born (5:30-6:15):** out of that partnership came Wink, a smart-home hub
  and app, spun off by Quirky as its own standalone company in mid-2014. GE named Wink its
  preferred connected-home platform; Home Depot signed on too. Visual: Wink logo/app-interface
  graphic (generic, non-trademarked representation).
- **Segment 7 — 400 Products, No Focus (6:15-7:00):** by the time it collapsed, Quirky had shipped
  roughly 400 distinct products across almost every consumer category imaginable — kitchen
  gadgets, office supplies, smart-home hardware — with thin retail margins and little iteration
  once something launched. On-screen framing card: "There was no version of this company that
  wasn't trying to do everything at once." Visual: sprawling grid of generic product-silhouette
  icons filling the screen.

### Main Content — The Collapse (7:00-9:00)
- **Segment 8 — The Slide (7:00-7:45):** layoffs began in late 2014 as funding got harder to
  raise. July 31, 2015: Ben Kaufman stepped down as CEO alongside a 111-person layoff. Visual:
  "JULY 31, 2015 — KAUFMAN STEPS DOWN, 111 LAID OFF" title card.
- **Segment 9 — Bankruptcy (7:45-8:30):** September 22, 2015 — Quirky filed Chapter 11, explicitly
  to facilitate an asset sale. Wink went up for auction; Flextronics won it with a $15 million bid,
  approved by the bankruptcy court that December. Visual: "SEPTEMBER 22, 2015 — CHAPTER 11" title
  card.
- **Segment 10 — What GE's $30 Million Actually Bought (8:30-9:00):** direct framing beat — a
  Fortune-500 company's money, patents, and retail partner (Home Depot) still weren't enough to
  overcome a strategy problem. Visual: "$30 MILLION. STILL NOT ENOUGH." title card.

### Main Content — The Messy Afterlife (9:00-10:30)
- **Segment 11 — Wink Didn't Die, Exactly (9:00-9:45):** Flex resold Wink to will.i.am's company
  i.am+ for $59 million in 2017 — a real, sourced jump from the $15 million Flex paid less than
  two years earlier. Visual: "$15M → $59M" title card.
- **Segment 12 — But It's Not a Happy Ending (9:45-10:30):** in May 2020, Wink imposed a mandatory
  $4.99/month subscription on existing hardware owners, sparking real backlash from people who
  already owned the devices. The platform has a documented history of outages continuing into
  2024 and 2025. On-screen framing card: "This channel has covered a company whose product
  outlived it and thrived [reference package 049, Parse]. This is the more honest, messier
  version — the product survived, but it's limping." Visual: split card, "PARSE SERVER: THRIVING"
  vs. "WINK: STILL ALIVE, STILL SHAKY."
- Mid-video CTA (~10:15): "Most of what's on this channel ends in a clean death or a clean
  survival. This one just ends messy — and I think that's actually more realistic than either."

### The Actual Lesson (10:30-11:45)
- Plain-language comparison: Quirky didn't fail because its idea was bad or its funding ran dry
  from mismanagement of any single deal — it failed because it never developed the discipline to
  say no. A working idea-generation engine without a filter isn't a company, it's a firehose.
- The core lesson, stated directly: more output is not the same as more strategy — a business
  that can build almost anything still has to decide what NOT to build.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founded 2009 → 2012 Series C ($68M) → 3 products/week →
  2013 GE $30M investment → 2014 Wink spinoff → 2015 bankruptcy → Wink resold 2017 → 2020
  subscription backlash → still shakily alive today.

### Final CTA + Outro (11:45-13:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — having ideas was never the constraint; having the discipline
  to say no to most of them was
- "Subscribe — this channel covers what actually happens when ambition outruns strategy, one real
  company at a time."
- Engagement prompt: "Can you actually name a Quirky product? Tell me in the comments — I'm
  curious how many people genuinely remember one."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the funding ladder and the 1-to-3-products-per-week acceleration graphic before
working through the GE deal, Wink spinoff, and collapse as supporting detail. Measured,
graphics-forward, treats the video as a case study in scale-without-focus as a specific, avoidable
strategic failure mode.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Ben Kaufman's mophie exit and the founding moment, builds slowly through the company's
real early wins before the GE partnership and product-count acceleration, then spends real time on
the collapse and Wink's tangled afterlife as the closing act. Slower pacing, more emotionally
textured.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one company, 400 products, one bankruptcy" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
funding ladder, GE deal, product-pace acceleration, collapse, Wink's three owners, present-day
status — landing on the "too many ideas, not enough focus" thesis as the final, clarifying beat.
Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the product-count montage and the $15M-to-$59M Wink
resale beat.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the funding-rounds segment (2:50-3:40) if it plays as a dry recitation of
  dollar figures without visual momentum
- **Fix:** keep the funding ladder animating upward in real time as each round is named, rather
  than a static graphic
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general crowdsourcing-model explanation if audience
  familiarity is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the "can you name one?" challenge again right before
  Segment 7's 400-product reveal if early-retention data shows drop-off before that point
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a running product-count ticker visible from Segment 4
  onward, climbing toward "~400" as the timeline advances
