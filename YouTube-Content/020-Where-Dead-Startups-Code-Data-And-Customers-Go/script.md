## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11–14 minutes

### Cold Open (0:00–0:20)
- VO: "What actually happens to a startup's code, data, and customers after it shuts down?"
- Visual: three-branch flow diagram animates in, single node splitting into three arrows toward question marks, with a heavy SFX hit
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "It doesn't just disappear. It goes somewhere. Here's the map."

### The Supply Chain, Overview (0:20–1:40)
- Plain-language framing: a startup isn't one indivisible thing — when it dies, its code/IP, its customer data, and its customer relationships each follow separate legal and commercial paths
- Preview: this video maps all three tracks, grounded in real, sourced cases (Sidecar/GM, RadioShack, Cambridge Analytica, Parse)

### Track 1: Where the Code Goes (1:40–4:00)
- Explain the two common outcomes: an acqui-hire (the team and sometimes the codebase move to the acquirer, product usually discontinued) vs. a pure asset sale (specific assets, including patents/IP, sold off separately, sometimes to an unrelated buyer)
- Real example: Sidecar's 2015 shutdown — GM acquired its technology/assets and hired roughly 20 employees, with a *license* to Sidecar's patent portfolio (Sidecar retained ownership) as a key deal component, not a straight patent sale. The twist: GM wasn't an unconnected buyer — the deal closed two weeks after GM's $500M investment in Lyft, as part of the same ride-sharing strategy against Uber. Even a "clean asset carve-out" story usually has a strategic buyer behind it
- Mention the broader pattern of patent-assertion entities ("patent trolls") acquiring dead startups' patent portfolios cheaply, sometimes later using them offensively — general, well-documented industry pattern, not tied to a single named case here
- Visual: flow-diagram Track 1 branch highlights, Sidecar/GM logos (sourced, on-screen citation)

### Track 2: Where the Data Goes (4:00–7:30)
- Explain that customer/user data is legally treated as a company asset that can be sold in a bankruptcy or asset sale — even when a privacy policy promised otherwise
- Real example: RadioShack's 2015 bankruptcy attempt to sell ~tens of millions of customer records; multiple state AGs objected citing RadioShack's own privacy policy; court-approved settlement significantly restricted the sale — the landmark case that shaped how this is handled today
- Explain the "consumer privacy ombudsman" mechanism (created by 11 U.S.C. §332, triggered when §363(b)(1) requires a hearing on a privacy-policy-conflicting data sale) that some bankruptcy courts now require when a proposed data sale conflicts with a privacy policy
- Real example: Cambridge Analytica's 2018 bankruptcy — but distinguish the mechanism from RadioShack's: this wasn't a §363 bankruptcy-court data-sale under review, it was a separate FTC Section 5 enforcement action (final order December 2019) requiring deletion of the improperly obtained Facebook data and associated work product — regulatory enforcement running alongside the bankruptcy, not a court-supervised asset sale
- Visual: flow-diagram Track 2 branch highlights, simplified bankruptcy-sale process graphic, "ombudsman" role explained visually

### Track 3: Where the Customers Go (7:30–9:30)
- Mid-video CTA (~7:30, opening this segment): "If you've ever used a startup product that shut down, the next part answers what actually happened on your end. Subscribe for a new breakdown every week."
- Explain the spectrum from graceful to abrupt: contract assignment clauses, migration paths, and the composite pattern of B2B SaaS shutdowns giving customers a short data-export window
- Real example: Parse's 2017 shutdown — roughly a year's notice, open-sourced server software, structured self-hosting migration path — the "graceful" end of the spectrum
- Contrast: the common, illustrative pattern of abrupt consumer-app shutdowns leaving users with little to no migration path or advance notice
- Visual: flow-diagram Track 3 branch highlights, side-by-side Parse timeline vs. illustrative abrupt-shutdown timeline

### What to Check Before You Trust a Startup With Your Data (9:30–11:00)
- Practical segment: what to look for in a privacy policy (data-sale/transfer-on-shutdown clauses), what a bankruptcy filing means for existing customers, questions B2B buyers should ask vendors about a shutdown/data-portability plan

### Final CTA + Outro (11:00–12:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a startup's death isn't an ending, it's a fork in three separate supply chains, and understanding where each branch leads matters for founders, employees, and every user
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these"
- Engagement prompt: "Have you ever tried to find out what happened to your data after an app you used shut down? Comment below."
- End screen: two related autopsies + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed three-branch flow diagram, then works backward through each track in legal/mechanical detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a genuine legal-education resource.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on a specific real moment — a RadioShack customer learning their data was part of a bankruptcy sale — before any diagram appears. Slower pacing, more narrative-driven, builds outward from the human stakes to the general mechanism.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "3 things that happen to a dead startup that nobody explains," each track covered in under 100 seconds. Strong Shorts-extraction potential.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** Track 2 (Data, 4:00–7:30) if the bankruptcy-law mechanics (Section 363, ombudsman) become too dense without enough visual pacing
- **Fix:** keep the bankruptcy-process explanation visual and procedural (a simple step-by-step graphic) rather than narrating legal citations at length
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** if audience feedback suggests strong familiarity with acqui-hires (Track 1 basics), compress that section and spend more time on the less-understood data-ombudsman mechanic
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the RadioShack case early (in the cold open or overview) to sustain interest through the more procedural middle segment
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the three-branch flow diagram persistently visible (mini version in a corner) throughout, highlighting the active branch, to maintain the "map" framing
