## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week:
23andMe — founded in 2006 by Anne Wojcicki, Linda Avey, and Paul Cusenza, built around
direct-to-consumer genetic testing and, later, a research and pharma data-licensing business built
on top of its accumulated genetic database. More than 15 million people used the product.

23andMe raised just under $900 million in private funding — including an $85 million Series F
round announced shortly before its SPAC deal — before going public in 2021 by merging with VG
Acquisition Corp, a SPAC backed by Sir Richard Branson's Virgin Group. The deal itself was valued
at approximately $3.5 billion when announced; after the merger closed, the stock briefly traded
high enough to cross a $6 billion market value — a separate, higher figure than the deal valuation,
and this video keeps the two clearly distinct throughout. In June 2021, Anne Wojcicki became a
self-made billionaire on paper as a result.

In October 2023, 23andMe disclosed a data breach with a very specific mechanism: a five-month
credential-stuffing attack, beginning around April 29, 2023, in which attackers reused usernames
and passwords leaked from OTHER, unrelated prior breaches to log into 23andMe accounts that
happened to reuse the same credentials. This was NOT a direct hack of 23andMe's own systems.
Roughly 14,000 accounts were compromised this way — but because of 23andMe's "DNA Relatives"
family-matching feature, that exposed data belonging to approximately 6.9 million total users. The
breach became public on October 6, 2023, after a hacker using the pseudonym "Golem" posted a
database on the cybercrime forum BreachForums labeled "Ashkenazi DNA Data of Celebrities," and
separately claimed to hold data belonging to 350,000 users of Chinese heritage, offering both for
sale. This video presents that detail factually and soberly, attributed directly to the reporting
that documented it (JTA, Forward, Times of Israel, Jerusalem Post) — it's a real, necessary part of
the story, not a detail to sensationalize.

A $30 million class-action settlement followed in September 2024, covering the roughly 6.9 million
affected users (a later-referenced $50 million bankruptcy-court figure is flagged as unconfirmed
rather than stated as final). In June 2025, a joint investigation by Canada's Privacy Commissioner
and the UK's Information Commissioner's Office publicly criticized 23andMe's security practices.

Then came the governance collapse. Throughout 2024, Anne Wojcicki repeatedly proposed taking
23andMe private. On September 17, 2024, all seven of the company's independent directors resigned
on the same day, stating in their resignation letter that Wojcicki had not delivered "a fully
financed, fully diligenced, actionable proposal that is in the best interests of the
non-affiliated shareholders." In November 2024, the company cut more than 200 jobs — about 40% of
its remaining workforce — and discontinued its cancer-research division. On March 23–24, 2025,
23andMe filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Delaware; Wojcicki resigned as CEO the same day.

In the bankruptcy auction that followed, Wojcicki — bidding through a nonprofit she founded called
TTAM Research Institute — beat out Regeneron Pharmaceuticals and won the company's core data
assets, including the genetic data of 15+ million customers, for $305 million. This video treats
that outcome as the genuine twist it is: the founder ends up back in control of the core asset, the
data itself, just through a nonprofit vehicle and for a fraction of the company's SPAC-era peak
valuation.

This is a security-and-governance story, not a fraud story — Anne Wojcicki has not been charged
with any crime. Cross-referenced throughout with this channel's other two SPAC-to-bankruptcy
stories, package 025 (Sonder) and package 026 (WeWork), and contrasted with package 029 (Ozy
Media/Carlos Watson), where the founder faces the opposite outcome: an actual criminal conviction.
