# Script Package

Anchored on 23andMe — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. This is a security-and-governance
story, not a unit-economics one: the 2023 breach did not bankrupt the company directly, it
triggered a board revolt that did. Non-negotiables for every version of this script: (1) the 2023
breach was a CREDENTIAL-STUFFING attack — reused passwords from OTHER companies' breaches, not a
direct hack of 23andMe's own systems — state this precisely every time the breach is described;
(2) present the Ashkenazi Jewish and Chinese-ancestry targeting factually and soberly, attributed
to the specific reporting that covered it (JTA, Forward, Times of Israel, Jerusalem Post) — this is
a real, legitimate, necessary part of the story, never a shock beat; (3) distinguish the $3.5
billion SPAC-deal-announcement valuation from the ~$6 billion peak market value the stock actually
reached shortly after listing — two different, both-real numbers, always labeled; (4) use $30
million as the settlement figure, flag a later-referenced $50 million bankruptcy-court figure as
[VERIFY] if mentioned, never state one final number without rechecking; (5) Anne Wojcicki has not
been charged with any crime — frame her story as governance and crisis management, never
"fraud/scam/lied" language; (6) the TTAM Research Institute outcome is the genuine ending twist —
Wojcicki reacquires the company's core data assets through a nonprofit she founded, for $305
million, after the public company itself was wiped out in bankruptcy. Cross-reference package 025
(Sonder) and 026 (WeWork) explicitly on screen as this channel's other SPAC-to-bankruptcy stories,
and 026 (WeWork/Neumann) against 029 (Ozy Media/Carlos Watson) for the "founder survives" vs.
"founder convicted" contrast.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 17–19 minutes (single-company deep dive with a five-act structure: build →
breach → board revolt → bankruptcy → twist ending)

- VO: "In June 2021, this company's founder became a self-made billionaire — on paper — as the
  stock briefly crossed a $6 billion market value. In March 2025, she resigned as CEO inside her
  own company's bankruptcy filing. In between, 6.9 million people's genetic data ended up for sale
  on a cybercrime forum."
- Visual: "JUNE 2021 — $6B PEAK MARKET VALUE" text, hard cut to "MARCH 2025 — CHAPTER 11 FILED,"
  a thin timeline connecting the two with a small marker at "OCTOBER 2023 — DATA BREACH REVEALED"
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This company's product was people's
  own DNA."

### Hook (0:20–1:00)
- VO: "This is 23andMe — a company millions of people trusted with their own genetic code. It went
  public via SPAC, briefly hit a $6 billion market value, survived a serious data breach, and then
  watched its entire independent board resign on the same day. Six months after that, it filed for
  bankruptcy. And its own founder ended up bidding against a pharmaceutical company to buy back the
  one thing that made the company valuable in the first place: the data itself."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how a credential-stuffing attack
  — not a hack of 23andMe's own systems — exposed millions of people's data, why that specific
  breach targeted people by ancestry, how that fallout led seven directors to quit in one day, and
  what actually happened to your data — or your relatives' data — when this company went bankrupt."

### Introduction (1:00–1:50)
- Method statement: reconstructed from CNBC, Fortune, Forbes, Built In SF, Security.org, HIPAA
  Journal, Dark Reading, JTA, Forward, Times of Israel, Jerusalem Post, the Office of the Privacy
  Commissioner of Canada, Yale Daily News, Al Jazeera, and Caproasia's bankruptcy-filing summary —
  sourced on screen throughout.
- One-sentence framing: this is not a fraud or wrongdoing story — Anne Wojcicki has not been
  charged with any crime — it's a story about a security incident that cascaded into a governance
  crisis, and a governance crisis that cascaded into bankruptcy.
- Direct cross-reference: "This channel has already covered two other SPAC-to-bankruptcy stories —
  package 025, Sonder, and package 026, WeWork. Each one collapsed for a different proximate
  reason. This is the third, and it's the strangest ending of the three."

### Main Content — The 23andMe Case (1:50–16:15)

- **Segment 1 — The Founding and the Business (1:50–3:15):** Founded 2006 by Anne Wojcicki, Linda
  Avey, and Paul Cusenza. The business: direct-to-consumer genetic testing — mail in a saliva
  sample, get back ancestry and health-risk information — later expanded into a research and
  pharma data-licensing business built on top of its accumulated genetic database. Over time, more
  than 15 million customers used the product. Visual: founder graphic, saliva-kit-to-database
  pipeline animation, customer-count climbing toward 15 million.

- **Segment 2 — The Raise and the SPAC (3:15–4:45):** Prior to going public, 23andMe raised
  "just under $900 million" in total private funding — stated as an approximate figure, not one
  precise all-time total, consistent with how this channel treats messy aggregator numbers (see
  package 025's Sonder funding note for the same pattern) — including an $85 million Series F
  round announced shortly before the SPAC deal. In February 2021, 23andMe announced it would go
  public by merging with VG Acquisition Corp., a SPAC backed by Sir Richard Branson's Virgin Group
  — expected to deliver roughly $984 million in cash at close. Visual: funding timeline building
  toward "just under $900M," SPAC-merger graphic with the Virgin Group / VG Acquisition Corp.
  branding described verbally, not rendered as a logo.

- **Segment 3 — Going Public: Two Different Numbers (4:45–5:45):** The SPAC deal itself was
  valued at approximately $3.5 billion when it was announced. After the merger closed later in
  2021, the stock briefly traded high enough to cross a $6 billion market value — a separate,
  higher figure that reflects where the stock actually traded shortly after listing, not the deal
  terms themselves. On-screen note, held for a beat: "$3.5B = the deal's announced valuation. $6B
  = the stock's peak market value after listing. Two different, both-real numbers." In June 2021,
  Anne Wojcicki became a self-made billionaire on paper as a result. Visual: side-by-side graphic,
  "$3.5B — SPAC DEAL VALUATION" next to "$6B — PEAK MARKET VALUE," clearly separated, never merged
  into one figure.

- **Segment 4 — What 23andMe Actually Sold (5:45–6:45):** The product wasn't just a one-time test
  result — it was an accumulating genetic database, made more valuable by features like "DNA
  Relatives," which shows customers their genetic connections to other users on the platform. That
  same feature is the reason a small breach could expose a much larger population. On-screen note:
  this segment sets up exactly how the math in the next segment works. Visual: network-graph
  animation showing one compromised node lighting up dozens of connected nodes.

- **Segment 5 — The 2023 Breach: How It Actually Happened (6:45–8:30):** Beginning around April
  29, 2023, and continuing for about five months, attackers used a credential-stuffing attack —
  reusing usernames and passwords leaked from OTHER, unrelated prior breaches to log into 23andMe
  accounts that happened to reuse the same credentials. State this precisely on screen: this was
  NOT a breach of 23andMe's own systems or database directly. Approximately 14,000 individual
  accounts were directly compromised this way. Because of the "DNA Relatives" family-matching
  feature explained in the previous segment, compromising those 14,000 accounts exposed data
  belonging to approximately 6.9 million total users. Contributing factors, per subsequent security
  analysis: 23andMe did not require two-factor authentication at the time, and its password policy
  required only a minimum of eight characters with minimal complexity requirements. Visual: "NOT A
  HACK OF 23ANDME'S SYSTEMS" text card, then the 14,000-to-6.9-million exposure animation building
  on the network graph from Segment 4.

- **Segment 6 — October 6, 2023: The Reveal (8:30–10:00):** The breach became public on October 6,
  2023, after a hacker using the pseudonym "Golem" posted a database on BreachForums, a cybercrime
  forum, labeled "Ashkenazi DNA Data of Celebrities," and separately claimed to possess data
  belonging to 350,000 users of Chinese heritage — offering both datasets for sale. State this
  factually and soberly, attributed directly to the reporting that covered it: JTA, Forward, Times
  of Israel, and the Jerusalem Post all specifically documented that the attacker marketed the
  stolen data by ancestry. This is a real, well-documented, and serious part of the story — it
  directly shaped the lawsuits and regulatory response that followed — present it without
  sensationalizing, and without dwelling past what the sourced reporting actually states. Visual:
  sober on-screen text card with source attribution ("According to JTA, Forward, Times of Israel,
  and the Jerusalem Post"), no dramatized imagery, no ethnicity-specific visual symbols of any kind.

- **Segment 7 — The Settlement and the Regulatory Findings (10:00–11:00):** In September 2024,
  23andMe agreed to a $30 million settlement of the resulting class-action lawsuit, covering the
  roughly 6.9 million affected users — this is the primary, most widely and consistently reported
  figure. On-screen [VERIFY] note: one source, HIPAA Journal, later references a revised $50
  million figure in bankruptcy-court proceedings; that later figure is flagged here as unconfirmed
  rather than stated as a final settlement number. In June 2025, a joint investigation by Canada's
  Privacy Commissioner and the UK's Information Commissioner's Office concluded and publicly
  criticized 23andMe's security practices in connection with the breach. Visual: "$30 MILLION —
  SEPTEMBER 2024 SETTLEMENT" primary card, small "$50M figure later referenced — [VERIFY]" flagged
  secondary card, joint-regulator-report citation card.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:30): "If you want the other two SPAC-to-bankruptcy stories this channel has
  covered — Sonder in package 025, WeWork in package 026 — links are in the description. Each one
  collapsed for a genuinely different reason. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

- **Segment 8 — The Board Collapse (11:00–12:30):** Throughout 2024, Anne Wojcicki repeatedly
  proposed taking 23andMe private, submitting multiple proposals to the board's special committee.
  On September 17, 2024, all seven independent directors resigned from the board on the same day:
  Roelof Botha, Patrick Chung, Sandra Hernández, Neal Mohan, Valerie Montgomery Rice, Richard
  Scheller, and Peter Taylor. Their resignation letter stated, directly quoted on screen, that
  Wojcicki had not delivered "a fully financed, fully diligenced, actionable proposal that is in
  the best interests of the non-affiliated shareholders," despite months of engagement. This is a
  rare, on-record, board-level statement of no confidence — use the quote directly rather than a
  paraphrase, the same way this channel used James Beriker's court-filed statement in package
  023's Munchery story. Visual: seven names appearing simultaneously on screen, resignation-letter
  quote typed out in full.

- **Segment 9 — The Layoffs and the Cancer-Research Cut (12:30–13:15):** In November 2024,
  23andMe cut more than 200 employees — about 40% of its remaining workforce — and discontinued its
  therapeutics/cancer-research division as an emergency cost-cutting measure following the board
  exodus. Visual: headcount graphic dropping sharply, "THERAPEUTICS DIVISION DISCONTINUED" card.

- **Segment 10 — Bankruptcy (13:15–14:15):** On March 23–24, 2025, 23andMe filed for Chapter 11
  bankruptcy in Delaware. Anne Wojcicki resigned as CEO effective immediately, remaining only as a
  board member. The company cited declining stock value, the fallout from the 2023 breach, the
  workforce cuts, and the board collapse as converging causes. Visual: dated filing card, "CEO
  RESIGNS SAME DAY" text stamp.

- **Segment 11 — The Auction: Regeneron vs. TTAM (14:15–15:15):** A two-stage bankruptcy auction
  followed, contested between Regeneron Pharmaceuticals and Anne Wojcicki herself — bidding through
  TTAM Research Institute, a nonprofit she founded specifically to try to reacquire the company's
  assets. Visual: two-bidder auction graphic, Regeneron on one side, TTAM Research Institute
  (described verbally, no logo) on the other.

- **Segment 12 — The Twist: What $305 Million Actually Bought (15:15–16:15):** The bankruptcy
  court approved a $305 million sale to TTAM Research Institute. Anne Wojcicki ultimately did
  regain control of the company's core assets, including the genetic data of more than 15 million
  customers — but only after the public-company structure itself had been wiped out through
  bankruptcy. Hold this explicitly as the genuine twist ending, not a simple "company died" story:
  the founder ends up back in control of the core asset — the data — just through a nonprofit
  vehicle, and for a fraction of the company's SPAC-era peak valuation. On-screen note: "$6B peak
  market value → $305M nonprofit sale. Same founder, same core asset, very different structure."
  Visual: valuation-collapse graphic running from $6B down to $305M, resolving into a hand-off
  animation — public company icon dissolving, nonprofit icon receiving the data-asset icon.

### The Actual Lesson (16:15–17:15)
- Plain-language comparison: 23andMe's collapse didn't start with a bankruptcy filing — it started
  with a security incident that most companies survive. What made it fatal here was the sequence:
  a breach that specifically undermined trust in how the company handled its most sensitive asset,
  a settlement, a regulatory rebuke, and then a board that ran out of confidence in its own
  founder's turnaround plan. The bankruptcy was the last domino, not the first one.
- The core irony, stated directly: this channel has now covered three SPAC-to-bankruptcy stories —
  Sonder (025), where a partner's contract termination was the trigger; WeWork (026), where chronic
  unprofitability plus a second market crash did it; and 23andMe, where a data breach's fallout
  compounded into a full board revolt. This is also the only one of the three where the founder
  ends the story back in control of a version of the company — worth contrasting directly with
  package 029 (Ozy Media), where the founder faces the opposite outcome: an actual criminal
  conviction. Anne Wojcicki has not been charged with any crime.
- Visual: three-package comparison graphic (025 Sonder / 026 WeWork / 027 23andMe) — trigger,
  timeline, ending — landing on the founder-outcome contrast with 029.

### Final CTA + Outro (17:15–18:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a data breach doesn't have to hack a company's own systems to
  end the company; it just has to break the board's confidence in the people running it.
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these. If you used 23andMe yourself, the TTAM
  transition is worth understanding for what it means for your own data going forward."
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever use 23andMe? Do you see the TTAM outcome as the founder coming
  back to make things right, or as something closer to self-dealing? Tell me in the comments —
  both readings are genuinely on the table."
- End screen: package 025 (Sonder) + package 026 (WeWork) + subscribe.
