# 027 — 23andMe Hit a $6 Billion Valuation. A Data Breach and a Board Revolt Ended It.

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built around 23andMe's full public history (CNBC, Fortune, Forbes, JTA/Times of Israel/Forward, HIPAA Journal, SEC filings — full citations in `sources-log.txt`). A genuinely different failure shape from everything else on this channel: not a burn-rate or platform-risk story, but one where a security incident cascaded into a board collapse, which cascaded into bankruptcy — with the company's core asset (real people's genetic data) at the center of the whole story.

**The real case:** 23andMe — founded 2006 by Anne Wojcicki, Linda Avey, and Paul Cusenza — went public via a SPAC merger with Richard Branson's VG Acquisition Corp in 2021, briefly crossing a $6 billion market value after listing. In October 2023, the company disclosed that a five-month credential-stuffing attack (reusing passwords leaked from OTHER breaches, not a hack of 23andMe's own systems) had compromised roughly 14,000 accounts directly — which, through 23andMe's "DNA Relative" family-matching feature, exposed data belonging to approximately 6.9 million users. The attacker specifically marketed stolen data by ancestry, posting a database labeled "Ashkenazi DNA Data of Celebrities" and separately claiming records for 350,000 users of Chinese heritage. A $30 million class-action settlement followed in September 2024. Weeks later, on September 17, 2024, all seven of 23andMe's independent board directors resigned on the same day, citing Wojcicki's failure to deliver a "fully financed, fully diligenced, actionable proposal" to take the company private. The company cut 200+ jobs and killed its cancer-research division in November 2024. On March 23-24, 2025, 23andMe filed Chapter 11; Wojcicki resigned as CEO the same day. In the bankruptcy auction that followed, Wojcicki — bidding through a nonprofit she founded called TTAM Research Institute — beat out Regeneron Pharmaceuticals and won back the company's core assets, including the genetic data of 15+ million customers, for $305 million.

**Framing requirement:** This is a security-and-governance story, not a unit-economics one — the breach didn't bankrupt the company directly, it triggered a board revolt that did. The Ashkenazi Jewish/Chinese-ancestry targeting detail must be handled soberly and factually, attributed to specific reporting — it's a legitimate, necessary part of the story (it shaped the resulting lawsuits), not a detail to sensationalize. The TTAM ending is the twist: the founder ends up back in control of the core asset, just via a nonprofit and a much smaller sum than the company's SPAC-era peak.
