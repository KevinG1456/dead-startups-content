# Script Package

Anchored on Parse, Inc. — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Tonal note that applies throughout
this entire script: this is one of the more upbeat entries in this channel's catalog. Let that
land — don't force the usual somber register onto a story that's genuinely, verifiably a good
outcome for the technology and its users, even though the company itself is dead.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11-13 minutes (founding, acquisition, the announced shutdown, the open-source
release, and the present-day afterlife all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00-0:15)
- VO: "Facebook killed this company in 2017. Its code has more users today than it ever did."
- Visual: a 2026-dated GitHub screenshot — "21,407 stars," a recent commit timestamp — slowly
  zooming out to reveal it belongs to a company logo with "SHUT DOWN 2017" stamped over it
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to black, white text: "This isn't a story about a company that
  failed. It's a story about what happened after."

### Hook (0:15-1:00)
- VO: "This is Parse — a Y Combinator company that Facebook bought in 2013, then shut down four
  years later. Normally that's where these stories end."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly what Facebook did differently
  here, and why the thing they killed is, right now, in 2026, doing better than it ever did as a
  company."

### Introduction (1:00-1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, TechCrunch, VentureBeat, and Parse Server's own
  live GitHub repository — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early: "One note up front — this channel is usually about failures. This
  one has a genuinely good ending. Stick around for that part."

### Main Content — The Company (1:40-4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40-2:15):** Parse, Inc. founded 2011, Y Combinator Summer 2011
  batch — Ilya Sukhar, James Yu, Tikhon Bernstam, Kevin Lacker. Built a mobile Backend-as-a-Service
  platform: cloud data storage, user logins, push notifications, and cloud-hosted code for mobile
  app developers, at a moment when almost every app needed to build that infrastructure from
  scratch. Visual: generic "before Parse / after Parse" mobile-dev-stack graphic.
- **Segment 2 — Genuine Cross-Reference (2:15-2:45):** on-screen callback — "One of these four
  founders, Tikhon Bernstam, also co-founded Scribd — the one company from the YC Summer 2006 batch
  we fully audited that's still independently operating today [reference package 007]. Same
  founder, two YC companies, five years apart, two completely different kinds of 'still around.'"
  Visual: split-screen, Scribd logo (still operating) vs. Parse logo (company dead, code alive).
- **Segment 3 — The Acquisition (2:45-3:45):** raised roughly $7-8.5M total, then April 25, 2013,
  Facebook acquired Parse for a reported ~$85 million, part-cash part-stock — explicitly positioned
  as Facebook's first-ever paid B2B product. Visual: "$85 MILLION — FACEBOOK'S ACQUISITION, APRIL
  2013" title card.
- **Segment 4 — Three Good Years (3:45-4:30):** Parse operated and grew as Facebook's developer
  platform for roughly three years, becoming one of the most widely used mobile BaaS platforms in
  the industry. Visual: adoption-growth graphic, generic upward curve.

### Main Content — The Shutdown, Done Right (4:30-7:30)
- **Segment 5 — The Announcement (4:30-5:30):** January 2016, Facebook announces it's sunsetting
  the hosted Parse.com service — but sets the actual shutdown date a full year out: January 28,
  2017. On-screen framing card: "This is a notably longer wind-down window than almost anything else
  on this channel." Visual: "ONE YEAR'S NOTICE" title card with a calendar graphic spanning Jan
  2016 to Jan 2017.
- **Segment 6 — The Part That Matters (5:30-6:45):** at the same moment as the shutdown
  announcement, Facebook does something most acquirers never do — it open-sources the entire Parse
  backend as "Parse Server," a Node.js project anyone can run on their own infrastructure, framed
  explicitly as a migration path so customers wouldn't be stranded. Visual: "OPEN-SOURCED — SAME
  DAY AS THE SHUTDOWN ANNOUNCEMENT" title card.
- **Segment 7 — Exactly on Schedule (6:45-7:30):** January 28, 2017 — Parse.com shuts down exactly
  on the date announced a year earlier. The large majority of existing Parse customers migrate
  directly to self-hosted Parse Server rather than being forced to rebuild from scratch. Visual:
  "JANUARY 28, 2017 — SHUT DOWN ON SCHEDULE" title card.

### Main Content — The Afterlife (7:30-10:00)
- **Segment 8 — Nine Years Later (7:30-8:45):** cut to present day. Parse Server has been
  continuously, independently maintained by a community organization — not Facebook — ever since.
  As of 2026: 21,407+ GitHub stars, active commits within the past year, current support for
  Node.js 18 through 24, a support roadmap extending to 2028. On-screen framing card: "Facebook has
  had zero involvement in this for almost a decade. This is a community that kept going without the
  company that started it." Visual: live-looking GitHub repo stats screenshot, "STILL ACTIVELY
  MAINTAINED — 2026" title card.
- **Segment 9 — Died vs. Won (8:45-9:30):** direct contrast card: "The company: dead since 2017.
  The code: more actively maintained right now than most software from companies that are still
  alive." Visual: split card, "COMPANY: DEAD" / "CODE: ALIVE" side by side.
- **Segment 10 — The Contrast With This Channel's Other Acqui-Hire Story (9:30-10:00):** brief,
  explicit comparison — "We covered a different acquisition on this channel where the acquirer
  bought a beloved product and just let it quietly die with nothing left behind [reference package
  046, Mailbox]. This is the version where the acquirer did the opposite." Visual: side-by-side
  card, Mailbox (gone entirely) vs. Parse Server (thriving today).
- Mid-video CTA (~9:45): "This channel is usually a graveyard tour. Every once in a while, we find
  one that actually ends well. Subscribe for the next one — good or bad."

### The Actual Lesson (10:00-11:30)
- Plain-language comparison: most companies treat a shutdown as something to minimize and hide from
  users. Facebook treated it as something to hand off responsibly — real advance notice, and a real,
  usable exit that didn't require Facebook's continued involvement at all.
- The core lesson, stated directly: a company dying and an idea dying are two different events —
  and how an acquirer handles the gap between those two events determines whether anything survives
  the transition.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founded 2011 → YC S11 → Facebook acquisition Apr 2013 ($85M)
  → shutdown announced Jan 2016 (1 year notice) → Parse Server open-sourced same day → Parse.com
  shuts down on schedule Jan 28 2017 → Parse Server still actively maintained, 21,000+ stars, 2026.

### Final CTA + Outro (11:30-12:45)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — the company and the idea are not the same thing, and how you
  handle the end of one determines whether the other survives it
- "Subscribe — most of what's on this channel is a cautionary tale. This is the exception, and I
  think it's worth understanding exactly as much as the failures are."
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever build on Parse, or migrate to Parse Server when it shut down?
  Tell me in the comments — I want to hear from people who actually lived through this one."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the 2026 GitHub activity numbers and the full-arc timeline, then works backward
through the founding, acquisition, and shutdown as supporting detail. Measured, graphics-forward,
treats the video as a genuine case study in what a responsible acquired-platform wind-down looks
like structurally.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the January 2016 shutdown announcement itself — the notice, the year-long countdown —
before cutting hard to 2026's GitHub activity, then rewinding to fill in the founding and
acquisition. Slower pacing, holds the "still actively maintained today" reveal until the final
third, resolving on the Tikhon Bernstam/Scribd cross-reference as the closing beat.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one wind-down, nine years of consequences" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
YC batch, acquisition, three good years, shutdown announcement, open-source release, shutdown on
schedule, present-day GitHub stats — landing on the "company vs. code" contrast as the final,
clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the cold-open GitHub-stats reveal
and the "died vs. won" split card.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the founding/three-good-years segment (1:40-4:30) if it feels like a
  standard company-history recap without enough forward momentum toward the actual hook
- **Fix:** keep explicit on-screen countdown language ("this is still four years before the part
  you're actually here for") to maintain the open loop from the cold open
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general mobile-BaaS market context if audience familiarity
  with the category is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the exact 2026 GitHub star count even more explicitly
  in the cold open if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 8 (7:30)
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "years since shutdown" counter visible from
  Segment 5 onward, ticking upward toward "9 YEARS" as the timeline advances to present day
