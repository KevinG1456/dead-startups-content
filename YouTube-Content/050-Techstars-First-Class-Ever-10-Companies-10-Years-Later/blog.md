# Blog Article: I Tracked Down Every Company From Techstars' First Class Ever. Half of Them, Nobody Can Even Confirm.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch's contemporaneous 2007-2008 coverage, Techstars co-founder Brad
Feld's own 2010 aggregate data post, CBInsights, and Crunchbase — see `sources-log.txt` for the full
citation map. This article does not force a clean failure-rate ratio; it presents the honest,
three-way split in outcomes exactly as the research supports it.*

## Introduction
Techstars is one of the biggest startup accelerators in the world today. In the summer of 2007, it
was 10 unknown companies in Boulder, Colorado — the very first class the program ever ran, founded by
David Cohen. This article tracks down what actually happened to every one of them, and is honest
about the parts that genuinely couldn't be confirmed.

## The Batch
10 companies went through the program: EventVue, Intense Debate, Socialthing!, J-Squared Media,
search-to-phone, Villij, Filtrbox, KBLabs, Brightkite, and madKast. Each gave up roughly 5-6% equity
for a small amount of seed capital (commonly cited around $15-18K per team) plus mentorship and
office space, culminating in an August 17, 2007 demo day.

## The Four Confirmed, Clean Outcomes

**EventVue** built private social networks for conference attendees. It raised roughly $500K from
angel investors after Techstars and shut down in 2010. Co-founder Rob Johnson gave a real, on-record
explanation: "We were not seeing enough customers or growth to justify our market or product." The
team published a detailed public post-mortem — later cited in Techstars' own book, "Do More Faster,"
as an example of building a "vitamin" instead of a "painkiller."

**Intense Debate** — a blog-commenting widget with threading, moderation, and reputation features —
was acquired by Automattic, WordPress's parent company, in 2008. This is the batch's clearest
survivor: as of 2025-2026, it's still actively maintained, distributed as a WordPress plugin,
described in its own current materials as "in maintenance mode" but genuinely alive. It has now
outlived its own founding company's involvement by roughly 18 years.

**Socialthing!** built a dashboard for managing a user's blogs, photos, and social activity in one
place. AOL acquired it in August 2008. It was rebranded "AOL Lifestream" in 2010, and finally shut
down on February 24, 2017 — an acquire-then-discontinue story, but nearly 8.5 years later, a much
longer arc than this channel's typical acquire-then-kill pattern.

**Brightkite**, an early location-based social network, merged with mobile social network Limbo in
April 2009. By December 2011, its apps were pulled from every app store and its site replaced with
"This is not goodbye... we're just moving on to something better." By April 2012, it was gone.

## The Complicated One

**Villij** was an early social-graph platform. The original company was, by its own team's later
account, massively underfunded — staff shrank to almost nothing, and the founders moved on to
separately build other startups. There's no clean shutdown announcement. Then in October 2012, a
completely different, unrelated company bought the Villij name and rebranded itself as Villij. The
name technically still exists; the company that built it doesn't.

## The Five Genuinely Unconfirmed Outcomes

**Filtrbox**, a social-media monitoring platform, was acquired by Jive Software in January 2010, with
its technology "absorbed into Jive's platform." **madKast**, a blog-syndication widget and reportedly
the first company from this class to publicly launch (weeks before the group demo day), was acquired
by ShareThis in September 2008. In both cases, no source found in this research confirms whether the
product continued to exist as a distinct, standalone thing afterward, or was fully folded away.

**J-Squared Media** built "Sticky Notes," an early viral Facebook app that hit 1.7 million users
within six weeks. **search-to-phone** was a voice-enabled Yellow Pages-style search service. Neither
has a shutdown announcement, an acquisition, or any evidence of continued operation in any source
found.

**KBLabs** is the weakest-sourced entry in the entire roster — appearing in secondary rosters of this
cohort, but with no primary source found that even confirms the basics of what it built.

## The Actual Finding

This channel already did a full audit of a real startup batch — Y Combinator's Summer 2006 class, 11
companies, every single outcome confirmable with real confidence. This batch is different, and that
difference is itself the finding: Techstars' first class is smaller, older, and was covered a lot
less at the time it happened. Forcing a clean "X of 10 gone" number here would mean guessing at
outcomes the public record simply doesn't support.

The honest split: 4 confirmed clean, 1 complicated but reasonably clear, 5 genuinely unconfirmed. That
split is the real lesson — "just go check what happened to a startup" is easy for a famous batch with
a famous name attached. For a real, smaller, once-covered batch that's just old enough, half the
trail can genuinely run out. That's not a research failure. It's what actually happens to most
startup history that isn't attached to a famous name.

## What Founders and Researchers Should Take From This
- **Acquisition doesn't always mean either "success" or "death"** — Intense Debate and Socialthing!
  were both acquired, and their outcomes over the following two decades were completely different
- **"Absorbed into the acquirer's platform" is not the same as confirmed survival or confirmed death**
  — Filtrbox and madKast show how easily that distinction gets lost to time
- **A company's name can outlive the company itself** — Villij's brand exists today attached to an
  entirely different business
- **Real research sometimes runs out of trail, and that's worth saying plainly** — presuming a
  shutdown just because no other evidence exists isn't the same as confirming one
- **The oldest, smallest, least-famous startup batches are the hardest ones to honestly audit** — a
  real methodological finding, not just a caveat

## The Lesson
Most "what happened to this startup batch" content picks a number and moves on. The more honest
version of this story is that the record itself has real gaps — and that a genuine audit means
showing exactly where those gaps are, not filling them with a guess.
