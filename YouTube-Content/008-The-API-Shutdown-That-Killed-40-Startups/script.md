# Script Package

Anchored on two real cases — Meerkat (Twitter) and Viddy (Facebook) — full citations in
`sources-log.txt`. Keep the mechanism distinction precise throughout: Twitter's action against
Meerkat was a direct, targeted access cutoff; Facebook's action that hurt Viddy was a broad
anti-spam algorithm change that hit Viddy as a side effect, not a decision aimed at Viddy
specifically. Present Ben Rubin's own account of why Meerkat really declined as his stated view,
explicitly flagged as single-sourced, not as a resolved fact. Do not use "40 startups" or any
other unsourced casualty count anywhere in this script.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 13–16 minutes

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "On March 13th, 2015, Twitter cut off a startup's access to its platform. That same day, Twitter announced it was buying that startup's biggest rival."
- Visual: date stamp "MARCH 13, 2015" animates in, split-screen forming — Meerkat logo silhouette fading on one side, Periscope acquisition headline forming on the other
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This is a true story. And it's only half of it."

### Hook (0:15–0:55)
- VO: "This is Meerkat — and it's one half of a story about two funded startups killed by two different platforms, in two completely different ways. One platform cut its target off on purpose. The other one didn't even know it was doing it. Both startups are gone."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand the two real failure modes of building on top of someone else's platform, and — because this is a forensic channel, not a nostalgia channel — you'll hear what Meerkat's own founder says really killed it, which isn't quite the story everyone remembers."

### Introduction (0:55–1:45)
- Method statement: reconstructed from contemporaneous tech press (TechCrunch, VentureBeat, BuzzFeed News, Engadget, CNN Money, Los Angeles Business Journal, Recode, Variety) and the founder's own later account — sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this isn't "platforms are evil," it's "here are the two different mechanisms by which platform dependency actually kills a company, and how to tell them apart"

### Case One — Meerkat: On Purpose (1:45–6:30)
- Segment 1 — The Breakout (1:45–3:00): March 2015, SXSW. Meerkat, a live-video-streaming app from Life On Air (founder/CEO Ben Rubin), becomes the buzziest app at the festival, spreading almost entirely through Twitter — users found each other through Meerkat's access to Twitter's social graph. Visual: SXSW footage-style B-roll, Twitter follower-count graphic climbing fast.
- Segment 2 — The Cutoff (3:00–4:30): Friday, March 13, 2015 — same day as the SXSW kickoff — Twitter cuts off Meerkat's access to its social graph, breaking the exact feature that had made Meerkat's growth possible. The same day, Twitter announces it's acquiring Periscope, a nearly-identical livestreaming app built in-house at Twitter. Visual: side-by-side date stamp, "graph access" icon breaking, Periscope acquisition headline.
- Segment 3 — The Raise That Happened Anyway (4:30–5:30): two weeks later, Meerkat closes a $14M round led by Greylock Partners — investors funding the company despite (or because of) the public drama, not before it. Visual: funding announcement graphic, clearly dated after the cutoff.
- Segment 4 — The Founder's Own Account (5:30–6:30): Meerkat operated for another 18 months before shutting down in October 2016 and pivoting the team to a new product, Houseparty. But Ben Rubin later told TechCrunch the pivot decision came about six months after launch, and that the real reason was that live broadcasting itself never became "a daily habit" for users — not primarily Twitter's cutoff. On-screen lower third: "Rubin's own account — not independently confirmed by a second source." Visual: quote card, timeline continuing past the cutoff to the 2016 shutdown.

### Case Two — Viddy: By Accident (6:30–11:00)
- Segment 1 — The Rocket Ship (6:30–8:00): Viddy, a video-sharing app, grows to 27M+ members by riding Facebook's frictionless-sharing feature — every video posted to Viddy could auto-post to a user's Facebook Timeline. May 2012: Viddy closes a $30M Series B (NEA, Goldman Sachs, Khosla Ventures, Battery Ventures) at a $370M valuation. Visual: funding graphic, member-count climbing.
- Segment 2 — The Rule Nobody Aimed At Viddy (8:00–9:30): May 31, 2012 — Facebook rolls out a "10-second rule," a broad crackdown on auto-sharing spam that reduces the News Feed visibility of automated posts across many apps, Viddy included. This wasn't Facebook revoking Viddy's access — it's a policy change aimed at the whole ecosystem. On-screen lower third: "Algorithm change — not an API shutdown. Facebook did not target Viddy specifically." Visual: News Feed visibility graphic dropping.
- Segment 3 — The Collapse (9:30–10:30): Viddy's daily active users fall from roughly 5 million in late April 2012 to roughly 1 million by the end of May 2012 — more than an 80% drop in about a month. February 2013: Viddy lays off 12 people, more than a third of its staff. Visual: DAU graph cratering.
- Segment 4 — The Fire Sale (10:30–11:00): January 2014, rebranded as "Supernova," Viddy is acquired by Fullscreen for $20M — down from its $370M valuation 20 months earlier. It fully shuts down in December 2014. Visual: valuation-to-sale-price contrast graphic ($370M → $20M).
- Mid-video CTA (~10:00): "If you're building on any platform's API or feed algorithm right now, the next part is exactly what you need. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson — Two Failure Modes (11:00–13:00)
- Plain-language comparison: Meerkat is a deliberate-access-cutoff story — a platform decided, specifically, to break a specific feature for a specific competitor to its own product. Viddy is an incidental-collateral-damage story — a platform changed a general rule for reasons that had nothing to do with Viddy, and Viddy simply couldn't survive the side effect.
- The practical distinction for builders: you can sometimes negotiate around or anticipate a deliberate cutoff (competitive dynamics are at least legible), but there's no negotiating with a policy change nobody aimed at you — the only real defense is not being so dependent on a single platform's rules that a policy change elsewhere can end your company as a side effect.
- Visual: the two-column "Deliberate / Incidental" comparison graphic, building beat by beat

### Final CTA + Outro (13:00–14:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — platform dependency risk isn't one thing, it's at least two different things, and the incidental kind is arguably scarier because there's no decision to see coming
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you use Meerkat at SXSW, or remember Viddy back when it was 'the Instagram for video'? Which platform failure mode scares you more as a builder — the deliberate one or the incidental one? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: two related autopsies (Title 003 — Google platform risk, most recent autopsy) + subscribe

Opens on the SXSW 2015 scene — the noise, the buzz, Meerkat as the app everyone's talking about — before revealing what happened three days later. Slower pacing, more narrative-driven, treats both companies' stories as human moments first, mechanism second.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "2 ways a platform can end your startup — and only one of them you can see coming," each illustrated with one company's story. Strong Shorts-extraction potential.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the transition between Case One and Case Two (6:30) if it feels like starting over rather than continuing one argument
- **Fix:** open Case Two with an explicit callback line ("If Meerkat is what a platform looks like when it's trying to kill you, Viddy is what one looks like when it isn't trying at all") so the pivot reads as the video's actual thesis unfolding, not a second unrelated story
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim the SXSW breakout B-roll in Segment 1 of Case One if retention data shows the cold open already established enough context
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease Ben Rubin's contradicting account early (already done in the Hook) and pay it off fully in Segment 4 of Case One
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the "Deliberate / Incidental" two-column framework visible in a corner graphic throughout both case sections, building as each fact lands, so viewers always know which column they're in
