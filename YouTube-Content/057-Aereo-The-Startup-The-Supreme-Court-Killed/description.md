## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Aereo —
the only company on this channel killed not by money, fraud, or a bad deal term, but by a 6-3 Supreme
Court vote.

Aereo was founded in February 2012 by engineer Chet Kanojia, with IAC chairman Barry Diller leading a
$20.5 million Series A and joining the board. Its product let subscribers stream local over-the-air
broadcast TV over the internet for about $8-12/month — no cable subscription required — using
thousands of individual, dime-sized remote antennas, one assigned per subscriber. That design wasn't
an engineering afterthought: it was built directly off the Second Circuit's 2008 ruling in Cartoon
Network v. CSC Holdings (the "Cablevision" case), which had held that a unique, individually-stored
copy per subscriber counted as a private performance, not a public one, under copyright law.

Major broadcasters — ABC, CBS, NBC, Fox — sued anyway. Aereo won at the district court level, then won
again at the Second Circuit, which denied broadcasters a preliminary injunction, citing the
Cablevision precedent directly. By early 2014, Aereo had raised roughly $97 million total.

On June 25, 2014, the Supreme Court reversed, 6-3, in American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. v. Aereo,
Inc. Justice Breyer's majority opinion — joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy,
Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan — held that Aereo's near-live retransmissions counted as public
performances because it functioned like a cable system. Justice Scalia dissented, joined by Justices
Thomas and Alito.

Aereo suspended service three days later. It tried one final legal maneuver — arguing that if it acted
like a cable system, it should get a cable system's compulsory license — and lost that too, in October
2014, when Judge Alison Nathan called the argument a "fallacy." Aereo filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in
November 2014. In March 2015, its remaining assets — trademarks and customer lists to TiVo for $1
million, its patent portfolio to RPX Corp for $225,000, equipment for roughly $300,000 — sold for less
than $2 million combined, against $97 million raised.

Coda: founder Chet Kanojia went on to found Starry, a fixed-wireless broadband company, the same year
Aereo collapsed. Starry went public via SPAC in 2022 at a $1.6 billion valuation — then, just over a
year later, filed its own Chapter 11 bankruptcy in February 2023. Unlike Aereo, Starry survived,
emerging restructured in August 2023, with Kanojia stepping down as CEO to join the board.

This channel has covered startups killed by money, fraud, and bad deal terms. This is the first one
killed by a courtroom.
