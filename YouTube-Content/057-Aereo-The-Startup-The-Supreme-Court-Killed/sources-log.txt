Sources Log — Package 057: Aereo
Filled in: 2026-08-16

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Aereo is a genuinely new failure mechanism for this channel: it wasn't killed by running out of money,
fraud, an API cutoff, or a bad deal term — it was killed by a 6-3 U.S. Supreme Court ruling, after
winning at every lower court. This is a legal/regulatory-risk story, not a mismanagement or fraud
story. No individual is accused of wrongdoing anywhere in this package.

THE PRODUCT AND THE LEGAL THEORY — precisely sourced
- Aereo's product: thousands of dime-sized individual remote antennas, one assigned per subscriber,
  capturing over-the-air broadcast television signals and storing an individual copy for that specific
  subscriber, streamed to them over the internet for roughly $8-12/month — no cable subscription
  required.
- This architecture was a direct, deliberate application of the Second Circuit's 2008 ruling in
  *Cartoon Network, LP v. CSC Holdings, Inc.* (commonly called the "Cablevision" case), which held that
  Cablevision's remote-storage DVR ("RS-DVR") did not constitute a "public performance" under copyright
  law because each subscriber's recording was a unique, individually-created and individually-accessed
  copy — a private performance, not a public one. Aereo's individual-antenna-per-subscriber design was
  built specifically to fit within this precedent.
- Aereo cited the Cablevision precedent directly in its own legal filings and won at the district court
  level and at the Second Circuit (which denied broadcasters' request for a preliminary injunction,
  consistent with Cablevision).
Source: Wikipedia ("Cartoon Network, LP v. CSC Holdings, Inc."; "Aereo"); TechCrunch (Oct 22, 2012,
"Streaming TV Startup Aereo Files Appeal In Network Case, Cites Cablevision Precedent"); Mass Law Blog
("Aereo, Antenna Farms and Copyright Law"); The Media Institute ("WNET v. Aereo: The Second Circuit
Persists in Poor (Cable)Vision").

THE LAWSUIT AND THE SUPREME COURT RULING — precisely sourced
- Major broadcasters — including ABC, CBS, NBC, and Fox — sued Aereo for copyright infringement,
  arguing its retransmissions constituted unauthorized public performances of their copyrighted
  broadcasts.
- Case: *American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. v. Aereo, Inc.*, 573 U.S. 431 (2014).
- Decided June 25, 2014, 6-3, in an opinion written by Justice Stephen Breyer. Majority: Breyer, joined
  by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan. Dissent: Justice
  Scalia, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito.
- Holding: Aereo's near-live retransmissions of copyrighted broadcast programming constituted "public
  performances" under the Transmit Clause of the Copyright Act. The majority analogized Aereo to
  community antenna television (CATV) systems — the precursor to modern cable — which Congress
  explicitly targeted when it rewrote the Copyright Act in 1976 to overrule two earlier Supreme Court
  decisions that had held CATV systems did not "perform" copyrighted works. The individual-antenna
  architecture, in the majority's view, did not meaningfully distinguish Aereo from a cable system for
  this purpose.
- Scalia's dissent argued Aereo should not be treated as a direct infringer under a "volitional
  conduct" copyright framework, since subscribers — not Aereo — initiated each individual transmission.
Source: SCOTUSblog case page ("American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. v. Aereo, Inc. (13-461)"); Justia
U.S. Supreme Court Center (573 U.S. 431 (2014)); Loeb & Loeb LLP client alert (July 2014); Ropes & Gray
LLP client alert (July 2014); Faegre Drinker client alert (June 2014); Wiley Rein LLP client alert.

THE AFTERMATH — precisely sourced
- Aereo suspended service on June 28, 2014 — three days after the ruling.
- Aereo then attempted one final legal maneuver on remand at the district court: arguing that, since
  the Supreme Court had compared it to a cable system for purposes of finding a public performance, it
  should now be entitled to a cable system's Section 111 compulsory license. Judge Alison Nathan
  (S.D.N.Y.) rejected this argument in October 2014, writing that it "suffers from the fallacy that
  simply because an entity performs copyrighted works in a way similar to cable systems it must then be
  deemed a cable system for all other purposes." Nathan granted broadcasters a nationwide preliminary
  injunction against Aereo at the same time.
- November 21, 2014: Aereo filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.
- March 2015: a bankruptcy court approved the sale of Aereo's remaining assets. TiVo acquired Aereo's
  trademarks and customer lists for $1 million. Patent aggregator RPX Corp bought Aereo's patent
  portfolio for $225,000. Alliance Technology Solutions was expected to buy Aereo's physical equipment
  for roughly $300,000. Total proceeds across all sales came to less than $2 million — both Aereo and an
  attorney for its unsecured creditors publicly called the result "disappointing," against roughly $97
  million raised over the company's life.
Source: NBC News (Oct 2014, "Federal Court Shuts Down Aereo's Final Argument to Save Itself"); Deadline
(Oct 2014, "Aereo Curb Stomped By Broadcasters As Court Grants Nationwide Injunction Against Shuttered
Streaming Service"); CNBC (Nov 21, 2014, "TV streaming service Aereo files for bankruptcy"); TechCrunch
(Mar 13, 2015, "TiVo Receives Approval To Acquire Aereo Assets"); Variety (Mar 2015, "TiVo Completes
Acquisition of Aereo Trademarks, Customer Lists for $1 Million"); Phys.org / Zacks.com (Mar 2015,
bankruptcy court approval coverage).

THE CODA — CHET KANOJIA'S SECOND COMPANY, STARRY — precisely sourced, handle carefully
- Chet Kanojia founded Starry (fixed-wireless broadband internet, using millimeter-wave technology
  instead of cable/fiber) in 2014, the same year Aereo collapsed.
- Starry went public via SPAC merger in 2022 at approximately a $1.6 billion valuation, with $452
  million in cash on the balance sheet at close.
- February 21, 2023 — just over a year after the SPAC merger — Starry Group Holdings filed for Chapter
  11 bankruptcy in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, entering a Restructuring
  Support Agreement with its lenders. The bankruptcy petition showed roughly $310 million in total debt
  against $270 million in assets. Lenders offered $43 million in debtor-in-possession financing to keep
  the company operating through the process.
- August 2023: Starry emerged from Chapter 11, restructured as a closely-held private company,
  refocused on its core markets (Washington D.C., Boston, New York City, Denver, Los Angeles).
- As part of the bankruptcy exit, Kanojia stepped down as CEO — a role he had held since Starry's
  founding — and moved to the company's board of directors. Co-founder and former COO Alex
  Moulle-Berteaux became CEO.
- *** CRITICAL FRAMING NOTE: Starry did NOT shut down. It survived Chapter 11 as a reorganized, ongoing
  company. This is accurately described as "the same founder's second company also collapsed into a
  serious, real bankruptcy" — a genuine second brush with company-ending financial failure — but NOT as
  "his second startup also died." Never state or imply that Starry shut down or ceased operating; as of
  the most recent reporting found in this research pass (2023 emergence coverage), it continues to
  operate under new CEO leadership. ***
