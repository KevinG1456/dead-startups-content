# Blog Article: This Startup Won Every Court Case. Then It Lost the One That Mattered.

*All figures sourced to SCOTUSblog, Justia, CNBC, TechCrunch, Variety, GlobeNewswire, Crain's New
York Business, and law-firm client alerts covering the ruling directly — see `sources-log.txt` for the
full citation map. This is a legal/regulatory-risk story, not a fraud or mismanagement story — no
individual is accused of any wrongdoing. Starry's 2023 bankruptcy is stated precisely throughout: it
survived Chapter 11 as a restructured company, it did not shut down.*

## Introduction
Every startup-failure story on this channel so far has had a familiar shape: money ran out, a fraud
was uncovered, a platform pulled access, a deal term was exploited. Aereo is different. It's the only
company in this channel's catalog that built a real, working product on a real, previously-winning
legal theory, litigated that theory all the way to the U.S. Supreme Court in good faith — and lost.

## The Idea
Aereo was founded in February 2012 by engineer Chet Kanojia, with IAC chairman Barry Diller leading a
$20.5 million Series A round and joining the company's board. The product: thousands of individual,
dime-sized remote antennas, one assigned per subscriber, each capturing local over-the-air broadcast
television signals and storing a personal copy for that specific subscriber, streamed to them over the
internet for roughly $8 to $12 a month — no cable subscription required.

This wasn't a gimmick dreamed up to dodge the law. It was a deliberate application of a real, then-
controlling legal precedent: the Second Circuit's 2008 ruling in Cartoon Network v. CSC Holdings (the
"Cablevision" case), which held that Cablevision's remote-storage DVR did not constitute a "public
performance" under copyright law, because each subscriber's recording was a unique, individually
created and individually accessed copy — a private performance, not a public one. Aereo's engineers
built the one-antenna-per-subscriber architecture specifically to fit within that precedent.

## Sued Anyway — and Winning
Major broadcasters, including ABC, CBS, NBC, and Fox, sued Aereo for copyright infringement almost
immediately. And, initially, Aereo's legal strategy worked exactly as designed: it won at the district
court level, then won again at the Second Circuit, which denied the broadcasters' request for a
preliminary injunction, citing the Cablevision precedent directly. By early 2014, having raised roughly
$97 million total, Aereo had a real, functioning product and a real, unbroken legal win streak behind
it.

## The Supreme Court
The case reached the Supreme Court as American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. v. Aereo, Inc. On June 25,
2014, the Court reversed, 6-3. Justice Stephen Breyer wrote the majority opinion, joined by Chief
Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Sotomayor, and Kagan. The majority held that Aereo's
near-live retransmissions of copyrighted broadcasts counted as "public performances" under the
Transmit Clause of the Copyright Act — reasoning that Aereo functioned like a community antenna
television (CATV) system, the exact kind of entity Congress had targeted when it rewrote the Copyright
Act in 1976 specifically to bring cable-like retransmission services within the law's reach. Justice
Scalia dissented, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito, arguing Aereo shouldn't be treated as a direct
infringer since subscribers, not Aereo, initiated each individual transmission.

It's worth sitting with the vote count: 6-3, not unanimous. Three of the nine justices who mattered
most agreed with Aereo's position. It wasn't enough.

## The End
Aereo suspended service three days later, on June 28, 2014. It tried one final legal maneuver on
remand: if the Supreme Court said Aereo performed like a cable system, then Aereo argued it should be
entitled to a cable system's compulsory license under Section 111 of the Copyright Act. Judge Alison
Nathan of the Southern District of New York rejected the argument in October 2014, writing that it
"suffers from the fallacy that simply because an entity performs copyrighted works in a way similar to
cable systems it must then be deemed a cable system for all other purposes." She granted broadcasters a
nationwide preliminary injunction at the same time.

On November 21, 2014, Aereo filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy. In March 2015, a bankruptcy court approved
the sale of its remaining assets: trademarks and customer lists to TiVo for $1 million, its patent
portfolio to patent aggregator RPX Corp for $225,000, and its physical equipment to Alliance Technology
Solutions for roughly $300,000. Total proceeds across every sale came to less than $2 million — against
approximately $97 million raised. Both Aereo and an attorney for its unsecured creditors publicly
called the result "disappointing."

## The Coda
Chet Kanojia didn't stop building. In 2014, the same year Aereo collapsed, he founded Starry, a
fixed-wireless broadband internet company using millimeter-wave technology instead of cable or fiber.
Starry went public via SPAC merger in 2022 at a roughly $1.6 billion valuation, with $452 million in
cash at close. Just over a year later, on February 21, 2023, Starry Group Holdings filed its own
Chapter 11 bankruptcy — around $310 million in debt against $270 million in assets.

Unlike Aereo, Starry survived. It emerged from bankruptcy in August 2023, restructured as a privately
held company refocused on its core markets. As part of the restructuring, Kanojia stepped down as CEO
— a role he'd held since founding the company — and moved to Starry's board; co-founder and former COO
Alex Moulle-Berteaux became CEO.

## The Actual Lesson
Aereo complicates a comforting assumption a lot of founders and operators make: that if you have a
sound legal strategy, backed by a real, controlling precedent, and you keep winning as you litigate it,
you're on solid ground. That assumption holds — right up until the final court disagrees, and there's
no round after the Supreme Court. The same precedent that let Aereo win twice in lower courts was the
exact thing the Supreme Court decided didn't apply once the stakes were highest.

This channel has told stories about startups killed by running out of money, by fraud, by a single
toxic contract clause, by a platform pulling API access. Aereo is a genuinely different mechanism: a
company that did nearly everything "right" by conventional startup standards — real product, real
legal counsel, real litigation wins — and still lost everything on a close vote it had no ability to
appeal further.

## What Founders and Operators Should Take From This
- **A legal strategy built on a real, controlling precedent is still not a guarantee** — precedents
  can be distinguished, reinterpreted, or reversed by a higher court, no matter how solid they look at
  the district court level
- **Winning at every lower court isn't the same as winning** — Aereo's two-court win streak meant
  nothing once the Supreme Court took the case and ruled the other way
- **There's no next round after the Supreme Court** — unlike a rescue round or a renegotiated deal
  term, a Supreme Court loss offers no further path to reverse the outcome
- **A close vote is still a final vote** — 6-3 ended Aereo just as completely as a unanimous ruling
  would have
- **A founder's second act can carry real risk too** — Kanojia's second company, Starry, faced its own
  serious bankruptcy less than a decade later, a reminder that surviving one collapse doesn't guarantee
  immunity from a second

## The Lesson
Aereo is a precise, real-world illustration of a risk that's genuinely different from every other
mechanism this channel has covered: sometimes a company does everything a founder is told to do —
build a real product, hire real lawyers, win in court — and it's still not enough, because the final
decision belongs to nine people who don't work for the company at all.
