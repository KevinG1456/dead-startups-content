# Script Package

Anchored on Aereo — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS
SCRIPT: state the mechanism precisely — Aereo built a real, deliberate legal strategy on a real,
controlling Second Circuit precedent (the Cablevision RS-DVR case), won at the district court and at
the Second Circuit itself, then lost 6-3 at the Supreme Court. This is not a story about a company
that "got in legal trouble" vaguely — be specific about the win streak, the precedent, and the exact
vote count throughout. No individual — Chet Kanojia, Barry Diller, any Justice — is accused of
wrongdoing anywhere in this story. Keep the tone analytical and a little awestruck, not accusatory.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 12–15 minutes (founding, the legal strategy, the win streak, the Supreme Court
ruling, the failed final argument, the bankruptcy, and the Starry coda all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company won at the district court. Won again at the Court of Appeals. Then lost at the
  Supreme Court — and that was the only loss that mattered. Here's exactly how a single 6-3 vote ended
  a $97 million company."
- Visual: two green checkmarks stacking, hard cut to a large red X stamped beneath them
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a story about running out
  of money. This company was killed by a courtroom."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Aereo — founded in 2012 by engineer Chet Kanojia, backed by IAC chairman Barry Diller,
  letting subscribers stream local broadcast TV over the internet for about $8 to $12 a month, no cable
  subscription required. It raised roughly $97 million. In June 2014, the Supreme Court ruled against
  it, 6-3. Weeks later, it was gone."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand the exact legal mechanism Aereo built its
  entire business on — a real, previously-winning precedent — and exactly how, and why, it still lost."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from SCOTUSblog, Justia, CNBC, TechCrunch, Variety, GlobeNewswire,
  and law-firm client alerts covering the ruling directly — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "No one in this story did anything wrong. Aereo built a
  real product on a real legal theory. The broadcasters had a real copyright claim. The Supreme Court
  made a real, close decision. This is what happens when a company's survival comes down to a single
  vote."

### Main Content — The Idea (1:40–3:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** February 2012, Chet Kanojia founds Aereo; IAC chairman
  Barry Diller leads a $20.5 million Series A and joins the board. Visual: generic startup-office
  establishing shot, no real branding.
- **Segment 2 — The Antenna Trick (2:15–3:30):** Aereo's product — thousands of dime-sized individual
  remote antennas, one per subscriber, each capturing over-the-air broadcast signals and storing a
  personal copy for that one subscriber, streamed over the internet. Visual: small antenna icon
  connecting to a single screen icon.

### Main Content — The Legal Strategy (3:30–5:30)
- **Segment 3 — Borrowing a Precedent (3:30–4:45):** this design wasn't accidental — it was built
  directly off the Second Circuit's 2008 Cablevision ruling, which held that a unique, individually-
  stored copy per subscriber was a private performance, not a public one, under copyright law. On-screen
  framing card: "Aereo didn't invent a loophole. It applied a precedent that had already won in court."
  Visual: document icon with a highlighted line reading "PRIVATE PERFORMANCE — NOT PUBLIC."
- **Segment 4 — Sued Anyway (4:45–5:30):** major broadcasters — ABC, CBS, NBC, Fox — sue Aereo for
  copyright infringement. Visual: network-icon silhouettes converging on a single antenna icon.

### Main Content — The Win Streak (5:30–7:00)
- **Segment 5 — Winning Twice (5:30–7:00):** Aereo wins at the district court level, then wins again
  at the Second Circuit, which denies broadcasters a preliminary injunction — citing the Cablevision
  precedent directly. On-screen scoreboard graphic builds: "DISTRICT COURT: AEREO WINS" → "COURT OF
  APPEALS: AEREO WINS." Visual: two green checkmarks stacking.

### Main Content — The Supreme Court (7:00–9:30)
- **Segment 6 — The Case (7:00–8:00):** *American Broadcasting Companies, Inc. v. Aereo, Inc.* reaches
  the Supreme Court. Visual: generic marble-column government-building silhouette, no real landmark.
- **Segment 7 — The Ruling (8:00–9:30):** June 25, 2014 — the Court rules 6-3 against Aereo. Majority
  opinion by Justice Breyer, joined by Chief Justice Roberts and Justices Kennedy, Ginsburg, Sotomayor,
  and Kagan: Aereo's near-live retransmissions count as public performances, functioning like a cable
  system — the exact kind of entity Congress targeted when it rewrote copyright law in 1976. Dissent by
  Justice Scalia, joined by Justices Thomas and Alito. On-screen framing card, held for a beat: "Three
  justices agreed with Aereo. Six didn't. That's all it took." Visual: large red X stamping down over
  the two-checkmark scoreboard.

### Main Content — The Final Argument and the End (9:30–11:00)
- **Segment 8 — One Last Try (9:30–10:15):** Aereo suspends service three days later, June 28, 2014.
  Then tries one final legal maneuver: if the Supreme Court said it acts like a cable system, it should
  get a cable system's compulsory license. Judge Alison Nathan rejects the argument in October 2014,
  calling it a "fallacy." Visual: document icon, red stamp "REJECTED" overlay.
- **Segment 9 — Bankruptcy and the Fire Sale (10:15–11:00):** November 21, 2014, Aereo files Chapter
  11. In March 2015, its assets — trademarks and customer lists to TiVo for $1 million, its patent
  portfolio to RPX Corp for $225,000, equipment to Alliance Technology Solutions for roughly $300,000 —
  sell for less than $2 million combined. On-screen framing card: "$97 million raised. Under $2 million
  recovered." Visual: dramatic bar-chart drop graphic.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:45): "This channel has covered startups killed by money, by fraud, by bad deal
  terms. This is the first one killed by a courtroom. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Coda (11:00–12:15)
- Chet Kanojia's second company, Starry — fixed-wireless broadband internet, founded 2014. Went public
  via SPAC in 2022 at a $1.6 billion valuation. Just over a year later, February 2023, Starry also filed
  for Chapter 11 bankruptcy — around $310 million in debt against $270 million in assets. On-screen
  framing card, stated precisely: "Unlike Aereo, Starry survived. It emerged from bankruptcy in August
  2023 as a restructured company — Kanojia stepped down as CEO and moved to the board." Visual: a second,
  smaller dip-and-recovery line graphic, distinct from Aereo's flat line-to-zero.

### The Actual Lesson (12:15–13:15)
- Plain-language comparison: a company can do everything right — build a real product, hire real
  lawyers, win at every lower court — and still lose everything if the final, deciding vote goes the
  other way.
- The core irony, stated directly: the same legal precedent that let Aereo win twice in lower courts
  was the exact thing the Supreme Court decided didn't apply once it mattered most.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2012) → funding ($97M) → lawsuit filed → wins at
  district court and Second Circuit → Supreme Court ruling, 6-3 (June 2014) → suspended service (3 days
  later) → failed cable-license argument (Oct 2014) → Chapter 11 (Nov 2014) → asset fire sale (Mar
  2015).

### Final CTA + Outro (13:15–14:15)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a legal strategy can be sound, well-precedented, and still lose
  if the final court disagrees, and there's no round after the Supreme Court
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want to see how legal and regulatory
  risk shows up in other startup stories on this channel, check out package 003 on platform risk"
- Engagement prompt: "Do you think the Supreme Court got this one right? And what do you think it
  actually feels like to win twice and still lose everything on the third try? Tell me in the
  comments."
- End screen: package 003 (Platform Risk) + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed win-streak-then-loss scoreboard and the $97M-to-$2M asset chart, then
works backward into the founding, the legal precedent, and the ruling itself. Measured, graphics-
forward, treats the video as a genuine case study in legal/regulatory risk rather than a company-
history piece.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on June 25, 2014 itself — the moment the ruling comes down — before cutting hard to Aereo
suspending service three days later, then rewinding to tell the full story in order, ending on the
Starry coda as the closing beat. Slower pacing, holds the 6-3 vote reveal until Segment 7, resolving
with the bankruptcy fire sale as the emotional low point before the coda.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "the exact sequence that ended this company," leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
antenna trick, precedent, lawsuit, two wins, Supreme Court loss, failed final argument, bankruptcy,
Starry coda — landing on the "even a real legal strategy has no round after the Supreme Court" lesson
as the final, clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the "won, won, lost"
scoreboard contrast.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the legal-precedent explainer segment (3:30–4:45) if the Cablevision case feels
  like a dry legal tangent before the courtroom drama begins
- **Fix:** frame the precedent explicitly as "the reason Aereo's lawyers thought they'd already won,"
  building anticipation for the Supreme Court reversal rather than treating it as a legal footnote
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general cord-cutting/streaming-market context if audience
  familiarity with the era is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the 6-3 vote count more explicitly in the cold open if
  early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 7 (8:00) — most viewers won't expect the company
  to have already won twice before that point
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running scoreboard graphic visible from Segment 5 onward
  (District Court: win, Court of Appeals: win, Supreme Court: pending), so the eventual reversal lands
  with full visual weight
