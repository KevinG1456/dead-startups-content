# Blog Article: Fast Raised $124M at a $580M Valuation. Its Real Revenue Was $600,000.

*All financial figures sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Personal-history claims discussed below are attributed explicitly to their original reporting throughout this article, primarily one NPR investigation (Feb 17, 2022) — they are presented as reported, not as independently confirmed by this publication. Domm Holland has not been charged or convicted of any crime.*

## Introduction
Fast is one of the starkest examples available of a startup's story outrunning its actual numbers. The company raised $124.5 million, reached a valuation of roughly $580 million, and generated approximately $600,000 in real revenue in its final full year. This article walks through the fully-sourced financial story first, then covers a second, more carefully-attributed thread about the founder's earlier career — clearly marked as reported by a specific outlet, not independently re-verified here.

## The Pitch
Fast was founded in March 2019 in San Francisco by CEO Domm Holland and COO Allison Barr Allen, who had previously led Global Product Operations at Uber. (A third co-founder, Joshua Abulafia, was reportedly pushed out roughly a year in, following a dispute with Holland over finances and direction.) The pitch was straightforward: a one-click checkout button any e-commerce site could add, competing directly with Shopify's Shop Pay, PayPal, and a rival startup, Bolt.

## The Raises
Fast raised a $20 million Series A in March 2020, led by Stripe. Less than a year later, in January 2021, it raised a $102 million Series B — led by Stripe again. That's a payments infrastructure company backing a startup operating in its own core category, twice. Total funding is most commonly reported at $124-124.5 million across contemporaneous US tech press (one later, single-sourced Australian outlet cites a higher $168 million figure that hasn't been independently reconciled — this article uses $124.5 million as the primary, better-sourced total). The Series B valued Fast at roughly $580 million.

## The Real Numbers
Here is the gap the rest of this story hangs on: Fast's actual 2021 revenue was approximately $600,000 — a figure that includes some branded merchandise sales, not purely core checkout transaction volume. Daily revenue was typically under $300,000 in total company-wide transaction volume processed, with some days as low as $2,000 in the metric actually tied to revenue. The company was burning roughly $10 million a month. For direct comparison, rival checkout startup Bolt reportedly generated around $40 million in revenue over the same period — roughly 65 times what Fast made.

## The Spending
Fast reportedly paid The Chainsmokers $1 million for a single performance at a retail industry conference — about 1.7 times the company's entire annual revenue that year. Headcount grew from 337 to 480 employees between October and November 2021, in the final months before the company's financial reality became public. One employee, quoted in reporting on the company's internal culture, described the spending pattern as: "It was like, 'how quickly can we set money on fire?'"

## The Collapse
By early 2022, Fast's hiring had slowed to a 13-month low. On March 29, 2022, The Information published reporting revealing the roughly $600,000 real revenue figure. The next day, March 30, The Information reported Fast was attempting to raise a new round at a lowered valuation of roughly $450 million. On March 31, the company reportedly hired Morgan Stanley to urgently find a buyer. On April 5, 2022 — six days after the lowered-valuation fundraising attempt began — Fast announced it was shutting down entirely. Approximately 450 employees, including roughly 150 engineers, were laid off, most given notice only about an hour before the public announcement. Holland's own statement: "difficult decision to close our doors... trailblazers don't make it all the way to the mountain top." Affirm subsequently extended job offers to about 100 of the laid-off engineers.

## A Second, More Carefully-Sourced Thread
The financial story above is solid and multi-sourced. What follows traces primarily to one investigative report — NPR, published February 17, 2022 — and is presented here explicitly as reported, not independently re-verified by this article.

NPR's investigation reported that Fast's original product prototype was built in 2019 by roughly a dozen engineers in Nigeria, paid approximately $800 a month, who were let go without notice after about three months. The investigation reports Holland told investors he built the prototype himself. Two engineers, quoted anonymously by NPR, described feeling erased from the company's own account of its founding.

Separately, NPR's investigation covered an earlier venture, Tow.com.au, an Australian towing marketplace Holland ran that collapsed in 2018, reportedly owing contractors around $5.7 million AUD. A named creditor, Joe Andriske of Harvey's Towing Services, is quoted on the record: "He made a promise, made a promise. We get a small payment then he disappeared, mate." NPR also reported that Holland threatened to sell the personal data of more than 21,000 platform users before a Queensland court intervened, and that the resulting dispute was settled for under $1 million in 2019. Separately, NPR's reporting on an earlier 2010 matter — the Qant.as domain — uses deliberately careful language: Holland "claimed" to have sold the domain for $1.3 million but never named a buyer. This article uses that same careful framing; a stronger word like "extorted," used by some secondary sources, is not supported by NPR's own primary reporting and is not used here.

**An important balancing detail:** a Fast company spokesperson told NPR that the Tow.com.au history "was revealed to investors at every round," and two investors told NPR they knew about it and didn't consider it disqualifying. A separate, unnamed investor said it would have discouraged them from investing had they known. This is not a simple story of investors being blindsided about the founder's history — at least some of them reportedly knew and proceeded regardless. The more interesting open question, based on available reporting, isn't whether investors knew who they were backing, but whether they adequately verified the specific metrics and revenue claims made about Fast itself.

## How This Differs From This Channel's Other "Faked Metrics" Case
Package 005 on this channel covers HeadSpin, whose founder, Manish Lachwani, pleaded guilty to wire and securities fraud and was sentenced to prison — that case has a court record and DOJ/SEC citations available. Fast's Domm Holland has not been charged with or convicted of any crime. This article and the accompanying video treat that distinction as important: verified financial facts are presented as fact, and personal-history reporting is presented as reporting, not as an accusation of criminality that hasn't been established.

## What Investors and Founders Should Take From This
- **A compelling story and a charismatic pitch can outrun real revenue for longer than seems possible** — Fast operated for roughly two years past its Series A with revenue that never approached a level proportional to its valuation
- **Spending patterns are a diligence signal in their own right** — a $1 million single-event booking against $600,000 in annual revenue is the kind of gap that shows up in expense reports well before it shows up in a headline
- **"The founder disclosed their history" and "the metrics were accurate" are two separate diligence questions** — Fast's case suggests investors may have done reasonably well on the first and poorly on the second
- **Watch daily/weekly revenue figures, not just headline ARR or valuation** — Fast's reported daily revenue figures (sometimes as low as $2,000) would have been a much harder number to spin than an annualized or narrative-driven growth story

## The Lesson
Fast's $580 million valuation and $600,000 in real revenue is one of the largest verified gaps of its kind in recent startup history. The company's collapse, once it started, was extremely fast — six days from a lowered-valuation fundraising attempt to total shutdown. The lesson isn't really about one founder; it's about how long a well-told story can survive without the numbers to back it, and how much verification actually happens — or doesn't — before a check that size gets written.
