WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Pets.com is one of five fresh, famous, cold-researched titles added this session (alongside
Theranos/052, Juicero/053, Jawbone/055, Yik Yak/056) — not drawn from the "dead-saas-30" batch file,

retold stories in the genre, so this log exists specifically to separate the well-sourced facts from
the commonly-repeated-but-imprecise pop-culture version.

CORRECTION TO INITIAL RESEARCH BRIEF
The original research brief for this package assumed co-founders "Greg McLeod and Josh Stevens." Both
names are WRONG. The real founders are Greg McLemore and Eva Woodsmall, who launched the site (as a
spinoff of McLemore's company WebMagic) in November 1998; the company was formally incorporated in
February 1999. Julie Wainwright then purchased the company/joined as CEO in early 1999 — she did not
co-found it. Every file in this package uses McLemore/Woodsmall as founders and Wainwright as the
CEO who built the company into what became famous, never conflating the two roles.
Source: Wikipedia ("Pets.com"); FourWeekMBA ("Eva Woodsmall & the Pets.com $300M Collapse").

FOUNDING AND CEO
- Founded by Greg McLemore and Eva Woodsmall, launched November 1998 as a spinoff of McLemore's
  company WebMagic; incorporated February 1999.
- Julie Wainwright purchased the company (with backing from Hummer Winblad Venture Partners) and
  became CEO in early 1999. Before Pets.com, Wainwright had been CEO of Reel.com (an online video
  retailer, 1997-1999; Reel.com was sold to Hollywood Video in a deal reported at $100M) and, earlier,
  CEO of Berkeley Systems (interactive entertainment/screensaver software). Purdue graduate; began her
  career in brand management at The Clorox Company.
- Redemption-arc note (use in closing beat, same pattern as 030/MoviePass's Stacy Spikes): after
  Pets.com, Wainwright went on to found The RealReal (online marketplace for authenticated pre-owned
  luxury goods) in 2011, and took it public in 2019 — reportedly the 23rd woman in history to found
  and lead a company to its own IPO. [VERIFY]: the specific "23rd woman" figure traces to secondary
  profile/press coverage of Wainwright, not an independently audited count — state it as "reportedly."
Source: Wikipedia ("Julie Wainwright"); BusinessOfFashion profile; HerMoney interview; Wantrepreneur
Show blog post ("Julie Wainwright on Failure, Fear, and Founding a Billion-Dollar Business at 53").

FUNDING — precisely sourced, three pre-IPO rounds
- March 1999: $10.5M from Amazon.com, Hummer Winblad Venture Partners, and Bowman Capital Management.
- June 1999: $50M, same lead investors.
- November 1999: $35M.
- Total pre-IPO venture funding: approximately $95.5M.
- February 2000: IPO raised an additional $82.5M, priced at $11/share, ticker IPET.
- *** [VERIFY]: Amazon's ownership stake is inconsistently reported across sources — one source states
  a 54% stake from the first funding round, another states 30%, another states 50%. Amazon's own 1999
  press release (press.aboutamazon.com) confirms the investment itself but this research pass could
  not fully reconcile the exact percentage; use "Amazon became the largest shareholder, reportedly
  holding as much as half or more of the company before the IPO" rather than committing to one precise
  number, unless a firmer primary filing (e.g. the S-1) is checked before recording. ***
Source: Amazon.com press release ("Amazon.com Announces Investment in Pets.com," March 1999);
FourWeekMBA; MarketRealist ("What Happened to Pets.com?"); Wikipedia.

MARKETING — precisely sourced
- 1999 advertising spend: $11.8M.
- Sock puppet mascot voiced by comedian Michael Ian Black.
- Super Bowl XXXIV, January 30, 2000: aired "Please Don't Go" (sock puppet singing Chicago's "If You
  Leave Me Now"), reported cost approximately $1.2M; ranked #5 on USA Today's Ad Meter that year.
- 1999 Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade: the sock puppet appeared as a 36-foot balloon.
- The puppet was also profiled by People magazine and appeared on Good Morning America.
Source: CampaignLive ("History of advertising: No 147: Pets.com's sock puppet"); NPR ("The growth,
marketing strategy, and demise of Pets.com," Planet Money/The Indicator transcript); Failure.Museum.

THE TRADEMARK DISPUTE — a genuinely fun, real, sourced side-story
- Robert Smigel, creator of "Triumph the Insult Comic Dog" (a Saturday Night Live/Late Night puppet
  character), had representatives send cease-and-desist letters to Pets.com, claiming the sock puppet
  was based on / infringed Triumph.
- Pets.com preemptively sued Smigel in California (rather than wait to be sued in New York), seeking
  $20M in damages, alleging the cease-and-desist letters defamed its mascot.
- Pets.com's suit against Smigel was dismissed roughly a month later.
- *** [VERIFY]: exact filing/dismissal dates not independently pinned down in this research pass —
  state the sequence (letters → Pets.com sues → dismissed about a month later) without citing specific
  calendar dates unless confirmed. ***
Source: Puppet Wiki ("The Pets.com sock puppet"); Wikipedia ("Triumph the Insult Comic Dog").

THE BUSINESS MODEL FLAW — precisely sourced
- Heavy, bulky, low-margin products (pet food, litter, supplies) shipped with free/subsidized
  delivery. Example: a 50-lb bag of dog food shipped for free lost the company $3-4 per order.
- Reported selling merchandise at approximately 27% below cost.
- For every $1 in sales, the company spent roughly $2 on marketing and fulfillment combined.
- Total capital burned before collapse is commonly cited as approximately $300M (this figure recurs
  across secondary retrospectives and should be treated as an aggregate/rounded figure, not a single
  audited number — flag as [VERIFY] if a more precise audited total is needed).
Source: MarketRealist; The Blunder Vault (Substack, "From Mascot to Meltdown: The Pets.com IPO
Disaster"); Silicon Underground ("Why Pets.com failed and became a dotcom joke"); CelebrityNetWorth
("From IPO To Complete Liquidation In 268 Days").

THE SHUTDOWN — precisely dated
- November 4, 2000: the board approved a plan of complete liquidation.
- November 9, 2000: Pets.com ceased online operations.
- Stock price collapsed from its $11 IPO price to approximately $0.19 by the November 7 liquidation
  announcement.
- The widely-cited "268 days from IPO to complete liquidation" figure is a press/retrospective count
  (CelebrityNetWorth headline figure); this research pass did not independently re-derive the exact
  day count from a confirmed IPO calendar date, so it is used here as a SOURCED, ATTRIBUTED figure
  ("reportedly just 268 days") rather than restated as an independently verified exact count. If a
  tighter primary IPO date is confirmed before recording, re-check the day math.
Source: Wikipedia; Failure.Museum; CelebrityNetWorth ("From IPO To Complete Liquidation In 268 Days.
How Pets.com Became The Biggest Disaster Of The Dotcom Bubble").

THE SOCK PUPPET'S AFTERLIFE
- The puppet's rights/likeness reportedly sold for $125,000 out of the bankruptcy proceedings.
- Multiple retrospectives state it went on to appear in advertising for a subprime/bad-credit auto
  lender, with a tagline along the lines of "Everybody deserves a second chance."

Source: 404memoryfound.com ("Why Pets.com Actually Failed"); general secondary retrospectives citing

CATEGORY / CROSS-REFERENCE NOTES
- No fraud, criminal charge, or named-individual misconduct anywhere in this story — pure overhype /
  unit-economics / marketing-outpacing-fundamentals failure, same category as 002 (Quibi) and
  014 (The Freemium Trap). Tone: the definitive, most iconic dot-com-bust story — treat with a mix of
  genuine "how did nobody stop this" incredulity and respect for the fact the core insight (people
  eventually DID buy pet supplies online at scale — see Chewy.com's later multi-billion-dollar success
  with a similar model plus better logistics/margins) was directionally right, just impossibly early
  and impossibly overspent.
- Optional closing beat: Chewy.com (founded 2011, acquired by PetSmart 2017 for $3.35B, later IPO'd)
  proved the pet-e-commerce thesis could work — a "too early" parallel to 038/Webvan→Instacart. This
  is NOT required for the package but is a strong, real, well-known comparison if a future rewrite
  wants to add it; flagged here as a documented lead, not built into this pass's script.
- Julie Wainwright's redemption arc (The RealReal, founded 2011, IPO'd 2019) is the second genuine
  "founder came back and built something real" ending on this channel after 030/MoviePass's Stacy
  Spikes — worth featuring as the closing beat rather than ending on the liquidation.
