# Script Package

Anchored on Pets.com — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Correction: founders are Greg McLemore
and Eva Woodsmall (NOT "Josh Stevens" — a research-brief error); Julie Wainwright joined as CEO in
1999, she did not co-found the company. Never commit to one precise Amazon ownership percentage
(sources range 30%-54%) or one precise IPO calendar date without attribution — the "268 days" figure
is a press retrospective count, cite it as such. Never name a specific auto-lender for the sock
puppet's post-collapse ad career. Tone: no fraud, no misconduct — pure overhype/unit-economics story,
told with genuine affection for how iconic the puppet became.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 13–15 minutes (founding, funding, marketing blitz, the business-model flaw, the
IPO and collapse, the puppet's afterlife, and the Wainwright redemption close)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company had a Super Bowl ad, a parade balloon, and ninety-five million dollars in
  funding. It still lost money every single time someone bought something."
- Visual: quick flash montage — a stylized stage spotlight, a balloon silhouette, a falling stock
  chart — landing on a single "-$4.00" ledger line
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to black, white text: "This isn't a story about a bad idea. It's a
  story about a good idea, priced to lose."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Pets.com — the company behind the most famous mascot of the entire dot-com bubble.
  You know the puppet. Almost nobody remembers the actual math behind it."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how much money it lost on every single
  order, why it sued Saturday Night Live over its own mascot, and what happened to its CEO after the
  company that made her famous completely collapsed."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Amazon's own 1999 investment press release, contemporaneous
  business press, and multiple retrospective case studies — full citations in `sources-log.txt`
- Honesty note, stated plainly: "One correction up front — a lot of retellings of this story get the
  founders wrong. The company was actually started by Greg McLemore and Eva Woodsmall in 1998. Julie
  Wainwright, the CEO everyone remembers, bought the company and took over the following year. Both
  matter to this story, and we're keeping them straight."

### Main Content — The Setup (1:40–4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** launched November 1998 by Greg McLemore and Eva
  Woodsmall as a spinoff of McLemore's company WebMagic; incorporated February 1999. Visual: simple
  "1998" title card with a plain shopping-cart-and-paw icon.
- **Segment 2 — The New CEO (2:15–2:50):** Julie Wainwright buys in and becomes CEO in early 1999 —
  previously ran Reel.com (sold to Hollywood Video for a reported $100M) and, before that, Berkeley
  Systems. Visual: simple resume-style title card.
- **Segment 3 — The Money Comes In (2:50–3:45):** three funding rounds in 1999 — $10.5M in March,
  $50M in June, $35M in November — roughly $95.5M total, led by Amazon.com, Hummer Winblad Venture
  Partners, and Bowman Capital Management. Amazon becomes the largest shareholder, reportedly holding
  as much as half the company or more. Visual: three ascending bar graphic building up.
- **Segment 4 — The Marketing Blitz (3:45–4:30):** $11.8M spent on advertising in 1999 alone — a
  36-foot sock puppet balloon in the 1999 Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, magazine profiles, a Good
  Morning America appearance. Visual: balloon-silhouette graphic rising over a skyline.

### Main Content — The Puppet's Peak (4:30–7:00)
- **Segment 5 — The Super Bowl (4:30–5:30):** January 30, 2000, Super Bowl XXXIV — Pets.com airs
  "Please Don't Go," the sock puppet singing Chicago's "If You Leave Me Now," voiced by comedian
  Michael Ian Black, at a reported cost of roughly $1.2 million. It ranks #5 on USA Today's Ad Meter
  that year. Visual: stage-spotlight graphic.
- **Segment 6 — The Lawsuit Nobody Remembers (5:30–7:00):** the puppet gets so famous, it catches the
  attention of Robert Smigel — creator of Triumph the Insult Comic Dog. His representatives send
  cease-and-desist letters claiming the puppet was based on Triumph. Pets.com's response: sue Smigel
  first, for $20 million, in California. The suit is dismissed about a month later. On-screen framing
  card: "A company that couldn't make money on dog food just sued a comedy writer over a puppet — and
  lost that too." Visual: lighthearted "courtroom sketch" style graphic, two puppet silhouettes facing
  off.

### Main Content — The Math That Killed It (7:00–10:00)
- **Segment 7 — The IPO (7:00–7:45):** February 2000, Pets.com goes public at $11 a share under the
  ticker IPET, raising $82.5 million. Visual: "IPO — $11/SHARE" title card with an opening-bell icon.
- **Segment 8 — The Real Problem (7:45–9:15):** here's the detail the puppet never told you — pet
  supplies are heavy, bulky, and cheap. Shipping a 50-pound bag of dog food for free cost the company
  $3 to $4 every single time. Merchandise was sold at roughly 27% below what it cost to buy. For every
  dollar in sales, the company spent about two dollars on marketing and fulfillment combined. On-
  screen framing card: "$1 in. $2 out. Every single order." Visual: shipping-box graphic, a dollar
  going in, half a dollar coming out.
- **Segment 9 — The Collapse (9:15–10:00):** the stock, which opened at $11, crashes to roughly $0.19
  by the time the November 7, 2000 liquidation announcement hits. The board approves complete
  liquidation November 4; the company ceases operations November 9. Commonly reported: 268 days from
  IPO to complete liquidation, having burned an estimated $300 million. Visual: falling stock-line
  graphic.

### Main Content — The Afterlife (10:00–11:30)
- **Segment 10 — What Happened to the Puppet (10:00–10:45):** the sock puppet's rights sold for
  $125,000 out of the bankruptcy proceedings and reportedly went on to appear in ads for a car-loan
  company, with a tagline about second chances. Visual: puppet-silhouette graphic, fading from
  spotlight to a generic "used car lot" background.
- **Segment 11 — What Happened to the CEO (10:45–11:30):** Julie Wainwright didn't disappear. In
  2011, she founded The RealReal, an online marketplace for pre-owned luxury goods — and in 2019, she
  took it public, reportedly becoming the 23rd woman in history to found and lead a company to its own
  IPO. Mid-video CTA (~11:00): "This channel usually ends on the shutdown. This one keeps going.
  Subscribe for a new autopsy every week." Visual: simple "2011 → 2019 → IPO" timeline card.

### The Actual Lesson (11:30–13:00)
- Plain-language comparison: Pets.com wasn't wrong that people would eventually buy pet supplies
  online — Chewy.com later proved exactly that thesis, at scale, profitably. Pets.com was right about
  the idea and catastrophically wrong about the economics underneath it: heavy, low-margin goods,
  subsidized shipping, and marketing spend that outran the business by years.
- The core lesson, stated directly: a beloved brand and a broken unit economy can exist in the exact
  same company at the exact same time — and the brand will not save you.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — funding rounds → marketing blitz → Super Bowl → IPO → shipping
  math revealed → stock collapse → liquidation → puppet sold → Wainwright's next company.

### Final CTA + Outro (13:00–14:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — the puppet was never the problem, the shipping math was
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this one has maybe the most famous mascot
  of any company on this channel"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you remember the Super Bowl ad? Did you know about the SNL lawsuit? Tell me
  in the comments what surprised you most."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Nostalgia-Forward Style
Opens on the Super Bowl ad and the parade balloon as the emotional spine, treats the shipping-cost
math as the "twist" revealed partway through rather than the framing from the start. Strongest fit
for a broad, general-audience cut aimed at viewers who remember the puppet but not the company.

### Script Version B — Business-Case-Study Style
Opens directly on the funding rounds and the per-order loss math, treating the puppet/marketing
material as illustrative b-roll rather than the narrative spine. Denser on the specific dollar figures.
Strongest fit for a founder/VC-audience-specific cut.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one company, five numbers": $95.5M raised, $11.8M spent on marketing in one year,
$3-4 lost per order, 268 days IPO-to-liquidation, $125,000 the puppet sold for — landing on the
Wainwright redemption beat as the closing twist. Strong Shorts-extraction potential.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the funding-rounds recitation (Segment 3) if presented as a dry list of dollar
  figures without visual pacing
- **Fix:** keep the three rounds visually building on screen (ascending bars) rather than narrated as
  a flat list
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim the Super Bowl segment's runtime if early-retention data shows
  the audience already knows this part well — most viewers recognize the puppet immediately
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the SNL lawsuit explicitly in the cold open if drop-off
  appears before Segment 6 — it's the single freshest beat in the story
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running dollar counter visible from Segment 7 onward
  ("$95.5M raised" → "$82.5M more from IPO" → "$300M burned") so the scale stays visible throughout
