# Blog Article

## Title: The Sock Puppet That Burned $300 Million in 268 Days: What Actually Killed Pets.com

Everyone remembers the sock puppet. It sang on the Super Bowl. It floated 36 feet tall over Manhattan
in the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade. It was profiled in People magazine and appeared on Good Morning
America. For a couple of years around the turn of the millennium, it was arguably more famous than
most of the products the company that made it actually sold.

Almost nobody remembers the math. This is that story.

**A correction, up front.** Some retellings of this story get the founders wrong. Pets.com was
launched in November 1998 by Greg McLemore and Eva Woodsmall, as a spinoff of McLemore's company
WebMagic, and formally incorporated in February 1999. Julie Wainwright — the CEO most people actually
picture when they think of this company — bought in and took over in early 1999. She built the
company into the cultural phenomenon it became, but she didn't found it. Both facts matter to this
story, and this article keeps them straight.

### The money comes in fast

Wainwright had run Reel.com (an online video retailer later sold to Hollywood Video for a reported
$100M) and, before that, Berkeley Systems. She brought in serious capital, fast: $10.5 million in
March 1999, $50 million in June, $35 million in November — roughly $95.5 million across three rounds
in a single year, led by Amazon.com, Hummer Winblad Venture Partners, and Bowman Capital Management.
Amazon became the company's largest shareholder, reportedly holding as much as half the company or
more (the exact percentage varies across sources, ranging from about 30% to 54%).

### The marketing blitz

In 1999 alone, Pets.com spent $11.8 million on advertising. The centerpiece: a Super Bowl XXXIV ad in
January 2000, "Please Don't Go," featuring the sock puppet singing Chicago's "If You Leave Me Now,"
voiced by comedian Michael Ian Black. It reportedly cost around $1.2 million and ranked #5 on USA
Today's Ad Meter that year. A few months earlier, the puppet had appeared as a 36-foot balloon in the
1999 Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade.

### The lawsuit almost nobody remembers

Here's the detail most retellings skip entirely. The puppet got so recognizable that it caught the
attention of Robert Smigel, creator of Triumph the Insult Comic Dog — the cigar-chomping puppet
character from Late Night and Saturday Night Live. Smigel's representatives sent Pets.com cease-and-
desist letters, claiming the sock puppet was based on Triumph.

Pets.com's response was to sue first: the company filed suit against Smigel in California, seeking
$20 million in damages, alleging the letters had defamed its mascot. The lawsuit was dismissed about a
month later.

A company that, as it turns out, couldn't make money on dog food had just spent legal resources suing
a comedy writer over a puppet — and lost that fight too.

### The IPO, and the number that actually explains everything

In February 2000, Pets.com went public at $11 a share under the ticker IPET, raising $82.5 million.

Here's the problem the puppet never told you about. Pet supplies are heavy, bulky, and cheap — a
brutal combination for an e-commerce shipping model. Sending a 50-pound bag of dog food with free
delivery cost the company $3 to $4 in losses per order. Merchandise was reportedly sold at roughly 27%
below what it cost the company to acquire it. Put those together with the marketing spend, and the
company was reportedly burning about two dollars for every one dollar of sales it brought in.

A beloved brand and a broken unit economy can exist in the exact same company at the exact same time.
The brand does not fix the economy.

### The collapse

The stock, which had opened at $11, had crashed to around $0.19 by the time the company announced its
liquidation plan on November 7, 2000. The board formally approved complete liquidation on November 4;
the company ceased online operations on November 9. It's commonly reported — as a press retrospective
figure, not an independently re-derived one — that this was 268 days after the IPO, with an estimated
$300 million burned in total.

### What happened next

The sock puppet's rights sold for $125,000 in the bankruptcy proceedings and reportedly went on to
appear in advertising for a car-loan company, with a tagline about second chances.

Julie Wainwright didn't disappear. In 2011, she founded The RealReal, an online marketplace for
authenticated pre-owned luxury goods. In 2019, she took it public — reportedly becoming the 23rd woman
in history to found and lead a company to its own IPO.

### The actual lesson

It would be easy to file this away as "the dot-com bubble was silly and this was one of the sillier
examples." That undersells what actually happened. The core idea — that people would eventually buy
pet supplies online — wasn't wrong. Chewy.com later built a multi-billion-dollar business on
essentially the same premise, with better logistics and better margins.

What killed Pets.com wasn't the idea, and it wasn't the puppet. It was the gap between how fast the
company could build a beloved brand and how slowly — if ever — it was going to fix a shipping model
that lost money on every single transaction. The puppet made people love the company. It never had a
chance to make the company profitable.
