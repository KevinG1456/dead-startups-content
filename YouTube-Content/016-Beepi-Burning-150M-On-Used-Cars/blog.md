# Blog Article: Beepi — Burning $150M on Used Cars (Marketplaces That Subsidize Both Sides)

## Introduction
Beepi, a used-car marketplace founded by Alejandro Resnik and Owen Savir, raised a reported $150 million to solve a genuine, well-understood problem: buying and selling a used car is a high-friction, low-trust transaction for almost everyone involved. Beepi's solution — in-person inspections, white-glove delivery, money-back guarantees, subsidized for both buyers and sellers — built real customer trust and enthusiasm. It also meant losing money on a large share of every transaction, a structural problem that ultimately ended the company. This is a breakdown of that subsidy economics problem, and what it teaches anyone building a two-sided marketplace.

## The Trust Problem in Used Cars
Used-car transactions carry unusually high friction: buyers worry about hidden mechanical issues and fair pricing, while sellers worry about payment reliability, liability, and the hassle of managing the sale process. Beepi's model addressed both sides directly — professional inspections gave buyers confidence, guaranteed payment and handled logistics gave sellers ease, and money-back guarantees reduced risk for everyone.

## What the Subsidy Actually Cost
`[VERIFY all figures before publishing]` — each of these trust-building services carried a real, direct cost: inspector time and travel, delivery logistics, and the financial exposure of guarantee commitments. Because Beepi absorbed much of this cost on behalf of both buyers and sellers rather than passing it through as a clear, sufficient fee, the company's gross margin per transaction was reportedly thin or negative on a significant share of sales.

## Why This Is a Generalizable Marketplace Problem
Beepi's specific challenge illustrates a broader pattern relevant to any two-sided marketplace: subsidizing both sides of a transaction can be an effective way to build initial trust and liquidity in a new or high-friction category, but it requires a credible, modeled path to reducing that subsidy as the marketplace scales — through network effects, reduced acquisition costs, operational efficiency gains, or a shift in what customers are willing to pay directly. Without that path clearly identified and pursued from early on, subsidy-funded growth simply delays the underlying unit-economics problem rather than solving it.

## The Lesson
Solving a genuine customer problem — even solving it well, with real customer love and engagement — is necessary but not sufficient for a sustainable business. For marketplace founders specifically, the practical lesson is to model the path from "subsidized trust-building" to "sustainable unit economics" explicitly and early, rather than assuming growth alone will eventually resolve the underlying cost structure.
