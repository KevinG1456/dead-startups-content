## Core Facts (do not alter across languages)

- **Company:** Homejoy, on-demand home cleaning marketplace connecting customers with independent
  cleaners.
- **Founders:** Adora Cheung and Aaron Cheung.
- **Accelerator:** went through Y Combinator.
- **Business model:** marketplace connecting customers with independent cleaners (contractors),
  not a vertically-integrated/owned-labor model.
- **Growth strategy:** grew quickly using deep discounts for first-time customers, driving rapid
  new-customer acquisition and word-of-mouth growth.
- **Retention weakness:** reported data suggests a large share of customers who tried Homejoy at a
  steep first-time discount did not convert into regular, full-price repeat customers — a
  business-model vulnerability independent of the lawsuits. Present this honestly alongside the
  legal story; do not oversimplify to a single cause.
- **Funding:** reported total raised roughly `$38-40M` [VERIFY exact figure before final citation]
  — investors reportedly include Google Ventures, among others [VERIFY full investor list before
  publishing]. Use "$40M" as the rounded headline figure in titles/thumbnails (matches the root
  package's established convention), but state "roughly $38-40M" in body prose/descriptions.
- **The classification question:** Homejoy classified its cleaners as independent contractors
  rather than employees. Starting in 2014-2015, at least four lawsuits were filed alleging this
  classification was improper given the degree of control Homejoy reportedly exercised over how
  cleaners performed their work [VERIFY specific case names/details before citing].
- **The shutdown:** Homejoy shut down operations in **July 2015**, reportedly after failing to
  close a new funding round, with leadership publicly citing the four lawsuits as a significant
  factor in why that round fell through.
- **Historical significance:** Homejoy's collapse predates much of the classification litigation
  that later became prominent in cases involving Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, and other gig-economy
  companies — frequently cited as an early, largely overlooked signal of this legal risk.

## The Two-Sided Framing (must survive localization intact)

Homejoy's failure was not purely a legal story or purely a business-model story — both compounded
each other. A discount-driven growth strategy with weak underlying retention made the company more
dependent on continued outside funding, and the legal exposure from the classification lawsuits
made that funding harder to secure at exactly the moment it was needed most. Every language version
must present both factors honestly, not oversell the lawsuits as the sole cause — this audience
will fact-check an oversimplified single-cause narrative.

## The One-Sentence Lesson (must survive localization intact in meaning)

Legal classification risk in a marketplace/gig-economy business model is a structural strategic
risk, not a footnote — it compounds with underlying business-model weaknesses (here, discount-
driven growth with weak retention) and should be evaluated and planned for from the earliest
stages, not treated as an afterthought.
