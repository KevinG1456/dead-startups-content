# Blog Article: Homejoy — How Misclassified Labor Lawsuits Ended a Rocket Ship

## Introduction
Homejoy, an on-demand home cleaning marketplace founded by Adora Cheung and Aaron Cheung, grew quickly after emerging from Y Combinator, raising significant venture funding — reported at roughly $38–40 million `[VERIFY]` — from investors including Google Ventures. In July 2015, the company shut down. Leadership publicly pointed to four lawsuits alleging Homejoy had misclassified its cleaners as independent contractors rather than employees as a major factor in why a new funding round fell through. This is a breakdown of both the legal exposure and the underlying business-model weakness that combined to end the company.

## The Rise
Homejoy's growth strategy leaned heavily on deep discounts for first-time customers — in some cases, cleanings priced far below the standard rate — to drive rapid new-customer acquisition and word-of-mouth growth `[VERIFY specific discount figures before citing]`. This approach generated impressive top-line growth numbers and made Homejoy one of the most closely watched marketplace startups of its era.

## The Retention Problem
Behind the growth numbers, reported data suggested that a large share of customers who tried Homejoy at a steep discount did not convert into regular, full-price repeat customers `[VERIFY specific retention figures before citing]`. This meant the company's growth was, in part, subsidized by unsustainable discounting rather than built on durable unit economics — a vulnerability independent of the lawsuits.

## The Classification Question
Homejoy, like many marketplace and gig-economy companies, classified its cleaners as independent contractors rather than employees. This classification affects who is responsible for benefits, minimum wage guarantees, overtime, and expense reimbursement. Starting in 2014–2015, at least four lawsuits were filed alleging this classification was improper given the degree of control Homejoy reportedly exercised over how cleaners performed their work `[VERIFY specific case details and outcomes before citing]`.

## The Fall
Homejoy was reportedly in the process of trying to close a new funding round when the lawsuits became a significant factor giving prospective investors pause `[VERIFY sourcing on this causal claim directly]`. The company shut down operations in July 2015.

## Why This Case Still Matters
Homejoy's collapse predates much of the classification litigation that later became prominent in cases involving Uber, Lyft, DoorDash, and other gig-economy companies. Its story is frequently cited as an early, largely overlooked signal of a legal risk that would go on to shape — and in many cases significantly cost — the broader gig-economy industry.

## The Lesson
Homejoy's failure wasn't purely a legal story or purely a business-model story — it was both, compounding each other. A discount-driven growth strategy with weak underlying retention made the company more dependent on continued outside funding, and the legal exposure from the classification lawsuits made that funding harder to secure at exactly the moment it was needed most. For founders building marketplace or gig-economy models, the practical lesson is to treat worker classification as a core strategic and legal risk from day one, not an afterthought.
