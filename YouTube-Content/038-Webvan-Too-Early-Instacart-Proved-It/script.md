# Script Package

Anchored on Webvan and its real successor Instacart — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE
MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS SCRIPT: state the real, sourced gap between Webvan's 2001 collapse and
Instacart's 2012 founding accurately (11 years; 16 years from Webvan's 1996 founding) — never force
the batch title's "8 years" framing onto facts that don't support it. Present George Shaheen's
lifetime-compensation contract neutrally as a real business fact, not misconduct. Attribute
"transformed from a Webvan clone" explicitly to business press coverage of Instacart's 2023 IPO, not
as this channel's own claim.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 13–16 minutes (Webvan's rise, its overextension and collapse, and Instacart's real
vindication all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company burned $800 million proving an idea that worked perfectly — for someone else,
  eleven years later. Here's the full story, on both ends."
- Visual: large glowing "$800M LOST" numeral, dotted timeline arrow crossing to "$9.9B IPO" numeral
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "The idea was never the problem. The
  calendar was."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Webvan — founded in 1996 by Louis Borders, co-founder of the Borders bookstore chain.
  Webvan wanted to deliver your groceries within a 30-minute window, using its own automated
  warehouses. It went public in 1999 at a $4.8 billion valuation. It was bankrupt by 2001, having lost
  over $800 million."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll see exactly why Webvan's version of this idea couldn't
  work in 1999 — and you'll see the real, publicly-filed proof that the idea itself was right all
  along, from a completely different company, eleven years later."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, CNN Money, Seattle Times, Forbes, and Bloomberg Law
  — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "This isn't really a story about a bad idea. It's a
  story about the exact infrastructure — smartphones, GPS, a gig-economy labor market — that didn't
  exist yet when Webvan tried this the first time."

### Main Content — The Rise (1:40–5:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** Webvan founded December 1996 by Louis Borders. Business
  model: online grocery delivery within a 30-minute window, using proprietary automated warehouses
  rather than partnering with existing stores. Visual: generic warehouse-under-construction graphic.
- **Segment 2 — Building It Themselves (2:15–3:15):** Webvan placed a $1 billion order with
  construction giant Bechtel to build its warehouse network, and bought its own delivery truck fleet.
  George Shaheen left Andersen Consulting — and a reported $4 million annual salary — to become CEO, on
  a contract reportedly including $375,000 per year for life. Visual: "$1 BILLION — BECHTEL WAREHOUSE
  DEAL" title card.
- **Segment 3 — The IPO (3:15–4:15):** June 1999, Webvan launches service in San Francisco. November 5,
  1999, Webvan IPOs at $15 a share, raising $375 million and valuing the company at $4.8 billion —
  despite roughly $395,000 in cumulative revenue and losses already over $50 million. Shares nearly
  doubled on day one, briefly pushing the market cap toward $8 billion. Visual: "$4.8 BILLION
  VALUATION / $395,000 REVENUE" side-by-side card.
- **Segment 4 — The Overextension (4:15–5:00):** in 2000, Webvan acquires competitor HomeGrocer for
  $1.2 billion in stock. That same year, Webvan reports $178.5 million in sales against $525.4 million
  in expenses. Visual: "$178.5M SALES / $525.4M EXPENSES" side-by-side card.

### Main Content — The Collapse (5:00–7:00)
- **Segment 5 — The Numbers Catch Up (5:00–6:00):** Webvan expanded into ten markets before proving its
  model worked in even one, targeted price-sensitive mainstream customers instead of a more profitable
  premium segment, and built entirely proprietary infrastructure — unlike competitor Peapod, which
  partnered with existing supermarkets instead. Visual: 10-market expansion map graphic.
- **Segment 6 — Bankruptcy (6:00–7:00):** July 9, 2001, Webvan announces it's shutting down and filing
  Chapter 11 — laying off about 2,000 employees and cutting off roughly 750,000 active customers across
  seven markets. Total losses: over $800 million. Visual: "CHAPTER 11 — JULY 9, 2001" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~6:45): "Most versions of this story end here. This one doesn't. Subscribe for a new
  autopsy every week — and stay for the part almost nobody covers."

### Main Content — The Vindication (7:00–11:00)
- **Segment 7 — A Different Company, a Different Model (7:00–8:30):** 2012, Apoorva Mehta — a former
  Amazon supply-chain engineer — founds Instacart. Instead of building warehouses, Instacart uses
  existing grocery stores' own inventory, plus a gig-economy network of independent shoppers — an
  asset-light model made possible by smartphone GPS, mapping, and payments infrastructure that simply
  didn't exist in Webvan's era. Visual: "OWN THE WAREHOUSE" vs. "USE EXISTING STORES" side-by-side
  comparison card.
- **Segment 8 — The Real Proof (8:30–10:00):** Instacart's valuation peaked near $39 billion in March
  2021 during the pandemic grocery-delivery boom, before being cut three times to about $13 billion by
  October 2023. In September 2023, Instacart filed its S-1 and completed a real IPO — pricing at $30 a
  share, a $9.9 billion public-market valuation. Business press covering the IPO explicitly described
  Instacart as having "transformed from a Webvan clone" into the country's largest grocery-delivery
  business. On-screen framing card: "This is the actual, audited, publicly-filed proof that Webvan's
  idea wasn't wrong." Visual: "S-1 FILED — SEPTEMBER 2023" title card.
- **Segment 9 — What Changed (10:00–11:00):** the founder's own exit — Apoorva Mehta stepped down as
  CEO in August 2021 and left the board at the IPO, ending an 11-year tenure, exiting with a reported
  fortune over $1 billion. Visual: "11 YEARS, FOUNDER TO IPO" title card.

### The Actual Lesson (11:00–12:30)
- Plain-language comparison: Webvan and Instacart solved the exact same customer problem. Webvan tried
  to own every piece of the infrastructure itself, in an era before the technology existed to make that
  affordable. Instacart rented the infrastructure that already existed by 2012 — stores, phones,
  independent workers with GPS-equipped devices — and built the software layer on top.
- The core lesson, stated directly: an idea can be completely correct and still fail, if the
  surrounding technology and labor infrastructure it depends on isn't ready yet — and there's no way to
  force that readiness with more capital.
- Visual: dual-timeline graphic, Webvan's arc (1996-2001) and Instacart's arc (2012-2023) shown
  stacked, landing on Instacart's real S-1/IPO as the visual payoff.

### Final CTA + Outro (12:30–13:45)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — being right about the idea doesn't protect you from being wrong
  about the decade
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this is the first one that actually follows
  the idea all the way to its real, public-market vindication"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever use Webvan back in the day? And do you think Instacart truly
  'proved' Webvan's original idea, or is that too generous a read? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

then works through Webvan's founding, collapse, and Instacart's vindication as supporting detail.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Webvan's November 1999 opening bell — the euphoria, the $8 billion day-one market cap — before
cutting hard to the July 2001 bankruptcy announcement, then rewinding to explain what went wrong, and
finally resolving with Instacart's 2023 IPO as the closing payoff. Strongest tonal-irony arc of the
three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one idea, two companies, eleven years apart" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
warehouse deal, IPO, overextension, bankruptcy, Instacart's founding, asset-light model, S-1, IPO —
landing on the timing-risk lesson as the final, clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential,
especially the $4.8B-vs-$395,000 and $800M-vs-$9.9B contrasts.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the transition from Webvan's bankruptcy to Instacart's founding (~7:00) if it
  feels like the video restarting rather than continuing the same thesis
- **Fix:** explicitly frame the transition as "the same idea, a different set of tools" and immediately
  show the infrastructure-comparison graphic, so viewers see the connective tissue instantly
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general dot-com-era context if audience familiarity with the
  period is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the Instacart connection even more explicitly in the cold
  open if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 7 (7:00) — many viewers won't expect this
  video to extend past Webvan's own ending
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "years since Webvan" corner counter visible from
  Segment 7 onward, landing on "11 YEARS" at the S-1 title card
