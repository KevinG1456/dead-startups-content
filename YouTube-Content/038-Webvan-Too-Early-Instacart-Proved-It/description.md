## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Webvan
— and, for the first time on this channel, the real successor company that proved its idea right.

Webvan was founded in December 1996 by Louis Borders, co-founder of the Borders bookstore chain. Its
plan: deliver groceries within a 30-minute window, using its own automated warehouses rather than
partnering with existing stores. Webvan placed a $1 billion order with Bechtel to build that warehouse
network and bought its own delivery fleet. George Shaheen left Andersen Consulting — and a reported $4
million salary — to become CEO, on a contract reportedly including $375,000 a year for life.

On November 5, 1999, Webvan went public at $15 a share, raising $375 million and reaching a $4.8
billion valuation — despite roughly $395,000 in cumulative revenue. In 2000, Webvan acquired
competitor HomeGrocer for $1.2 billion in stock, while reporting $178.5 million in sales against
$525.4 million in expenses for the year. On July 9, 2001, Webvan filed Chapter 11, laying off about
2,000 employees and cutting off service to roughly 750,000 customers. Total losses: over $800 million.

Here's the part almost no Webvan retrospective covers: in 2012, Apoorva Mehta, a former Amazon supply-
chain engineer, founded Instacart — solving the exact same customer problem with a completely
different, asset-light model. Instead of building warehouses, Instacart used existing grocery stores'
own inventory, paired with a gig-economy network of independent shoppers, made possible by smartphone
GPS, mapping, and payments infrastructure that simply didn't exist in Webvan's era.

Instacart's valuation peaked near $39 billion in March 2021, was cut three times to about $13 billion
by late 2023, and then, in September 2023, the company filed a real S-1 and completed its IPO — $30 a
share, a $9.9 billion public-market valuation. Business press covering the IPO explicitly described
Instacart as having "transformed from a Webvan clone" into the largest grocery-delivery business in
the country.

Webvan's idea wasn't wrong. It was eleven years too early.
