# Blog Post (Companion Article)

## Title
He Tried 5 Marketing Channels. All 5 Failed. Here's the Startup That Told the Truth About Why.

## Body

Most startup failure stories get written by someone else, after the fact. A journalist pulls court
filings. A blogger reconstructs a timeline from LinkedIn posts and layoff tweets. The founders,
understandably, tend to go quiet.

Tali didn't follow that pattern.

Founded in February 2017 by three coworkers at a healthcare company — Matthew Volm, Matt
Anthes-Washburn, and Matt Hoiland, all three of them coincidentally named Matt (the company's legal
entity name was literally "Three Matts Inc.") — Tali built a voice-powered timekeeping assistant for
lawyers. Speak your billable hours to Amazon Alexa or Google Assistant, and it synced directly into
legal billing platforms like Clio, Rocket Matter, and PracticePanther.

By most early measures, Tali looked like it was working. The team raised nearly $1 million over 18
months from Cascade Angels, Mucker Capital, Geekdom Fund, and SoFi Ventures. In October 2018 they won
$100,000 in Clio's Launch//Code pitch competition. They launched a paid beta at the Clio Cloud
Conference in September 2017 and had 101 paying customers within three months.

Then the cracks showed. The subscription auto-renewal system hadn't been built before launch — when the
first billing cycle ended, Volm had to personally track down all 101 customers and ask them to manually
re-enter payment details. Only 22 did. A 78% churn rate, driven entirely by a missing feature rather
than a bad product.

What followed was a full year of trying nearly every acquisition channel available to an early-stage
B2B startup. In his own words, published later in a detailed Medium postmortem: outbound sales had
"unit economics [that] were not sustainable." Digital marketing produced "results [that] were poor."
Google AdWords was "expensive and conversion rates were low." Facebook ads and reseller partnerships
fared no better — the resellers, he wrote, "weren't driving any volume."

They tried cutting the price from $30 a month to $12, hoping cost was the barrier. User count roughly
doubled, to around 250 by the end of 2018. Monthly recurring revenue stayed essentially flat, at around
$3,000 — the price cut had erased almost exactly as much revenue as the new users added.

By December 2018, Tali had about 2.5 months of runway left, and at its current growth rate would have
needed 26 more months to reach cash-flow positive. Volm went back to existing investors, then angels,
then later-stage funds, looking for a bridge. Every one said no.

Mentors advised against limping forward in what Volm's advisors called "zombie mode" — continuing to
operate with no real path to survival. Rather than do that, Volm and his co-founders shut Tali down in
2019.

And then, instead of moving on quietly, Volm wrote it all up: the channels, the numbers, the exact
reasons things didn't work. That choice — to be this specific, this soon, about a real and still-recent
failure — is what makes Tali worth studying. Not the size of the raise, and not the shutdown itself, but
the fact that one of the internet's clearest answers to "why do startup marketing channels fail" comes
from a founder who simply decided to tell the truth about it.
