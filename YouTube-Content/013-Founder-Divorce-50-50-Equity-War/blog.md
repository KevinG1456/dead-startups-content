## What This Case Illustrates About Co-Founder Equity Risk
Regardless of which side's account is more accurate, this case illustrates a structural risk common to many founding teams: relying on informal understandings about credit, roles, and equity rather than documenting them clearly and early. When a founding relationship is strong, formal agreements can feel unnecessary or even distrustful to propose — but that's precisely when they're easiest to put in place fairly, before any disagreement has emerged.

## A Practical Co-Founder Equity Checklist
- **Put the equity split in writing from day one**, even if verbally agreed upon informally first — ambiguity is far more dangerous once a relationship becomes strained
- **Use a standard vesting schedule** (commonly four years with a one-year cliff) so equity is earned over time and tied to continued contribution
- **Document IP assignment clearly**, ensuring any pre-existing ideas or contributions brought into the company are formally assigned to it
- **Revisit the agreement as roles evolve** — equity splits made at the very beginning may not reflect contributions a year or two later, and proactively addressing this can prevent resentment
- **Involve a lawyer early**, even for a co-founder agreement that feels like a formality — the cost is minor compared to the cost of a dispute later

## The Lesson
Founding relationships often begin with high trust and informal understandings — which is exactly why formal protections matter most at that stage, not after a disagreement has already begun. This case is a reminder that credit, contribution, and equity are worth discussing explicitly and documenting clearly, however uncomfortable that conversation might feel in the moment.
