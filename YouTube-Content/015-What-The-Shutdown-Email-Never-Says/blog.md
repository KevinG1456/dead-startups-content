# Blog Article: I Read 23 Real Startup Shutdown Letters. Nobody Actually Said "Sunset."

*All quotes and figures sourced to real, independently verified startup shutdown letters — see `sources-log.txt` for the full letter-by-letter citation map. This started as a plan for 50 letters; 23 real, independently verifiable ones is the honest number, and that's what this article and video use.*

## Introduction
"Sunset" is the joke everyone makes about startup shutdown letters — the assumed go-to euphemism for a product or company dying. Nobody had actually checked whether that's true. This article compiles 23 real, independently verified startup shutdown letters, spanning 2015 to 2023, and counts the actual patterns instead of assuming them. The result corrects the assumption: "sunset" appears zero times across the real corpus. The actual dominant term is "wind down."

## The Real Corpus
Twenty-three genuine founder or company shutdown communications make up the core of this analysis: Secret (2015), Quibi (2020), Yik Yak (2017), Sidecar (2015), Juicero (2017), Munchery (2019), Take Eat Easy (2016), Doppler Labs (2017), Brandless (2020), Vine (2016), Katerra (2021), SpoonRocket (2016), Homejoy (2015), Zirtual (2015), Beepi (2016), Fast (2022), Ozy Media (2021), Josephine (2018), Maple (2017), Airware (2018), MoviePass (2018-2019), Karhoo (2016), and Convoy (2023). A separate set of four modern bankruptcy-filing statements — WeWork, Bird, 23andMe, and the weaker-fit Managed by Q — is kept distinct throughout, since bankruptcy PR is a structurally different genre from a founder's farewell letter.

## What the Letters Actually Say

### The "Heavy Heart" Cluster
Four of the 23 letters — Quibi, Munchery, Zirtual, and Airware — use some version of "heavy heart." That's about 17%, real and present, but far from universal.

### The Reframe Cluster
Five letters actively resist framing the shutdown as an ending at all. Sidecar's founders wrote that it was "a turning point... by no means the end of the journey." Juicero's statement said it needed "an acquirer," never using the words "shutting down." Maple framed its shutdown as "joining" Deliveroo. This reframing pattern (about 22% of the corpus) is one of the more consistent tonal moves across otherwise very different letters.

### The Outliers
Yik Yak's farewell post was almost breezy — "so long for summer to y'all too" — a tone that sits nowhere near the rest of the corpus. Karhoo's statement, at the opposite extreme, was flat and legalistic: "no longer financially viable," with no emotional language at all, and its founder declined further comment. These two letters mark the real emotional range of the genre.

### The Ones That Actually Said Something
A meaningful minority of the corpus is genuinely candid. Take Eat Easy's founder, Adrien Roose, wrote: "desperately tried... none of them materialised... no plan B" — no softening euphemism anywhere in it. Beepi's founders wrote, "we take full responsibility for letting our investors... down," a rare, direct accountability statement. SpoonRocket named a specific competitor (Sprig) and "a tightening funding environment" directly, rather than staying vague. Josephine named a specific failed $500,000 wire transfer and a legal subpoena. Convoy's CEO Dan Lewis named the real causes with unusual precision: "an unprecedented freight market collapse" combined with "dramatic monetary tightening."

## The Homejoy Gap
The clearest single example of this whole video's thesis is Homejoy. Its public 2015 shutdown letter cited "difficult decision" and vague "unresolved challenges." Privately, CEO Adora Cheung told journalists that pending worker-misclassification lawsuits were the real deciding factor. That detail was never in the public letter — it only surfaced in separate press interviews. The gap between what a company says publicly and what actually happened is not a hypothetical concept; it's a documented fact in at least this one case, and probably an underlying pattern across more of the corpus than can be directly proven.

## The Real Numbers
Counted from the real 23-letter corpus, not assumed:
- **"Sunset" (literal):** 0 of 23 — the headline finding. Despite being the most commonly assumed shutdown euphemism, it never appears.
- **"Wind down":** 3 of 23 (≈13%) — the real dominant term in its place.
- **"Heavy heart":** 4 of 23 (≈17%).
- **"Difficult decision" or a direct variant:** 3 of 23 (≈13%).
- **"Explored/exhausted every option" or a variant:** 3 of 23 (≈13%).
- **Names a specific, real internal cause:** 7 of 23 (≈30%).
- **Stays vague about the cause:** 9 of 23 (≈39%).
- **Addresses investors, employees, and customers as genuinely distinct audiences:** only 2 of 23 (≈9%) — Zirtual and Convoy.
- **Reframes the shutdown as not really an ending:** 5 of 23 (≈22%).

## A Different Genre: Bankruptcy Statements
Four modern bankruptcy-filing statements were compiled separately and deliberately not folded into the percentages above, because they represent a structurally different genre — corporate PR minimizing an event rather than a founder mourning one. WeWork's 2023 statement said "no change to the way we operate." Bird's 2023 statement called its bankruptcy "a significant milestone in Bird's transformation." 23andMe CEO Anne Wojcicki's 2025 statement paired "I equally take accountability" with "my belief in the company... is unwavering" in the same breath. These statements minimize; the Tier A founder letters, even at their vaguest, generally don't.

## The Lesson
"Sunset" is a myth, at least across this real, checkable corpus — "wind down" is the actual term startups use when they shut down. More broadly, the letters that say the least ("unresolved challenges," vague market language) are consistently the ones with the most interesting private story behind them, and only a small minority of real shutdown letters actually differentiate their message for investors, employees, and customers, even though those audiences plainly need different information. If you're ever the one writing this letter, the data suggests candor (Take Eat Easy, Beepi, Convoy) reads as more distinctive and more respected than euphemism, if only because so few letters actually choose it.
