## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Bird
— founded September 2017 in Santa Monica, California, by Travis VanderZanden, a former COO of Lyft
and former Uber executive. Bird's business was dockless electric-scooter rental: a rider finds and
unlocks a scooter through the app, rides it, and leaves it. Unlike almost every other company this
channel has covered, Bird's core asset wasn't software — it was a literal fleet of physical
scooters that had to be found, charged, and redistributed every single night, and that were
constantly damaged, stolen, or vandalized. This is this channel's first hardware-fleet
unit-economics story, built around a "cost of one scooter" framework — acquisition cost, nightly
charging and collection labor, and damage/theft/vandalism loss — as a recurring educational device
distinct from the software-based autopsies covered elsewhere on this channel.

Bird's total funding is reported inconsistently across sources: roughly $700 million cumulative as
of October 2019, and separately a $700 million Series C round that closed April 28, 2021 — two
different, coincidentally similar-sounding figures describing different things at different times.
Aggregator totals across all rounds range from roughly $765 million to $1.1 billion. This video uses
"roughly $700 million-plus in venture funding before going public" throughout, never one single
precise lifetime total.

In November 2021, Bird went public via SPAC merger with Switchback II Corp, reaching a reported
market value as high as $2.3-2.5 billion. The SPAC transaction itself brought in approximately $414
million in cash proceeds — combined with Bird's pre-SPAC VC funding, a rough total north of $1
billion raised across Bird's full history, presented as an approximate combined figure, not one
precise number.

In November 2022, Bird's own audit committee determined that its financial reports covering Q1 2020
through Q2 2022 — roughly 2.5 years — "should no longer be relied upon," per an 8-K filed with the
SEC. The issue: Bird had been recognizing revenue from unpaid customer rides, overstating top-line
revenue. Bird initially planned to revise its first two quarters of 2022 alone by $12.5 million,
part of a total revision of $31.6 million across the full 2020-2022 restatement period. This video
states plainly: no individual was criminally charged in connection with this restatement in this
video's sourcing. This was a disclosed, self-reported accounting failure, not a fraud allegation.

September 22, 2023, Bird received formal notice from the NYSE that its shares would be delisted,
effective September 25, 2023, after failing to maintain the exchange's minimum market
capitalization requirement. Bird's market value had fallen from its SPAC-era peak to approximately
$70 million within about 12 months of the deal closing. December 20, 2023, Bird Global Inc. filed
for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Florida federal bankruptcy court, seeking to sell the
company.

April 5, 2024, Bird Global's assets sold in an approximately $145 million transaction — not to an
outside buyer, but to Third Lane Mobility, a new private holding company formed by a consortium of
Bird's own first- and second-lien lenders, led by MidCap Financial (an Apollo Global Management
affiliate) and Obelysk. This was Bird's own lenders converting their debt into ownership — a
structure this channel previously documented with Convoy (package 017), on a completely different
kind of business. The $145 million sale price is well above the roughly $70 million market value
Bird held as a public company a year earlier — this reflects the value of the underlying operating
business and fleet assets sold free of the public company's debt and legal liabilities, not a claim
that the company was actually worth more than the stock market thought.

Bird is this channel's fourth SPAC-to-bankruptcy story, after Sonder (025), WeWork (026), and
23andMe (027) — all four went public via SPAC around the 2021 SPAC boom and later failed. Bird is
the only one of the four where the company's own accounting was later found to be wrong.
