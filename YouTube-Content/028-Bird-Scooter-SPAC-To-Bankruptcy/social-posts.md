## LinkedIn
"A real, underused case study in hardware-fleet unit economics — and in what happens when a public
company's own accounting turns out to be wrong:

Bird raised roughly $700 million-plus in venture funding before going public via SPAC merger in
November 2021, reaching a valuation as high as $2.3-2.5 billion. Unlike most companies I cover on
this channel, Bird's core asset wasn't software — it was a literal fleet of physical scooters that
had to be found, charged, and redistributed every night, and that were constantly damaged, stolen,
or vandalized.

In November 2022, Bird's own audit committee disclosed that the company had overstated revenue for
two and a half years by recognizing revenue from rides customers never actually paid for. To be
precise: this was a self-reported SEC filing, not a fraud charge — no individual was criminally
charged in connection with it.

Bird's market value fell to roughly $70 million within about a year of going public. NYSE delisted
the stock effective September 25, 2023. Bird filed Chapter 11 on December 20, 2023. In April 2024,
its assets sold for approximately $145 million — not to an outside acquirer, but to Third Lane
Mobility, a new holding company formed by Bird's own lenders converting their debt into ownership.

Full breakdown, including the 'cost of one scooter' hardware-economics framework and the direct
comparison to this channel's Convoy video (package 017) on lender-takeover endings: [video link]
