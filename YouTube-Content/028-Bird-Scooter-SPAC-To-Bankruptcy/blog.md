# Blog Article: Bird Went Public at $2.3 Billion. Its Own Accounting Was Wrong. It Sold Itself to Its Own Lenders.

*All figures sourced to Bird's own SEC filings, public reporting (TechCrunch, Forbes, Fortune,
CNBC, Bloomberg), and deal-advisory materials on the Third Lane Mobility sale — see
`sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Bird's total lifetime funding has no single reconciled
figure across aggregators, so this article uses "roughly $700 million-plus in venture funding before
going public" throughout, never one all-inclusive lifetime total. The November 2022 accounting
restatement is presented as a disclosed, self-reported SEC filing about overstated revenue
recognition — not a fraud allegation — because no individual was criminally charged in connection
with it in this research pass.*

## Introduction
Most startup-failure stories on this channel are software stories: a subscription that never found
enough paying users, a marketplace that couldn't hit liquidity, a SaaS company whose growth was
faked. Bird is different. Bird's core product was never code — it was a literal fleet of physical
electric scooters sitting on real sidewalks, in real cities, getting ridden, charged, redistributed,
damaged, stolen, and vandalized every single day. That difference matters, and it's the spine of this
article. Bird also did something almost none of this channel's other companies have done: it went
public, reached a valuation as high as $2.3 billion, and then told its own investors, in an SEC
filing, that its own revenue numbers had been wrong for two and a half years. This is not a fraud
story — no individual, including founder Travis VanderZanden, has been criminally charged with
anything in this article's sourcing. It's a story about what a hardware fleet actually costs, and
what happens when a public company's own numbers turn out not to hold up.

## The Founder and the Build
Bird was founded in September 2017 in Santa Monica, California, by Travis VanderZanden, a former
COO of Lyft and former Uber executive. The business model: dockless electric-scooter rental. A rider
finds and unlocks a scooter through the app, rides it, and leaves it wherever the ride ends. Unlike
a ride-share driver's own car or a hotel room a company merely manages, every scooter in Bird's
fleet was Bird's own physical asset — one that required nightly charging and redistribution, and one
that was constantly exposed to damage, theft, and vandalism simply by existing outdoors and
unattended.

## Why Hardware Is a Different Business
Before getting into Bird's numbers, it's worth naming the structural difference explicitly. A
software subscription business has costs, but a scooter is not a software subscription. It's useful
to think about a "cost of one scooter" framework as three broad categories: **acquisition cost** —
every scooter has to be bought before it earns a single ride; **nightly charging and collection
labor** — someone has to physically find, charge, and redistribute every scooter, every night, in
every city, whether or not it got ridden that day; and **damage, theft, and vandalism loss** — every
scooter is a real object sitting outside, unattended, that can be stolen, thrown in a river, or
simply broken, at any time. This article presents these as illustrative, educational categories, not
as Bird's own confirmed per-scooter dollar figures — Bird-specific unit-cost data isn't part of this
research pass's sourcing. But the categories themselves explain, structurally, why a hardware-fleet
business is a fundamentally different economic bet than a software company or even a leased-real-
estate company: its costs recur physically, every night, regardless of revenue.

## The Raise
Bird's funding figures are reported inconsistently across sources and time periods. As of October
2019, cumulative venture funding was reported at approximately $700 million. Separately, on April
28, 2021, Bird closed a $700 million Series C round — a later, much larger single round that
happens to share a similar figure with the 2019 cumulative number. These are two different things at
two different times; this article does not conflate them. Aggregator totals across all funding
rounds range from approximately $765 million to $1.1 billion depending on source and cutoff date.
This article uses "roughly $700 million-plus in venture funding before going public" as the safe,
hedged pre-SPAC figure, consistent with this channel's standard practice for messy aggregator
totals.

## Going Public
In November 2021, Bird went public via SPAC merger with Switchback II Corp, reaching a reported
market value as high as $2.3-2.5 billion around the deal. The SPAC transaction itself brought in
approximately $414 million in cash proceeds. Combining Bird's pre-SPAC VC funding (roughly $700
million-plus) with the SPAC's own $414 million in cash proceeds gives a rough combined total north
of $1 billion raised across Bird's full history — presented here as an approximate combined figure,
not a single precise number.

## The Accounting Restatement
In November 2022, Bird's own audit committee determined that its financial reports covering Q1 2020
through Q2 2022 — roughly 2.5 years — "should no longer be relied upon," per an 8-K filed with the
SEC. The specific issue: Bird had been recognizing revenue from unpaid customer rides — counting
rides as revenue even when the rider was never actually charged or didn't pay — which overstated
top-line revenue. Bird initially planned to revise its first two quarters of 2022 alone by $12.5
million, part of a total revision of $31.6 million across the full 2020-2022 restatement period.

This needs to be stated precisely: no evidence was found in this research pass of any individual
executive being criminally charged or personally sanctioned specifically in connection with this
accounting restatement. This article presents it as a disclosed, self-reported accounting failure —
an SEC filing Bird itself made — not as a fraud allegation. The accurate description is overstated
or misstated revenue recognition, self-disclosed via SEC filing, not fraud.

## The Collapse
On September 22, 2023, Bird received formal notice from the NYSE that its shares would be delisted,
effective at market open September 25, 2023, after Bird failed to maintain the exchange's required
minimum market capitalization ($15 million for 30 consecutive trading days). Bird moved to OTC
trading. Bird's market value had fallen from its roughly $2.3-2.5 billion SPAC-era peak to
approximately $70 million within about 12 months of the SPAC deal closing — and continued falling
from there before the eventual delisting roughly two years after the SPAC.

On December 20, 2023, Bird Global Inc. filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Florida federal
bankruptcy court, seeking to sell the company.

## The Sale to Its Own Lenders
On April 5, 2024, Bird Global's assets were sold, completing an approximately $145 million
transaction, to Third Lane Mobility — a new private holding company formed by a consortium of Bird's
own first- and second-lien lenders, led by MidCap Financial (an Apollo Global Management affiliate)
and Obelysk. This is a notable structural detail worth stating explicitly: this was Bird's own
lenders buying the company out of its own bankruptcy — a debt-for-ownership conversion, not an
outside strategic acquirer stepping in. It's similar in spirit to how this channel covered Convoy's
lender-foreclosure ending (package 017), where a secured lender, not the company's own board or an
outside buyer, ended up controlling what happened next.

One important nuance: the $145 million sale price is well above the roughly $70 million market value
Bird held as a public company a year earlier. This does not mean the company was secretly worth more
than the stock market thought, or that the bankruptcy was unnecessary. It reflects the value of the
underlying operating business and fleet assets sold free of the public company's debt and legal
liabilities — a different, cleaner asset than the distressed public equity investors had been
holding.

## The Actual Lesson
Bird is this channel's first hardware-fleet unit-economics story, and its fourth SPAC-to-bankruptcy
story, after Sonder (025), WeWork (026), and 23andMe (027) — all four went public via SPAC around
the 2021 SPAC boom and later failed. Bird is the only one of the four where the company's own
accounting was later found to be wrong. Going public didn't fix Bird's underlying hardware-fleet
economics, and when the market lost confidence, an outside buyer didn't step in — Bird's own lenders
did, converting their debt into ownership, the same structural ending this channel documented with
Convoy on a completely different kind of business.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A hardware fleet carries permanent, recurring physical costs that software doesn't** —
  acquisition, nightly labor, and damage/theft/vandalism loss are structural, not one-time, and they
  don't shrink just because a company goes public
- **A public listing is not the same thing as solved unit economics** — Bird reached the highest
  legitimacy marker in its category and its underlying fleet economics and internal accounting still
  weren't resolved
- **"Restatement" and "fraud" are not the same word** — Bird's own audit committee self-reported an
  overstated-revenue issue via SEC filing; no individual was criminally charged in this research
  pass, and conflating the two mischaracterizes a real but different kind of failure
- **A higher sale price than the prior market value isn't vindication** — Third Lane Mobility's $145
  million purchase reflects operating assets sold free of debt and liability, not proof the public
  market undervalued Bird
- **Watch who actually ends up in control when a company fails** — Bird's own secured lenders, not an
  outside acquirer, converted debt into ownership, a structural pattern this channel has now
  documented twice (Convoy, package 017; Bird, package 028)

## The Lesson
Bird raised more than $700 million, reached a public valuation as high as $2.3 billion, and still
ended in Chapter 11 after its own accounting was found to be wrong — with its own lenders, not an
outside buyer, ending up owning what was left. A public listing and a multi-billion-dollar valuation
don't erase the recurring physical costs of a hardware fleet, and they don't guarantee a company's
own internal numbers are right.
