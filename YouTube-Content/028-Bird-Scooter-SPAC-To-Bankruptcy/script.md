### Main Content — The Bird Case (1:45–12:30)

- **Segment 1 — The Founder and the Build (1:45–3:00):** founded September 2017 in Santa Monica,
  California, by Travis VanderZanden — a former COO of Lyft and former Uber executive. The business:
  dockless electric-scooter rental. A rider finds and unlocks a scooter through the app, rides it,
  and leaves it wherever the ride ends. Unlike almost every other company this channel has covered,
  Bird's core asset wasn't code or a subscription base — it was a literal fleet of physical scooters
  that had to be found, charged, and redistributed every single night, and that were constantly
  damaged, stolen, or vandalized. Visual: founder graphic, a simple "software company vs. hardware
  fleet company" split-screen setting up the video's central contrast.

- **Segment 2 — Why Hardware Is a Different Business (3:00–5:00):** the "cost of one scooter"
  framework — introduced explicitly as an illustrative, educational breakdown of the categories that
  make a hardware fleet fundamentally different from a software subscription or a leased apartment,
  not as Bird's own confirmed per-unit numbers (none are in this package's sourcing). Three
  categories, presented as labeled buckets rather than filled-in dollar figures: (1) acquisition cost
  — every scooter has to be bought before it earns a single ride; (2) nightly charging and collection
  labor — someone has to physically find, charge, and redistribute every scooter, every night, in
  every city, whether or not that scooter got ridden that day; (3) damage, theft, and vandalism loss
  — every scooter is a real object sitting outside, unattended, and it can be stolen, thrown in a
  river, or simply broken, at any time. On-screen disclosure: "Illustrative categories — Bird's own
  specific per-scooter costs are not confirmed in this video's sourcing." Visual: the three-box "cost
  of one scooter" template graphic, empty/labeled only, no invented numbers filled in — a device the
  video returns to at each stage of Bird's story.

- **Segment 3 — The Raise (5:00–6:30):** funding figures for Bird are reported inconsistently across
  sources and time periods. As of October 2019, cumulative venture funding was reported at roughly
  $700 million. Separately, on April 28, 2021, Bird closed a $700 million Series C round — a later,
  much larger single round that happens to share a similar figure with the 2019 cumulative number;
  these are two different things at two different times, never conflate them. Aggregator totals
  across all funding rounds range from roughly $765 million to $1.1 billion depending on source and
  cutoff date. On-screen phrasing used throughout: "roughly $700 million-plus in venture funding
  before going public" — the safe, hedged figure, never one single precise lifetime total. Visual:
  funding-round timeline graphic building, with an explicit on-screen callout separating the Oct 2019
  figure from the Apr 2021 Series C round.

- **Segment 4 — Going Public (6:30–8:00):** November 2021, Bird went public via SPAC merger with
  Switchback II Corp, reaching a reported market value as high as $2.3-2.5 billion. The SPAC
  transaction itself brought in approximately $414 million in cash proceeds. Combining the pre-SPAC
  VC funding (~$700M+) with the SPAC's own cash proceeds gives a rough combined total north of $1
  billion raised across Bird's full history — presented as an approximate combined figure, not one
  precise number. Visual: SPAC-merger graphic, valuation figure, funding total climbing toward "north
  of $1 billion" with an explicit "approximate" label.

- **Segment 5 — The Accounting Restatement (8:00–10:00):** November 2022, Bird's own audit committee
  determined that its financial reports covering Q1 2020 through Q2 2022 — roughly 2.5 years —
  "should no longer be relied upon," per an 8-K filed with the SEC. The specific issue: Bird had been
  recognizing revenue from unpaid customer rides — counting rides as revenue even when the rider was
  never actually charged or didn't pay — which overstated top-line revenue. Bird initially planned to
  revise its first two quarters of 2022 alone by $12.5 million, part of a total revision of $31.6
  million across the full 2020-2022 restatement period. On-screen disclosure, stated plainly and
  slowly: "No individual was criminally charged in connection with this restatement in this video's
  sourcing. This was a disclosed, self-reported accounting failure — an SEC filing Bird itself made —
  not a fraud allegation." Visual: 8-K filing graphic, restatement-period timeline (Q1 2020 to Q2
  2022), the $12.5M and $31.6M figures displayed side by side with clear labeling.

- **Segment 6 — The Collapse (10:00–11:15):** September 22, 2023, Bird received formal notice from
  the NYSE that its shares would be delisted, effective at market open September 25, 2023, after
  failing to maintain the exchange's required minimum market capitalization ($15 million for 30
  consecutive trading days). Bird moved to OTC trading. Bird's market value had fallen from its
  ~$2.3-2.5B SPAC-era peak to approximately $70 million within about 12 months of the SPAC deal
  closing — and kept falling from there before the eventual delisting roughly two years after the
  SPAC. Visual: valuation graph collapsing from $2.3-2.5B toward $70M, delisting-notice document
  graphic.

- **Segment 7 — Bankruptcy and the Lender Sale (11:15–12:30):** December 20, 2023, Bird Global Inc.
  filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in Florida federal bankruptcy court, seeking to sell the
  company. April 5, 2024, Bird Global's assets were sold, completing an approximately $145 million
  transaction, to Third Lane Mobility — a new private holding company formed by a consortium of
  Bird's own first- and second-lien lenders, led by MidCap Financial (an Apollo Global Management
  affiliate) and Obelysk. State this explicitly: this was Bird's own lenders buying the company out
  of its own bankruptcy — a debt-for-ownership conversion, not an outside strategic acquirer stepping
  in — similar in spirit to how this channel handled Convoy's lender-foreclosure ending (package
  017). Careful framing: the $145 million sale price is well above the ~$70 million market value Bird
  held as a public company a year earlier — this reflects the value of the underlying operating
  business and fleet assets sold free of the public company's debt and legal liabilities, not "the
  company was actually worth more than the stock market thought." Do not imply the bankruptcy was
  unnecessary. Visual: bankruptcy-filing graphic, then a "your own lenders" ownership-conversion
  diagram — equity holders at the bottom, secured lenders converting debt into ownership at the top.

- **Mid-video CTA (~11:45):** "If you haven't seen this channel's Convoy video — package 017 — the
  general mechanism behind a secured lender ending up in control, rather than a company's own board
  or an outside buyer, is explained there in full, on a completely different kind of business.
  Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson — A Hardware Fleet Is a Different Bet (12:30–15:00)
- Return to the "cost of one scooter" framework one final time, now populated with what Bird's
  public story actually demonstrates in outcome, not in invented specific figures: a hardware fleet
  carries acquisition cost, nightly labor cost, and constant loss-to-damage/theft/vandalism as
  permanent, recurring line items that a software subscription or a leased-unit business simply does
  not carry in the same way. Bird reached the highest legitimacy marker available — a public listing
  at a multi-billion-dollar valuation — and its own accounting still turned out to be wrong, and its
  underlying fleet economics still weren't solved by the time the market lost confidence.
- Cross-reference the channel's own SPAC-era pattern: Bird is this channel's fourth SPAC-to-
  bankruptcy story, after 025 (Sonder), 026 (WeWork), and 027 (23andMe) — all four went public via
  SPAC in and around the 2021 SPAC boom, and all four later failed. Bird is the only one of the four
  where the company's own accounting was later found to be wrong.
- The core, carefully-framed irony: going public didn't fix Bird's underlying fleet economics — and
  when the market lost confidence, it wasn't an outside buyer that stepped in to take over what was
  left. It was Bird's own lenders, converting debt into ownership, the same structural ending this
  channel documented with Convoy (package 017) on a completely different kind of business.
- Visual: four-company SPAC-wave comparison graphic (Sonder / WeWork / 23andMe / Bird) — how each
  went public, how each ended — landing on Bird's unique "own accounting was wrong" distinction.

### Final CTA + Outro (15:00–17:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a hardware fleet carries real, physical, recurring costs that a

  027/23andMe) + subscribe

  and collapse arrive

  restatement and collapse timeline as quickly as possible

  retention data shows strong baseline product familiarity (near-universal for this specific
  company)
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease "its own numbers turned out to be wrong" in the cold open

- **Recommended visual change:** keep the "cost of one scooter" three-box framework visible in a
  corner throughout Segments 2, 6, and 7, so the hardware-economics thesis stays concrete across the
  whole video, not just its own dedicated segment
