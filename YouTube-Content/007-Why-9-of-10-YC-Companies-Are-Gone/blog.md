# Blog Article: I Audited All 11 Companies From a Real YC Batch. Here's What "9 Out of 10 Gone" Actually Means.

*All company statuses sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map and audit table. One company's status (Zanbazaar) is flagged as single-sourced rather than fully confirmed.*

## Introduction
"9 out of 10 startups fail" is one of the most repeated statistics in startup culture, and almost nobody actually checks it against a real, complete cohort. This article does — a full, individually-verified audit of all 11 companies from Y Combinator's Summer 2006 batch, not a sample. The honest finding is more interesting than a single number: depending on how you define "gone," the real ratio for this batch is either 91% or 73%, and both are defensible.

## Why This Batch
YC's actual first batch, Summer 2005 — Reddit, Kiko, and six others — was considered and rejected for this audit. It's YC's own origin-myth batch, famous for Reddit's success and the "Kiko sold on eBay" story, and using it would be exactly the kind of cherry-picking that makes a statistic meaningless. Summer 2006 (S06), YC's third batch, was chosen instead: 11 companies, small enough to fully and individually verify rather than sample, with no single company dominating the narrative the way Reddit would.

## The Full Roster
Six companies — Jamglue (music remixing), Jumpchat (bridged messaging, raised $125K from YC), Likebetter (photo-comparison game), Pollground (online polls), Shoutfit (fashion community), Talkito (chat service), and Thinkature (collaboration whiteboard) — shut down with little lasting trace, confirmed via TechCrunch's contemporaneous November 2006 coverage of the batch and cross-checked against the aggregator YCDB.

**OMGPOP** started as a dating site called "i'minlikewithyou," pivoted to casual social gaming under CEO Dan Porter, and became a genuine breakout hit with the game Draw Something. Zynga acquired it in March 2012 for a reported $180-200 million (sources don't agree on the exact figure). Zynga shut the product down roughly 14 months later, in June-August 2013 — and, per reporting on the shutdown, the original team reportedly tried to buy the site back from Zynga and was refused.

**Xobni**, an Outlook plugin and email-analytics tool built by Adam Smith and Matt Brezina, was acquired by Yahoo in July 2013 for a reported $30-60 million or more. Yahoo shut it down about a year later, in July 2014, folding a few of its features into Yahoo Mail — the same acquire-then-discontinue pattern as OMGPOP, on a smaller scale.

**Scribd**, pitched in 2006 under the working name "Social Documents" and built by Trip Adler, Tikhon Bernstam, and Jared Friedman, is the batch's sole independent survivor. It raised $105 million over its life, transitioned to a new CEO, Tony Grimminck, in October 2023, and has reported usage and margin growth since.

**Zanbazaar**, the eleventh company, appears to have shut down — but this status is confirmed only by a single aggregator source (YCDB), not independently corroborated by a second outlet or primary source. This article flags that distinction explicitly rather than presenting it with the same confidence as the other ten.

## The Real Ratio — Why It's Two Numbers, Not One
Here's where most "X out of 10 startups fail" content stops short: it picks a number and moves on. The honest answer for this batch depends on a specific definitional choice.

**Definition 1 — "No longer exists in any form today":** the 8 outright shutdowns, plus OMGPOP and Xobni (both acquired and later discontinued by their acquirers) — that's 10 of 11, roughly 91%. Only Scribd survives as an independent product. This is close enough that "9 out of 10" genuinely holds up, under this specific definition.

**Definition 2 — "The founders and investors got a real outcome":** if OMGPOP's and Xobni's acquisitions count as what they actually were for the people who built and funded them — real, multi-million-dollar cash exits — regardless of what the acquirer later did with the product, the ratio drops to 8 of 11, roughly 73%.

Neither number is wrong. They're answers to two different, both legitimate questions: "does the product still exist" and "did the people who built it get paid." Most repetitions of "9 out of 10 startups fail" implicitly answer the first question while letting listeners assume it answers the second, which is exactly the kind of conflation that makes the statistic more myth than fact.

## What This Audit Actually Teaches
- **"Acquired" is not a single outcome** — OMGPOP and Xobni represent one of the most common and least-discussed patterns in startup outcomes: a real financial exit followed by the product's quiet death under new ownership, sometimes within about a year
- **Small-batch audits are more honest than aggregate statistics** — a repeated statistic without a checkable source is not the same as a verified count, and this batch shows both a nuanced 91%/73% split behind what looks like a simple number
- **Survivorship over 20 years is genuinely rare** — even in a curated, funded, mentored cohort, only one company out of eleven still operates independently two decades later

## The Lesson
"9 out of 10 startups fail" is close to true for this batch under one honest definition, and meaningfully overstated under another. The only way to know which one you're looking at is to actually check — company by company, source by source — rather than repeat a number that's been detached from its original definition through years of casual citation.
