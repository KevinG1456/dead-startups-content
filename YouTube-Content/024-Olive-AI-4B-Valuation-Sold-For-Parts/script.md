## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 13–16 minutes

### Hook (0:15–0:55)
- VO: "This is Olive AI — a healthcare startup that hit a $4 billion valuation selling hospitals on 'the AI workforce for healthcare.' Then an investigation went looking for the AI, and eighteen months later, Olive didn't exist anymore — not because it went bankrupt, but because it sold off every valuable piece of itself to competitors first."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly what an investigation found when it tested Olive's claims, why the ending took eighteen months instead of one dramatic week, and where every piece of a $4 billion company actually ended up."

### Introduction (0:55–1:45)
- Method statement: reconstructed from two primary-sourced funding press releases (Dec 2020, Jul 2021), an April 2022 Axios investigation (via corroborated secondary reporting), and dated coverage of the 2022–2023 layoffs and asset sales — sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this is the first story on this channel that lands directly in the AI-hype-versus-reality conversation happening right now

### Main Content — The Olive AI Case (1:45–9:45)
- Segment 1 — The Origin (1:45–3:00): founded 2012 in Columbus, Ohio, by CEO Sean Lane and CTO Jeremy Yoder, originally as CrossChx doing patient identity verification. Lane, a former Air Force intelligence officer, was inspired by dysfunction he saw at a hospital in his home region during the opioid crisis. Rebranded to Olive in 2017 with a new pitch: automate hospitals' administrative and revenue-cycle work using AI. Visual: CrossChx logo morphing into the Olive logo, timeline marker at 2017.
- Segment 2 — The Climb (3:00–4:30): December 2020, a $225.5M round values Olive at $1.5 billion. Seven months later, July 2021, a $400M round led by Vista Equity Partners values Olive at $4 billion — its peak. Sean Lane's own pitch, on record: "For every dollar Olive makes, healthcare saves five." Visual: valuation graphic climbing from $1.5B to $4B, Lane quote on screen, on-screen note: "Olive's own marketing claim — not independently audited."
- Segment 3 — The Investigation (4:30–6:00): April 2022, Axios reports that Olive is overpromising its capabilities and that its claimed savings for hospital customers relied on rough estimates rather than delivered, audited results. On-screen lower third: "Findings drawn from multiple independent reports on the original investigation." Visual: "AI" label on screen with a magnifying glass icon, question mark overlay.
- Segment 4 — The First Cut (6:00–7:00): July 2022, roughly 450 employees — about 35% of the company — laid off. CEO Sean Lane's stated reason, on record: "fast-paced growth and lack of focus." Visual: headcount bar chart dropping.
- Segment 5 — The Second Cut (7:00–7:45): February 2023, a second layoff round — roughly 200 more employees. On-screen note: "Exact count reported inconsistently across sources — presented as an approximation." Visual: headcount bar chart dropping again.
- Segment 6 — Sold For Parts (7:45–9:15): April 2023, Olive sells its utilization management tools business to Availity. Later in 2023, Olive sells its clearinghouse business and its patient access business — described in coverage as "the heart of Olive's business" — to Waystar and Humata Health. On-screen note: "Exact sale date not confirmed — occurred in the months before the final shutdown." Visual: three separate business-unit icons flying off to three different buyer logos, one at a time.
- Segment 7 — What's Left (9:15–9:45): October 31, 2023, Olive formally announces it is winding down what remains. By this point, the company had already sold off the businesses that generated most of its revenue — the announcement closed an emptied-out shell, not a going concern. Visual: the "$4B" figure from the cold open, now reduced to an empty outline.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:00): "If you want to understand exactly what 'AI-hype collapse' looks like in slow motion instead of theory, the next part connects every piece. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson — What "Sold Off, Not Collapsed" Actually Means (9:45–11:45)
- Plain-language comparison: unlike this channel's Fast video (022, a six-day collapse) or its Munchery video (023, a single bankruptcy filing naming causes), Olive's ending has no single moment — it's assembled from an investigation, two rounds of layoffs, and three separate business sales spread across 18 months.
- The practical distinction: Olive's core assets didn't disappear — they went to Availity, Waystar, and Humata Health, all of whom presumably judged those specific pieces of the business to be worth buying, even as the whole no longer was. The failure wasn't that nothing Olive built had value; it's that the company built around an "AI" story bigger than what its technology, and its market, could actually sustain at a $4 billion valuation.
- Visual: side-by-side comparison graphic — Fast (6 days), Munchery (1 filing), Olive (18 months, 3 buyers) — landing on "same ending, completely different shape"

### Final CTA + Outro (11:45–13:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a company can be worth $4 billion, get investigated, laid off twice, and sold off in three separate pieces, and still never file for bankruptcy at all
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want to see the fastest version of this same story, the Fast video is the direct contrast"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you work in healthcare tech during Olive's rise? Do you think the 'AI' label was fair, or oversold? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 022 (Fast) + most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** Segment 1 (the CrossChx origin story) if it runs too long before the $4B valuation appears
- **Fix:** keep the origin segment under 75 seconds and get to the valuation climb quickly — the AI-hype hook is what's pulling viewers in, not the founding story
- **Slow section risk:** the three separate business-sale beats (Segment 6) could feel repetitive if presented identically
- **Fix:** vary the visual treatment for each sale (different buyer logo animation, different on-screen framing) so the "sold in pieces" structure reads as escalating rather than repetitive
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the three-way Fast/Munchery/Olive comparison early (in the Hook) and pay it off fully in the lesson segment
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a shrinking "$4B" figure visible in a corner throughout the second half, visually counting down as each piece is sold off
