# Script Package

Anchored on WeWork — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Never state SoftBank's total exposure
(~$18.5B) and its reported loss (~$16B) as the same number — they describe two different things.
Always present the valuation arc as three distinct, dated points ($47B 2019 private peak / $9B
October 2021 SPAC enterprise value / ~$750M post-2024-bankruptcy-emergence) — never state one of
these as "the" WeWork valuation without its date attached. Present the November 6, 2023 Chapter 11
explicitly as a REORGANIZATION — WeWork continues operating today under new ownership — never
describe it the same way as this channel's liquidation stories (023/Munchery, 025/Sonder Chapter 7).
No "fraud/scam/lied" language anywhere — Adam Neumann has not been charged with any crime related to
WeWork; this is a governance and incentive-structure story, not a fraud story like 005/HeadSpin or
029/Ozy Media. Present the Flow/Andreessen Horowitz coda neutrally as "what happened next," never as
vindication or condemnation.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 17–20 minutes (single-company deep dive with an unusually long, multi-act
timeline — founding, first IPO collapse, first rescue, second IPO, second collapse, reorganization,
and founder coda — longer than most single-company packages on this channel by design)

- VO: "In 2019, this company was valued at $47 billion — the highest valuation of any company this
  channel has ever covered. In 2023, it filed for Chapter 11. In between, its founder walked away
  with up to $1.7 billion."
- Visual: "$47B" text stamping in large and gold, hard cut to "CHAPTER 11 — NOVEMBER 6, 2023" in
  smaller white text beneath it, a thin descending line connecting the two
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "No one in this story has been
  charged with a crime. This is what legal, incentive-structure failure looks like at the highest
  valuation this channel has ever covered."

### Hook (0:15–0:55)
- VO: "This is WeWork — the company that reached the single highest valuation of any company on
  this channel, watched that valuation collapse by nearly half within weeks of trying to go public,
  got rescued by its own largest investor, went public again years later at a fraction of its old
  value, and still ended up in bankruptcy court. Its founder, meanwhile, secured one of the largest
  personal exit packages in startup history along the way — and by 2025 was running an entirely new
  company, backed by one of the top venture capital firms in the world."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how a company reaches $47 billion
  and still collapses, what actually happened in the six weeks between its IPO filing and its
  founder's resignation, why its 2023 bankruptcy wasn't the same kind of ending as most of the
  stories on this channel, and what its founder is doing right now."

### Introduction (0:55–1:50)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Britannica Money, SEC filings (BowX Acquisition Corp Form
  425/S-4/424B3), TechCrunch, CNBC, Forbes, Al Jazeera, Orrick, Davis Polk, and Yahoo Finance —
  sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this isn't a story about fraud — no one named in this video has been charged
  with or convicted of any crime — it's a story about governance, incentive structure, and what
  happens when a founder's personal control and a company's actual financial health are allowed to
  drift apart
- Direct cross-reference: "If you watched this channel's video on Sonder — package 025 — you already
  heard this company's name once. An analyst comparing Sonder's own collapse called it 'the WeWork
  of apartments.' This is that original story, told in full, for the first time on this channel."

### Main Content — The WeWork Case (1:50–13:30)

- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:50–2:50):** Founded 2010 by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey. First
  location opened April 2011 on Grand Street, SoHo, Manhattan. Business model: lease large blocks of
  commercial office space on long-term leases, subdivide and redesign it, then re-rent it to
  individuals and companies on flexible, short-term membership terms — a fixed-long-term-obligation
  versus flexible-short-term-revenue mismatch, structurally similar in shape (though a different
  industry — commercial real estate, not residential) to the master-lease model this channel covered
  in package 021 (Stay Alfred) and package 025 (Sonder, Zeus Living). Visual: SoHo storefront
  graphic, lease-vs-membership mismatch diagram.

- **Segment 2 — The Raise and the Peak (2:50–4:15):** Total lifetime funding reported at roughly $13
  billion across WeWork's investment history — a widely cited aggregate figure. In 2019, SoftBank led
  a Series H round investing $1 billion, part of a financing history that valued WeWork at $47
  billion at its peak — the highest valuation reached by any company covered on this channel.
  On-screen note: SoftBank's cumulative debt-and-equity exposure to WeWork over the years reached
  approximately $18.5 billion; by 2023, SoftBank's reported cumulative loss on the investment was
  approximately $16 billion — two different, both-real numbers, never conflated. Visual: funding
  timeline climbing to $47B, split callout showing "$18.5B exposure" next to "$16B reported loss" as
  two separate boxes.

- **Segment 3 — The IPO Filing and Its Collapse (4:15–6:30):** August 2019, WeWork released its
  public S-1 IPO prospectus. It met immediate, intense backlash — scrutiny fell on Neumann's
  outsized control through super-voting shares, numerous related-party transactions (including
  WeWork paying Neumann personally for the trademark rights to the word "We"), and the company's
  self-invented non-GAAP metric — "community-adjusted EBITDA" — that excluded most of its actual
  operating costs. Mid-October 2019, the IPO was shelved and formally withdrawn shortly after.
  Visual: S-1 document graphic, "community-adjusted EBITDA" displayed as on-screen text with a plain-
  language translation of what it actually excluded.

- **Segment 4 — The Resignation and the Exit Package (6:30–8:15):** September 24, 2019, Adam Neumann
  resigned as CEO under board pressure, amid the IPO collapse. His exit package was reported at up to
  $1.7 billion total, comprising: up to $970 million for his shares, a $185 million "consulting fee,"
  and $500 million in credit facilities to help him repay personal loans he had taken out partly
  against WeWork stock. On-screen note, stated plainly and without embellishment: this package was
  secured in the same weeks the company's valuation was collapsing, before the layoffs that followed
  hit ordinary employees — a useful, factual contrast to founders on this channel who walked away
  with nothing (package 001, Sprig) or who are facing real criminal exposure (package 029, Ozy
  Media) — WeWork is neither of those; this is a governance story, not a fraud story. Visual: exit
  package breakdown graphic — three line items building to $1.7B.

- **Segment 5 — The First Rescue (8:15–9:15):** October 2019, SoftBank stepped in with a rescue
  package reported at roughly $9.5 billion — a mix of new equity investment, debt financing, and an
  accelerated warrant/tender offer to buy out existing shareholders — taking effective control of
  the company. Visual: rescue-package breakdown graphic.

- **Segment 6 — Going Public, For Real This Time (9:15–10:45):** October 20, 2021, WeWork completed
  a SPAC merger with BowX Acquisition Corp., beginning to trade on the NYSE under ticker "WE" at an
  enterprise value of approximately $9 billion — already a roughly 80% discount from the 2019 private
  peak valuation, even before any further decline. The deal brought in about $1.3 billion in gross
  cash proceeds, from a fully committed PIPE plus a Cushman & Wakefield backstop facility. Visual:
  valuation-arc graphic updates — "$47B (2019)" now joined by "$9B (Oct 2021 SPAC)," the gap between
  them shown visually.

- **Segment 7 — The Second Collapse (10:45–12:30):** WeWork's Class A common stock declined more
  than 98% over the course of 2023 alone. On November 6, 2023, WeWork Inc. and 517 affiliated
  entities filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy, simultaneously entering a restructuring support agreement
  with SoftBank and other major debt holders. At filing: approximately $15 billion in assets against
  approximately $18.6 billion in liabilities, plus roughly $100 million in unpaid rent across U.S.
  and Canada filings only. Adam Neumann's public statement on the filing was notably muted: he called
  it "disappointing." Visual: assets-vs-liabilities graphic, stock-decline chart falling through
  2023, Neumann's one-word quote displayed on screen.

- **Segment 8 — Emergence, Not Liquidation (12:30–13:30):** June 11, 2024, WeWork emerged from
  Chapter 11. Ownership restructured to: Yardi Systems' affiliate Cupar Grimmond at approximately
  60%, SoftBank at approximately 20%, other lenders holding the remaining 20%. Approximately $4
  billion in pre-petition debt was eliminated in the process — not "debt-free," a large but partial
  reduction. Post-emergence valuation reported at roughly $750 million — the third and final point on
  the valuation arc. On-screen note, stated explicitly and directly: WeWork continues operating today
  as a smaller, private company under this new ownership structure — this was a reorganization, not a
  liquidation, a real structural distinction from this channel's other bankruptcy stories (023/
  Munchery liquidated for parts, 025/Sonder's Chapter 7 liquidation). Visual: completed three-point
  valuation-arc graphic — $47B → $9B → ~$750M — held on screen as the video's central visual.

- **Mid-video CTA (~12:45):** "If you haven't seen this channel's video on Sonder — package 025 —
  the analyst quote that inspired this video, calling Sonder 'the WeWork of apartments,' is explained
  there in full. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Coda — What Adam Neumann Did Next (13:30–15:45)
- Segment 9: After his 2019 exit, with the up-to-$1.7B package above, Neumann founded Flow, a
  residential real-estate company built around shared-amenity rental housing — conceptually similar
  in spirit to WeWork's community-focused pitch, applied to apartments instead of offices. Andreessen
  Horowitz invested in Flow at its 2022 launch — a notable bet on Neumann personally, made months
  after WeWork's SPAC-era stock was already cratering, and well after the reputational damage of the
  2019 IPO collapse was fully public. As of 2025, Flow was reportedly valued at approximately $2.5
  billion. Stated plainly, without editorializing either direction: within about three years of the
  event most associated with WeWork's excess, the same founder was backed by a top-tier VC firm to
  build something similar again, and by most public reporting is still running that company today.
  Visual: Flow launch graphic, Andreessen Horowitz logo-free "top-tier VC" text treatment, $2.5B
  figure displayed as the coda's final number.

### The Actual Lesson — Valuation Isn't the Same Thing As Health (15:45–17:30)
- Plain-language comparison: WeWork's core structural bet — long-term fixed lease obligations funded
  against flexible, cancel-anytime revenue — is the same shape of risk this channel has already
  covered at smaller scale in Stay Alfred (package 021) and Sonder/Zeus Living (package 025), just in
  commercial office space instead of residential short-term rental.
- The core irony, stated directly: WeWork reached the highest valuation of any company on this
  channel, went public twice, and still ended up in Chapter 11 — proof that a valuation number, even
  an enormous one, is not the same thing as a healthy underlying business, and that governance
  problems (concentrated founder control, related-party dealings, invented accounting metrics) can
  sit underneath a headline number for years before the market corrects it.
- Visual: valuation-arc graphic held one final time, alongside a brief side-by-side comparison card
  with Stay Alfred (021) and Sonder (025) — "same structural shape, three different scales."

### Final CTA + Outro (17:30–19:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — the highest valuation on this channel still wasn't a
  substitute for sound governance, and reaching a huge number doesn't mean the underlying business
  can support it
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you haven't seen the Sonder or Stay
  Alfred videos, both are direct companion pieces to this one — same underlying structural risk, very
  different scales"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever work out of a WeWork location, or invest in the stock after the
  SPAC merger? What do you think is the bigger lesson here — the governance failure, or the exit
  package? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 025 (Sonder) + package 021 (Stay Alfred) + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed three-point valuation-arc graphic ($47B → $9B → ~$750M), then works
backward into the founding, the S-1 collapse, the two rescues, and the bankruptcy in chronological
detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the SoftBank exposure-vs-loss distinction and the
reorganization-not-liquidation distinction as the two load-bearing facts the whole video is built to
get right. Best for retention with a finance-literate, diligence-minded audience.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the August 2019 S-1 filing itself — the confidence, the "community-adjusted EBITDA" language,
the scale of the ambition — before revealing, scene by scene, how quickly it all came apart across
six weeks. Slower pacing, holds Neumann's resignation and exit package as a mid-video turn rather than
front-loading it, resolves with the SPAC, the 2023 filing, and the Flow coda as three sequential final
beats. Strongest emotional arc of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one company, three valuations, two bankruptcy-adjacent rescues, and one very unusual
ending" — rapid-fire through the founding, the $47B peak, the IPO collapse, the SoftBank rescue, the
SPAC, the Chapter 11 filing, the reorganization, and the Flow coda, landing on the valuation-arc
graphic as the closing image. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $47B→$9B→$750M
sequence and the exit-package breakdown (keep the SoftBank exposure-vs-loss distinction intact even
in fast-cut form — it is too easily garbled).

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** Segment 5-6 (the SoftBank rescue into the SPAC mechanics) if the deal-structure
  detail (PIPE, Cushman & Wakefield backstop, warrant/tender offer) becomes a technical finance
  lecture before the second collapse arrives
- **Fix:** keep deal-structure specifics brief and visual, get to the "$9B, an 80% discount" headline
  fact quickly, save deeper mechanics for on-screen text rather than narration
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general coworking-industry background if audience-retention
  data shows strong baseline familiarity with WeWork as a brand
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the Flow/Andreessen Horowitz coda in the cold open or
  hook framing ("by 2025, running an entirely new company") without revealing the $2.5B figure until
  Segment 9
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the three-point valuation-arc graphic ($47B / $9B / ~$750M)
  visible in a corner from Segment 2 onward, updating live as each new figure is introduced, so the
  video's central thesis stays concrete and visible throughout
