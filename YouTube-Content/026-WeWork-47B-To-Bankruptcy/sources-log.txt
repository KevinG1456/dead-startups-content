Sources Log — Package 026: WeWork
Filled in: 2026-08-15

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
WeWork is the most famous overhype/governance-collapse story in this entire genre —
already name-checked as a direct comparison point in this channel's own package 025
research (Commercial Observer analyst Daniel Lesser called Sonder "the WeWork of
apartments" when explaining Sonder's over-leased-inventory collapse). This is that
story told in full for the first time on this channel. Distinct failure shape from
everything else here: not a single company that quietly ran out of money, but a
company that reached the single highest valuation on this channel ($47B), had its
IPO publicly collapse in six weeks, got bailed out once by its own largest investor,
went public again years later via SPAC at a fraction of that value, and STILL ended
in Chapter 11 — then emerged from bankruptcy and continues operating today, smaller.
Worth flagging: unlike every other package on this channel, WeWork's founder is
alive, not charged with any crime related to WeWork specifically, and is currently
running and successfully fundraising for a NEW company (Flow) — a genuinely
different "where are they now" ending than any other package.

---

FOUNDING AND EARLY GROWTH
- Founded 2010 by Adam Neumann and Miguel McKelvey. First location opened April
  2011 on Grand Street, SoHo, Manhattan.
- Business model: leases large blocks of commercial office space on long-term
  leases, subdivides and redesigns it, then re-rents it to individuals and
  companies on flexible, short-term membership terms — a fixed-long-term-
  obligation-vs-flexible-short-term-revenue mismatch that is structurally similar
  in shape (though a different industry) to the master-lease residential model
  covered in this channel's packages 021 (Stay Alfred) and 025 (Sonder/Zeus
  Living) — worth a brief on-screen comparison, though WeWork is commercial real
  estate, not residential.
Source: Britannica Money; 2727coworking.com company history summary.

FUNDING AND VALUATION PEAK
- Total lifetime funding raised is reported as approximately $13 billion across
  its investment history (widely cited aggregate figure).
- 2019: SoftBank led a Series H round investing $1 billion, part of a financing
  history that valued WeWork at $47 billion at its peak — the highest valuation
  reached by any company covered on this channel.
- SoftBank's CUMULATIVE debt-and-equity exposure to WeWork over the years reached
  approximately $18.5 billion; by 2023 SoftBank's reported cumulative LOSS on the
  investment was approximately $16 billion (loss figure is smaller than total
  exposure because SoftBank recovered some value / restructured some debt — these
  are two different, both-real numbers, don't conflate "total invested" with
  "total lost"). *** Use "SoftBank's exposure reached roughly $18.5 billion; its
  reported loss was roughly $16 billion" — do not state one single number for
  both concepts. ***
Source: Britannica Money; Yahoo Finance ("How SoftBank bet and lost billions on
WeWork"); Forbes (SoftBank rescue coverage).

THE 2019 IPO COLLAPSE — precisely dated
- August 2019: WeWork released its public S-1 IPO prospectus. It met immediate,
  intense backlash — scrutiny fell on Neumann's outsized control (super-voting
  shares), numerous related-party transactions (including WeWork paying Neumann
  personally for the trademark rights to the word "We"), and the company's
  self-invented non-GAAP metric ("community-adjusted EBITDA") that excluded most
  of its actual operating costs.
- Mid-October 2019: the IPO was shelved/put on hold indefinitely; formally
  withdrawn shortly after.
- September 24, 2019: Adam Neumann resigned as CEO under board pressure, amid the
  IPO collapse.
- Neumann's exit package was reported at up to $1.7 billion total, comprising:
  up to $970 million for his shares, a $185 million "consulting fee," and $500
  million in credit facilities to help him repay personal loans (personal loans
  he had taken out partly against WeWork stock). *** This exit package, secured
  in the same weeks the company's valuation collapsed and ordinary employees were
  later laid off, is one of the strongest "who actually pays the price when a
  hyped startup fails" beats on this whole channel — a useful contrast to
  founders on this channel who walked away with nothing (e.g., Sprig, package
  001) or who are conviction-facing (Ozy Media, package 029). ***
- October 2019: SoftBank stepped in with a rescue package reported at roughly
  $9.5 billion (a mix of new equity investment, debt financing, and an
  accelerated warrant/tender offer to buy out existing shareholders), taking
  effective control of the company.
Source: idealsvdr.com IPO-failure summary; Britannica Money; 2727coworking.com;
Al Jazeera (SoftBank rescue coverage, Oct 2019).

THE FIRST PUBLIC LISTING — SPAC MERGER (2021)
- October 20, 2021: WeWork completed a SPAC merger with BowX Acquisition Corp.,
  beginning to trade on the NYSE under ticker "WE" at an enterprise value of
  approximately $9 billion — already a roughly 80% discount from the 2019 peak
  valuation, even before any further decline. The deal brought in about $1.3
  billion in gross cash proceeds (a fully committed PIPE plus a Cushman &
  Wakefield backstop facility).
Source: SEC filings (BowX Acquisition Corp Form 425/S-4/424B3, 2021); Caproasia.

THE SECOND COLLAPSE — CHAPTER 11 (2023), precisely dated
- Class A common stock declined more than 98% over the course of 2023 alone.
- November 6, 2023: WeWork Inc. and 517 affiliated entities filed for Chapter 11
  bankruptcy, simultaneously entering a restructuring support agreement with
  SoftBank and other major debt holders.
- At filing: approximately $15 billion (some sources: $15.06 billion) in assets
  against approximately $18.6 billion (some sources: $18.65 billion) in
  liabilities, plus roughly $100 million in unpaid rent (U.S./Canada filings
  only, not global).
- Adam Neumann's public statement on the bankruptcy filing: he called it
  "disappointing," a notably muted reaction from the company's own co-founder.
Source: Orrick ("WeWork Bankruptcy: The Basics"); TechCrunch (Nov 6 2023); CNBC
(Nov 7 2023); Forbes (Nov 7 2023, "WeWork's Rise to $47 Billion — And Fall to
Bankruptcy: A Timeline").

EMERGENCE FROM BANKRUPTCY
- June 11, 2024: WeWork emerged from Chapter 11. Ownership restructured to: Yardi
  Systems' affiliate Cupar Grimmond at approximately 60%, SoftBank at
  approximately 20%, other lenders holding the remaining 20%. Approximately $4
  billion in pre-petition debt was eliminated in the process.
- Post-emergence valuation reported at roughly $750 million — a small fraction of
  either the 2019 private-market peak ($47B) or even the 2021 SPAC valuation
  ($9B). *** Use this three-point valuation arc ($47B private peak -> $9B SPAC ->
  ~$750M post-bankruptcy) as the clearest single visual/graphic for the whole
  package. ***
- WeWork continues operating today as a smaller, private company under this new
  ownership structure — NOT liquidated, a genuinely different ending than most of
  this channel's other bankruptcy stories (compare: 023/Munchery liquidated for
  parts, 025/Sonder Chapter 7 liquidation). WeWork's Chapter 11 was a
  reorganization that let the company keep operating, which is worth stating
  explicitly on screen as a real structural distinction.
Source: Davis Polk ("WeWork emerges from chapter 11"); Caproasia (restructuring
approval coverage); elevenflo.com ("WeWork: Debt-Free Emergence After Chapter
11" — note this headline overstates the outcome; WeWork eliminated a large
portion of pre-petition debt, it did not emerge completely debt-free in an
absolute sense — present as "eliminated roughly $4 billion in debt," not as
"emerged debt-free").

ADAM NEUMANN'S AFTERMATH — THE CODA
- After his 2019 exit (with the up-to-$1.7B package above), Neumann founded Flow,
  a residential real-estate company built around shared-amenity rental housing —
  conceptually similar in spirit to WeWork's community-focused pitch, applied to
  apartments instead of offices.
- Andreessen Horowitz invested in Flow at launch (2022) — a notable bet on
  Neumann personally, just months after WeWork's SPAC-era stock was already
  cratering (well before the Nov 2023 bankruptcy, but after the reputational
  damage of the 2019 collapse was already fully public). As of 2025, Flow was
  reportedly valued at approximately $2.5 billion.
- *** Editorial framing note: this is a genuinely unusual ending for this
  channel's genre — the founder most associated with the excess that sank the
  company was, within about three years, backed by a top-tier VC firm to do a
  similar thing again, and by most public reporting is still running that
  company today. Present as the irony/coda beat, not as vindication or
  condemnation either way — let the facts (the size of his exit package, the
  timing, the new funding) carry the point. ***
Source: Fortune ("Adam Neumann goes from flop to Flow", Aug 2022); Medium ("The
Capital Review" analysis piece, secondary/opinion source — use only for the
framing observation, not for any factual claim not corroborated elsewhere).

BEFORE RECORDING
1. Do not conflate SoftBank's total debt-and-equity EXPOSURE to WeWork (~$18.5B)
   with SoftBank's reported cumulative LOSS (~$16B) — these are two different
   numbers describing two different things.
2. Present the valuation arc as three distinct, correctly-labeled points: $47B
   (2019 private peak) -> $9B (Oct 2021 SPAC enterprise value) -> ~$750M
   (post-2024-bankruptcy-emergence) — never state one of these as "the" WeWork
   valuation without the date/context attached.
3. WeWork's 2023 Chapter 11 was a REORGANIZATION, not a liquidation — the company
   continues operating today under new majority ownership (Cupar Grimmond/Yardi).
   Do not describe this the same way as this channel's Chapter 7/full-liquidation
   stories (e.g., 025/Sonder).
4. Neumann has NOT been charged with any crime in connection with WeWork — frame
   his story as governance/incentive-structure failure and personal enrichment
   amid collapse, not wrongdoing. No "fraud/scam/lied" language applies here;
   this is a different category of story than 005/HeadSpin or 029/Ozy Media.
5. "Community-adjusted EBITDA" is a real, documented, widely-mocked metric from
   WeWork's own 2019 S-1 — safe to use and explain on screen as a clear, concrete
   example of the IPO's credibility problem.
6. The Flow/Andreessen Horowitz coda is real and well-sourced but should be
   presented neutrally — a factual "what happened next," not editorialized as
   either a redemption arc or an indictment of Silicon Valley.
