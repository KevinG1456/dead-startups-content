# Blog Article: They Raised $929.9 Million. They Still Went Out Of Business.

*All figures sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. The
Polymath Holdings lawsuit against founder Hosain Rahman's successor company is an ongoing, unresolved
civil complaint; Rahman, his co-founder, and the company explicitly deny the allegations. Rahman has
not been charged or convicted of anything — this is an overfunding-and-overextension story, not a
settled fraud story.*

## Introduction
Most startup collapses this channel covers have a shape: a bankruptcy filing, a single brutal week,
a leadership implosion. Jawbone doesn't quite have that shape either. It raised $929.9 million — more
than almost any hardware startup in history — built three genuinely successful product categories,
picked a legal fight with its biggest rival, lost that fight, and then simply disappeared in 2017
through a quiet legal filing most people have never heard of. No bankruptcy. No single bad headline.
Just the slow accumulation of too much money spread across too many bets.

## The Origin
Jawbone began in 1999 in San Francisco as AliphCom, founded by Stanford graduates Hosain Rahman and
Alexander Asseily. The original technology had nothing to do with consumer electronics — it was
military-derived noise-cancellation voice technology, nicknamed "NoiseAssassin," aimed at helping
soldiers communicate clearly in combat noise. In 2002, the company won a DARPA (the Pentagon's
research arm) contract to develop the concept further. That military-grade noise-cancellation core
later became the foundation of the company's first consumer product.

## Three Product Lines, One Company
In 2006, AliphCom launched the first consumer Jawbone Bluetooth headset. From there, the company
expanded aggressively into two more entirely separate hardware categories: the Jambox line of
wireless Bluetooth speakers, and the UP line of wrist-worn fitness and activity trackers — competing
directly with Fitbit in that third category. Three different product lines, three different
competitive markets, one company trying to win all three simultaneously.

## The Climb To $3.3 Billion
Jawbone's funding accelerated fast. In February 2014, a $250 million round led by Rizvi Traverse
Management valued the company at $3.3 billion — its peak. Over its full lifetime, Jawbone raised
$929.9 million, the most of any venture-backed hardware startup on this channel, and by most
accounts the most of any hardware startup to ultimately fail during the wearable-tech boom. CNBC's
own July 2017 headline summed it up directly: "death by overfunding."

## The Loan Disguised As an Investment
In 2015, BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager — put $300 million into Jawbone, publicly
described at the time as an investment. Bloomberg later reported, based on California state filings,
that the $300 million was actually structured as debt rather than equity. By 2016, with that debt
position in hand, BlackRock was reportedly pushing for a sale of the company. CEO Hosain Rahman told
employees he had funding commitments sufficient to keep Jawbone independent — a direct disagreement
with his own largest backer over the company's future, according to Fortune's reporting at the time.

## The Legal War With Fitbit
Around the same period, Jawbone picked a fight it would ultimately lose. Shortly after Fitbit filed
for its 2015 IPO, Jawbone sued Fitbit three separate times in under two months, alleging Fitbit had
systematically poached Jawbone talent and stolen roughly 300,000 confidential files' worth of trade
secrets. In 2016, an International Trade Commission judge ruled against Jawbone, finding no evidence
that any Jawbone trade secrets had actually been misappropriated or used in a Fitbit product. Running
in the opposite direction, Fitbit separately sued Jawbone for patent infringement — and Fitbit's own
asserted patents were ruled invalid in that proceeding. Both cases closed against their respective
plaintiffs. The two companies reached a global settlement in December 2017, months after Jawbone had
already shut down.

## The Criminal Case Nobody Expected
The Fitbit story had one more chapter. In 2018, federal prosecutors indicted six current or former
Fitbit employees — all of whom had previously worked at Jawbone between 2011 and 2015 — for knowingly
possessing Jawbone trade secrets after they left to join Fitbit. This was a separate criminal case
against individuals, distinct from the earlier civil ITC ruling that had cleared Fitbit the company.
In February 2020, a jury acquitted one defendant, Katherine Mogal, on all charges; the government then
moved to dismiss the remaining charges against four of the other defendants. The case ended with no
convictions against any of the six.

## The Quiet Ending
On July 13, 2017, Jawbone — legally AliphCom, doing business as Jawbone — filed a Notice of Assignment
for the Benefit of Creditors, a California-specific alternative to formal bankruptcy in which a
company's assets are handed to a third-party fiduciary for liquidation. Jawbone never filed for
bankruptcy in court. Sherwood Partners, the firm appointed to administer the wind-down, sold off
remaining wearable inventory and took over the tail end of the Fitbit litigation.

## What Happened To Hosain Rahman
Immediately after the liquidation, Rahman launched a successor company, Jawbone Health Hub, later
continued as All.health — moving into health-data and biometric technology. BlackRock, the same firm
that had held Jawbone's $300 million debt position, reportedly took a stake in the new company. In
January 2023, an investor, Polymath Holdings, sued All.health, Rahman, and co-founder Michael Luna in
San Francisco Superior Court, alleging fraud, misrepresentation, and breach of contract — characterized
in the complaint, per Bloomberg's reporting, as a "classic 'fake-it-until-you-make-it' tale of fraud."
All.health, Rahman, and Luna have explicitly and publicly denied every claim. As of this research pass,
the case remains unresolved — no confirmed final ruling or settlement was found. This is the only open
thread in this channel's entire catalog: every other package here has a confirmed final chapter. This
one doesn't, yet.

## What Made This Different
Unlike this channel's Fast video (a six-day collapse) or its Olive AI video (an 18-month sold-for-parts
wind-down), Jawbone's failure doesn't trace to one bad call. It's the accumulation of three
simultaneously ambitious product bets, a debt position dressed up as an investment, a legal fight the
company itself started and lost twice over, and finally a quiet filing that most consumers who owned a
Jambox or an UP band never even heard about.

## The Actual Lesson: What "Overfunded" Actually Means
Raising more money than almost anyone else in your category doesn't automatically translate into a
winning strategy — sometimes it buys you enough runway to avoid making the hard choice a leaner
company would have been forced into years earlier. Jawbone had the capital to compete in headsets,
speakers, and fitness trackers all at once for years. A company with a fraction of that funding would
likely have had to pick one lane much sooner — and might have survived by doing so.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **More funding buys time, not focus** — three good product bets pursued simultaneously can still
  add up to one company that never wins any single market decisively
- **Read the fine print on "investment" language** — a $300 million check described publicly as an
  investment turned out, per later reporting, to be structured as debt, with real consequences for
  who controls the company's fate when things get hard
- **Winning a lawsuit isn't guaranteed just because you filed it first** — Jawbone was the aggressor
  in its legal war with Fitbit and still lost the case it started
- **A quiet liquidation filing can be just as final as a headline bankruptcy** — Jawbone's actual end
  came through a mechanism (an Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors) most people have never heard
  of, not a dramatic court filing

## The Lesson
Jawbone is proof that the most funding in your category doesn't guarantee an exit. $929.9 million,
three hit products, and a $3.3 billion valuation still ended in a quiet liquidation filing — and a
legal fight against its biggest rival that the company itself started, and still lost.
