FOUNDING

- Founded 1999 as AliphCom (later Aliph, Inc.) by Hosain Rahman and Alexander Asseily, both
  Stanford graduates (Rahman: mechanical engineering).
- Original focus: military-derived noise-cancellation voice technology, later dubbed
  "NoiseAssassin" — a headset that could filter background noise for soldiers in combat. Won a
  2002 DARPA (Pentagon research arm) contract.
- First consumer product: the original Jawbone Bluetooth headset, launched 2006, built on the
  military-derived noise-cancellation tech.
- Later created two more product categories: the Jambox line of wireless Bluetooth speakers, and
  the UP line of wrist-worn fitness/activity trackers (UP, UP24, UP3, UP4).
Source: Britannica Money (Hosain Rahman profile), Wikipedia "Jawbone (company)", Sequoia Capital's
own "Crucible Moments: Jawbone" podcast/retrospective. Founding year, founders, and military-tech
origin corroborated across at least 3 independent sources.

FUNDING — total lifetime figure and peak valuation

- Total lifetime funding: $929.9M raised over Jawbone's 15+ year existence — the figure most
  consistently cited across CNBC and CB Insights, and the basis for CNBC's own July 2017 headline
  characterization: "Jawbone's demise a case of 'death by overfunding' in Silicon Valley." Use this
  figure as the anchor lifetime-funding number.
  *** [VERIFY] flag: one Fortune piece (on the 2015 BlackRock deal) states Jawbone "had raised more
  than $400 million during its 16-year existence" at that specific point in time (2015) — this is
  NOT a lifetime-total contradiction, it's an earlier-dated snapshot before the 2014 $250M round and
  the 2015 BlackRock $300M debt round are added; the two figures are compatible once dated correctly,
  but do not present $400M as a lifetime total in any file — always use $929.9M for "total ever
  raised" and date any $400M figure explicitly if used at all. ***
- February 13, 2014: Jawbone closes a $250M round led by Rizvi Traverse Management, valuing the
  company at $3.3 billion. TechCrunch/CNBC both confirm this figure at the time. By September 2014,
  additional closing brought the valuation to a reported $3.2–3.3B range — treat "$3.3 billion,
  February 2014" as Jawbone's peak, primary-sourced valuation.
  Source: CNBC ("Jawbone raises $250 million, valuing company at $3.3 billion," Feb 13 2014),
  TechCrunch, Tech City News.
- 2015: BlackRock, the world's largest asset manager, invests $300M in Jawbone. Originally reported
  publicly as an equity infusion. Bloomberg later reported — based on California state filings — that
  the $300M was actually structured as DEBT (a loan), not new equity. Use "BlackRock's $300M debt/
  loan position," not "BlackRock's $300M investment," per the corrected Bloomberg reporting.
  Source: Time ("Jawbone: BlackRock's $300 Million Investment May Have Been Loan"), Fortune (Feb 27
  2015, "Jawbone nears big investment from BlackRock").
Source overall: CNBC, CB Insights financials page, Time, Fortune, TechCrunch — cross-corroborated
across independent outlets.

- By October 2016, BlackRock — holding the $300M debt position — was reportedly pushing for a sale
  of Jawbone. CEO Hosain Rahman told employees he had funding commitments sufficient to keep the
  company independent, but a larger investor constituency (BlackRock) was pushing to sell instead.
  *** This is Fortune's own reporting and characterization of the internal dynamic — attribute it to

THE FITBIT LITIGATION — two SEPARATE, opposite-direction cases; keep them distinct

1) Jawbone v. Fitbit (trade secrets/talent poaching) — Jawbone was the plaintiff here.
   - Shortly after Fitbit filed for its 2015 IPO, Jawbone filed three separate lawsuits against
     Fitbit in under two months, alleging Fitbit had "systematically plundered" Jawbone talent and
     stolen roughly 300,000 confidential files' worth of intellectual property/trade secrets via
     departing employees.
   - 2016: an International Trade Commission (ITC) judge ruled AGAINST Jawbone on this claim, finding
     "no Jawbone trade secrets were misappropriated or used in any Fitbit product, feature, or
     technology." This closed the corporate-misappropriation claim in Fitbit's favor.
   Source: AppleInsider, Wareable, Inc.com (all reporting the 2016 ITC ruling).

2) Fitbit v. Jawbone (patent infringement) — Fitbit was the plaintiff here, the reverse direction.
   - Fitbit separately sued Jawbone at the ITC over three patents (a wearable heart-rate monitor,
     portable monitoring devices, and motion-detector-based activity monitoring), seeking to block
     import of Jawbone's products.
   - An ITC judge (Thomas Prender) ruled the underlying ideas were not patent-eligible and recommended
     the case be thrown out "in its entirety" — Fitbit's patents were effectively invalidated in this
     proceeding. Fitbit also separately dropped one of its own ITC cases against Jawbone before a
     final ruling.
   Source: IT Pro ("Fitbit patents ruled invalid in Jawbone lawsuit"), Bloomberg (Apr 29 2016,
   "Fitbit Wins Ruling Knocking Out Jawbone Patents" — note headline nuance: Fitbit "won" procedurally
   by getting an ITC import-ban case dismissed, but its own asserted patents were the ones ruled
   invalid), Courthouse News Service, TechCrunch.

3) December 2017: Fitbit and Jawbone reached a GLOBAL SETTLEMENT resolving all outstanding
   litigation between the two companies (both directions) — filed after Jawbone had already entered
   liquidation (see below); Sherwood Partners (Jawbone's court-appointed liquidation administrator)
   handled Jawbone's side of the settlement.
   Source: Suiter Swantz IP summary, Courthouse News Service.

4) SEPARATE, LATER, CRIMINAL case — do not conflate with the two civil ITC cases above:
   - June 2018: federal prosecutors (US Attorney's Office, Northern District of California) indicted
     SIX current or former Fitbit employees — Katherine Mogal, Ana Rosario, Patrick Narron, Patricio
     Romano, Rong Zhang, and Jing Qi Weiden — for knowingly possessing Jawbone trade secrets after
     leaving Jawbone (where all six had worked between 2011–2015) to join Fitbit. This is a criminal
     case against INDIVIDUALS for possession, not a civil case against Fitbit the company for use —
     a materially different claim from the 2016 ITC ruling above; state this distinction on screen if
     using this case, since a casual viewer could otherwise think it contradicts the earlier ITC
     ruling.
   - February 3, 2020: a trial jury returned NOT GUILTY verdicts on all charges against defendant
     Katherine Mogal.
   - February 14, 2020: the U.S. government filed a motion to DISMISS the remaining criminal trade-
     secret charges against Patrick Narron, Jing Qi Weiden, Patricio Romano, and Rong Zhang.
   - Headline takeaway: the criminal case ended with NO convictions against any of the six defendants.
     *** [VERIFY] flag: this research pass did not independently confirm the specific final
     disposition for the sixth defendant, Ana Rosario, beyond the original 2018 indictment — do not
     state her case was dismissed or resolved a specific way; if her name is used at all, note her
     final case outcome as unconfirmed in this pass. ***
   Source: Forbes (Jun 16 2018, "Six Former Or Current Fitbit Employees Charged With Possessing
   Jawbone Trade Secrets"), DOJ USAO-NDCA press release, Axios, The Hustle.

THE LIQUIDATION (2017) — not a formal bankruptcy

- July 13, 2017: Jawbone (legally AliphCom, dba Jawbone) filed a Notice of Assignment for the Benefit
  of Creditors (ABC) — a California-specific alternative to formal Chapter 7/11 bankruptcy in which a
  company assigns its assets to a third-party fiduciary for liquidation and creditor distribution.
  Jawbone did NOT file for bankruptcy in court; it went through this ABC process instead.
  *** Framing note: an ABC is a real, distinct legal mechanism from bankruptcy — do not describe this
  as "filed for bankruptcy" anywhere in the package; use "entered liquidation via an assignment for
  the benefit of creditors" or "wound down its assets outside of bankruptcy court." ***
- Sherwood Partners was the firm appointed to administer the wind-down, liquidate remaining assets
  (including UP2/UP3/UP4 wearable inventory, sold to a third-party reseller), and it also assumed
  control of the ongoing Fitbit litigation on Jawbone's behalf (see above, item 3).
Source: Dezeen (Jul 11 2017, "Technology company Jawbone goes into liquidation"), Wolf Street/
CNBC (Jul 10 2017, "death by overfunding" piece), tms-outsource retrospective.

WHAT MAKES THIS DIFFERENT FROM THIS CHANNEL'S OTHER PACKAGES

- Scale: $929.9M lifetime raised is the largest funding total on this channel for a company that
  ultimately failed outright (higher than Bird's ~$700M+, Hopin's ~$1B is comparable but Hopin sold
  for real consideration in two deals rather than liquidating with no buyer).
- Mechanism: unlike 022 (Fast, a 6-day collapse) or 023 (Munchery, one bankruptcy filing naming
  causes), Jawbone's ending is a slow-motion, multi-year overfunding-and-overextension story — three
  simultaneous hardware product lines (headsets, speakers, wearables), a peak $3.3B valuation, a
  board fight with its own largest creditor (BlackRock) over whether to sell, and finally a quiet
  ABC liquidation rather than a headline bankruptcy filing.
- The Fitbit rivalry adds a rare structural element: TWO opposite-direction civil IP lawsuits (both
  resolved against the plaintiff in each case) PLUS a separate later criminal case against Fitbit
  employees that ended in zero convictions — a legal battle Jawbone effectively lost every stage of,
  even though it was Fitbit's employees who ended up criminally investigated.
- The unresolved coda (the active Polymath Holdings lawsuit against Rahman's successor company) is
  the only genuinely open, unresolved thread on this entire channel — every other package's story has
  a confirmed ending. State this explicitly on screen rather than implying resolution.
