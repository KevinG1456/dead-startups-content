## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Jawbone, the company that raised more money than almost any hardware startup in history — and still went out of business.

Founded in 1999 in San Francisco as AliphCom by Stanford graduates Hosain Rahman and Alexander Asseily, the company started with military-derived noise-cancellation voice technology, including a 2002 DARPA contract. Its first consumer product, the Jawbone Bluetooth headset, launched in 2006. From there, it expanded into two more categories at once — the Jambox line of wireless speakers and the UP line of wrist-worn fitness trackers, competing directly with Fitbit.

Funding climbed fast: a $250 million round led by Rizvi Traverse Management in February 2014 valued Jawbone at $3.3 billion, its peak. In 2015, BlackRock — the world's largest asset manager — put in $300 million, publicly described as an investment. Bloomberg later reported, based on California filings, that it was actually structured as debt. By 2016, BlackRock was reportedly pushing for a sale; CEO Hosain Rahman told employees he had commitments to stay independent.

Around the same time, Jawbone picked a legal fight it would lose. Shortly after Fitbit's 2015 IPO filing, Jawbone sued Fitbit three times in under two months, alleging the theft of roughly 300,000 confidential files' worth of trade secrets. In 2016, an ITC judge ruled against Jawbone — no misappropriation was found. Fitbit's own patent case against Jawbone also failed, with Fitbit's asserted patents ruled invalid. A global settlement closed both cases in December 2017. Separately, a 2018 federal criminal case charged six Fitbit employees with possessing Jawbone trade secrets — it ended in 2020 with one acquittal and the remaining charges dismissed.

On July 13, 2017, Jawbone quietly wound down — not through a bankruptcy filing, but through an Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors, administered by Sherwood Partners. Hosain Rahman immediately launched a successor company, Jawbone Health Hub, later continued as All.health, which BlackRock also took a stake in. As of this video, that successor company faces an active, unresolved lawsuit from investor Polymath Holdings alleging fraud — which Rahman, his co-founder, and the company explicitly deny.

Jawbone's real lesson: raising more money than almost anyone in your category doesn't buy you a winning strategy — it can buy you enough time to avoid choosing one, right up until the money runs out.
