# Script Package

Anchored on Jawbone — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. Anchor the $929.9M lifetime-funding
figure and the $3.3B peak valuation (Feb 2014, Rizvi Traverse-led round) as the two headline
numbers. Present BlackRock's 2015 $300M as a "loan/debt position," not an "investment," per
Bloomberg's corrected reporting. Keep the two Fitbit ITC civil cases directionally distinct
(Jawbone sued Fitbit and lost the trade-secrets claim; Fitbit sued Jawbone and its patents were
ruled invalid). The 2018 criminal case against six Fitbit employees is separate from those two
civil cases — ended in one acquittal and dismissed charges against four others; Ana Rosario's
individual outcome is unconfirmed, don't state a resolution for her. The 2017 wind-down was an
Assignment for the Benefit of Creditors, not a bankruptcy filing. The Polymath Holdings lawsuit
against Rahman's successor company (All.health) is ONGOING/UNRESOLVED — attribute every claim to
the lawsuit, always pair with the explicit denial, never state as fact. Never use "fraud," "scam,"
or "lied" as this channel's own conclusion about Hosain Rahman.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 14–17 minutes

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company raised $929.9 million. It had three hit products. And it still went out of
  business — quietly, with no bankruptcy filing at all."
- Visual: a giant "$929.9M" figure on screen, fracturing down in three descending steps toward an
  empty outline
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to black, white text: "This is the most-funded startup failure of
  the entire wearable tech era."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Jawbone — the company that invented the mainstream Bluetooth headset, then built
  the wireless speaker everyone owned, then built a fitness tracker to compete with Fitbit. All
  three, at the same time. They raised more money doing it than almost any hardware startup in
  history — and then picked a legal fight with their biggest rival that backfired completely."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how a company with three hit
  products and nearly a billion dollars in funding still ran out of road, why the lawsuit they
  started against Fitbit made things worse, not better, and why this story still isn't fully
  over."

### Introduction (1:00–1:50)
- Method statement: reconstructed from CNBC's and CB Insights' funding data, primary-sourced
  funding-round reporting (CNBC, TechCrunch), Bloomberg and Fortune's reporting on the BlackRock
  relationship, ITC and DOJ court records on the Fitbit litigation, and Bloomberg's 2023 reporting
  on the still-active Polymath Holdings lawsuit — sourced on screen throughout
- One-sentence framing: this is the channel's clearest case study in overextension — not one bad
  bet, but three good ones made at the same time, without the funding discipline to sustain any of
  them

### Main Content — The Jawbone Case (1:50–11:00)
- Segment 1 — The Origin (1:50–3:00): founded 1999 in San Francisco as AliphCom by Stanford
  graduates Hosain Rahman and Alexander Asseily, originally building military-derived
  noise-cancellation voice technology — a 2002 DARPA contract to help soldiers communicate in
  combat noise. Visual: AliphCom logo, DARPA insignia (generic, not official use), timeline marker
  at 2002.
- Segment 2 — Three Product Lines, One Company (3:00–4:30): 2006, the first consumer Jawbone
  Bluetooth headset launches, built on the military-derived tech. Then the Jambox wireless speaker
  line. Then the UP wristband fitness tracker line — going head-to-head with Fitbit. On-screen
  note: "Three separate hardware categories, three separate competitive markets, one company."
  Visual: three product silhouettes appearing one at a time.
- Segment 3 — The Climb to $3.3 Billion (4:30–5:45): February 2014, a $250M round led by Rizvi
  Traverse Management values Jawbone at $3.3 billion — its peak. On-screen note: "$929.9 million
  raised over the company's lifetime — the most of any hardware startup on this channel." Visual:
  valuation graphic climbing toward $3.3B.
- Segment 4 — The Loan Disguised As an Investment (5:45–7:00): 2015, BlackRock — the world's
  largest asset manager — puts in $300 million, publicly described as an investment. Bloomberg
  later reports, based on California filings, that it was actually structured as debt. On-screen
  note: "Not an equity investment — a loan." By 2016, BlackRock is reportedly pushing for a sale;
  CEO Hosain Rahman tells employees he has commitments to stay independent (Fortune's reporting).
  Visual: "$300M INVESTMENT" text on screen, then a stamp overlay reading "ACTUALLY: A LOAN."
- Segment 5 — The Legal War With Fitbit (7:00–9:30): shortly after Fitbit's 2015 IPO filing,
  Jawbone files three lawsuits against Fitbit in under two months, alleging systematic poaching of
  talent and roughly 300,000 confidential files' worth of stolen trade secrets. 2016: an ITC judge
  rules against Jawbone — no misappropriation found. Meanwhile, Fitbit's own patent-infringement
  case against Jawbone results in Fitbit's asserted patents being ruled invalid. Both cases close
  against their respective plaintiffs; a global settlement follows in December 2017 — after
  Jawbone had already shut down. On-screen scoreboard: "JAWBONE SUED FITBIT → LOST." / "FITBIT
  SUED JAWBONE → ALSO LOST." Visual: two-column legal scoreboard graphic.
- Segment 6 — The Criminal Case Nobody Expected (9:30–10:15): 2018, federal prosecutors indict six
  current or former Fitbit employees for possessing Jawbone trade secrets after leaving Jawbone to
  join Fitbit — a separate criminal case, not the same as the civil rulings above. February 2020:
  a jury acquits one defendant (Katherine Mogal); the government dismisses the remaining charges
  against four others. On-screen note: "Ended with zero convictions." Visual: six silhouette
  figures, five fading with a checkmark, one flagged "outcome not confirmed" (Ana Rosario, per
  sources-log.txt).
- Segment 7 — The Quiet Ending (10:15–11:00): July 13, 2017, Jawbone files a Notice of Assignment
  for the Benefit of Creditors — not a bankruptcy filing, a different legal mechanism entirely —
  and Sherwood Partners takes over the wind-down and the remaining Fitbit litigation. On-screen
  note: "No bankruptcy filing. Just a quiet handoff to a liquidation firm." Visual: the "$929.9M"
  figure from the cold open, now an empty outline.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:00): "If you want to see exactly how a legal fight this company started ended
  up backfiring completely, the scoreboard is coming up right after this. Subscribe for a new
  autopsy every week."

### The Unresolved Coda — What Happened To Hosain Rahman (11:00–12:45)
- Immediately after the 2017 liquidation, Rahman launches a successor, Jawbone Health Hub — later
  continued as All.health — and BlackRock, the same firm holding Jawbone's debt, takes a stake in
  it. On-screen note: "Hosain Rahman has never been charged or convicted of anything."
- January 2023: an investor, Polymath Holdings, sues All.health, Rahman, and co-founder Michael
  Luna, alleging fraud and breach of contract. On-screen lower third, held on screen for the full
  duration of this beat: "This is one investor's civil lawsuit — not a criminal charge, not a
  ruling. All.health, Rahman, and Luna have explicitly denied every claim." Case status, stated
  plainly: unresolved as of this video.
- Visual: a small, still-open case-file icon — deliberately left "open," not checked off, to match
  the honest unresolved status.

### The Actual Lesson — What "Overfunded" Actually Means (12:45–14:30)
- Plain-language comparison: unlike 022 (Fast, a six-day collapse) or 024 (Olive AI, an 18-month
  sold-for-parts wind-down), Jawbone's failure isn't about one bad decision — it's about having
  enough capital to compete in three separate hardware categories at once, and never having to
  choose just one before the money ran out.
- The practical distinction: nearly a billion dollars of funding didn't just fail to save Jawbone —
  it may have actively delayed the hard choice that could have saved it, by making three
  simultaneously mediocre product lines feel survivable for years longer than a leaner company
  could have afforded.
- Visual: side-by-side comparison graphic — Fast (6 days, $124.5M), Olive AI (18 months, ~$900M),
  Jawbone (years, $929.9M) — landing on "different timelines, same lesson: the money bought time,
  not a plan."

### Final CTA + Outro (14:30–16:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a company can raise more money than almost anyone in its
  category, build three genuinely successful product lines, and still lose, because funding buys
  time, not focus
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want to see what happens when a
  company runs the opposite play — one clear bet instead of three — the Fast video is a sharp
  contrast"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you own a Jambox or an UP band? Which product do you think Jawbone should
  have bet everything on instead? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 024 (Olive AI) + most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** Segment 1 (the AliphCom/DARPA origin story) if it runs too long before the
  product lines and funding numbers appear
- **Fix:** keep the origin segment under 70 seconds and get to the three-product-lines setup
  quickly — the scale and rivalry hooks are what's pulling viewers in
- **Slow section risk:** the Fitbit legal segment (Segment 5) could feel procedurally dense with
  two separate ITC cases
- **Fix:** keep the two-column "who sued whom, who lost" scoreboard graphic on screen for the
  entire segment so viewers never lose track of direction
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the "this founder is being sued again, right now" coda
  early (in the Hook) and pay it off fully at 11:00
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a shrinking "$929.9M" figure visible in a corner throughout
  the second half, visually counting down as each product line and legal case closes
