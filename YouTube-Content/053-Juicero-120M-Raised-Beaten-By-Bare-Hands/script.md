~$120M — do not force a single exact figure. Do NOT present a "Series C" round as real — it's very
likely a Crunchbase data-duplicate of the Series B. The Bloomberg hand-squeeze test numbers are exact:
7.5 oz by hand in ~90 seconds vs. 8 oz from the machine — nearly identical, state precisely. Do NOT use
the unverified "$32 million R&D for the packets alone" figure. Tone: comedic/absurd is well-earned and
appropriate for this story — unlike most packages on this channel — but every number, date, and quote
must stay exactly as sourced.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11–13 minutes (founding, funding rocket, the video, the fallout, and the complete
redemption coda all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:20)
- VO: "A hundred and twenty million dollars. And your bare hands could do what the machine did."
- Visual: split screen — sleek machine on one side, a bare hand on the other, both producing a stream of
  juice at roughly the same time
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to black, white text: "This actually happened. On camera. In 2017."

### Hook (0:20–1:05)
- VO: "This is Juicero — a $699 WiFi-connected juicer that raised roughly $120 million from some of the
  smartest investors in Silicon Valley, before one ninety-second video ended it."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll know exactly how this got funded, exactly what happened
  in that video, and exactly what the founder did next — including a real second act almost nobody
  follows up on."

### Introduction (1:05–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from TechCrunch, Bloomberg, Fortune, CNN Business, Forbes, and
  Crunchbase News — sourced on screen throughout
- Framing note, stated early: "This one's genuinely funny. It's also a real lesson in what happens when
  you engineer a very impressive solution before you check whether anyone needed it."

### Main Content — The Build-Up (1:40–5:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Founder Who'd Done This Before (1:40–2:20):** Doug Evans co-founded Organic Avenue
  in 2002, a cold-pressed juice bar chain that grew to about 10 NYC stores and sold in 2012. Founds
  Juicero in San Francisco in 2013 to bring "farm to glass" juice into the home. Visual: generic juice-
  bar-to-startup-office transition graphic.
- **Segment 2 — The Funding Rocket (2:20–3:30):** $4M Seed (Oct 2013) → $15.8M Series A (Apr 2014) →
  $70M Series B (Mar 2016), led by Artis Ventures, with Kleiner Perkins, Google Ventures, Campbell Soup
  Company, Thrive Capital, and Carmelo Anthony's fund all participating — valuation over $270 million.
  ~$118.5 million total, commonly rounded to ~$120 million. Visual: funding-rounds bar chart, ascending.
- **Segment 3 — What $120 Million Actually Built (3:30–4:30):** the Juicero Press launches March 2016 at
  $699 — a WiFi-connected machine exerting four tons of force to squeeze proprietary, single-serving
  "Produce Packs" of pre-chopped fruit and vegetables, each with a QR code the machine had to verify
  online before dispensing, officially for freshness/expiration safety. Packs sold on subscription, $5-7
  each. Visual: "$699 — FOUR TONS OF FORCE — WIFI REQUIRED" title card.
- **Segment 4 — The First CEO Change (4:30–5:00):** October 31, 2016 — seven months after launch, Doug
  Evans steps down as CEO. Jeff Dunn, previously president of Coca-Cola North America, takes over.
  Visual: "NEW CEO — OCT 2016" title card.

### Main Content — The Video (5:00–7:45)
- **Segment 5 — The Price Cut That Wasn't Enough (5:00–5:35):** January 17, 2017 — price cut from $699
  to $399, moved up 12-18 months ahead of schedule after a Black Friday test showed a sub-$400 price
  doubled signups in a single day. Visual: "$699 → $399" title card.
- **Segment 6 — The Ninety Seconds That Ended It (5:35–7:00):** April 19, 2017, Bloomberg publishes a
  video showing Produce Packs squeezed entirely by hand — 7.5 ounces of juice in about 90 seconds,
  against 8 ounces from the $399 machine. Nearly identical. Two Juicero investors told Bloomberg they
  were surprised; one, Doug Chertok, is quoted directly: "There is no doubt the packs can be squeezed
  without the machine." Visual: the split-screen hand-vs-machine demonstration from the cold open,
  replayed in full with the real numbers on screen — "7.5 OZ BY HAND (90 SEC) vs. 8 OZ BY MACHINE."
- **Segment 7 — The Response (7:00–7:45):** CEO Jeff Dunn publicly defends the machine's freshness-
  verification function, then within days offers 30-day refunds: "if you send us your Press, we'll
  refund the money you paid for it." On-screen framing card: "Nobody had lied about what the machine
  did. It just turned out nobody needed it to do it." Visual: refund-offer title card.

### Main Content — The End (7:45–9:45)
- **Segment 8 — The Layoffs (7:45–8:15):** July 14, 2017 — 25% of staff cut, mostly sales and marketing,
  alongside plans for a cheaper second-generation machine (reportedly ~$200, never officially confirmed).
  Visual: "25% OF STAFF — JUL 2017" title card.
- **Segment 9 — The Shutdown (8:15–9:15):** September 1, 2017 — sixteen months after launch, Juicero
  suspends sales of the machine and the packs entirely, offers to buy back remaining machines from
  customers, and searches for a buyer for the company and its intellectual property. No buyer is found.
  The company winds down completely. Visual: "SEPT 1, 2017 — 16 MONTHS AFTER LAUNCH" title card. Mid-
  video CTA folded in here (~9:00): "This is one of the fastest, funniest collapses I've covered on this
  channel — and it's not even the most interesting part of the story. Subscribe for a new autopsy every
  week."
- **Segment 10 — The Redemption Coda (9:15–9:45):** Doug Evans later founds The Sprouting Company, a
  subscription home-sprouting business. October 2025: pitches on Shark Tank, reportedly growing from
  about $200,000/month before the episode to a roughly $12 million annualized run rate by early 2026 —
  this time building the subscription in from day one, no hardware dependency required. Visual: "THE
  SPROUTING COMPANY — ~$12M RUN RATE, 2026" title card.

### The Actual Lesson (9:45–11:15)
- Plain-language comparison: the engineering here wasn't fake or dishonest — the machine did exactly
  what it claimed to do. The mistake was building an enormously sophisticated solution to a problem
  (extracting juice from a pre-chopped, pre-portioned packet) that turned out to have an almost trivially
  simple existing solution: a hand.
- The core lesson, stated directly: sophistication is not the same thing as necessity, and the more
  impressive the engineering, the more important it becomes to test the simplest possible alternative
  before scaling around the complicated one.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2013) → funding rounds (2013-2016) → launch (Mar 2016) →
  CEO change (Oct 2016) → price cut (Jan 2017) → the video (Apr 2017) → layoffs (Jul 2017) → shutdown
  (Sept 2017) → The Sprouting Company (2020s-2025).

### Final CTA + Outro (11:15–12:15)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — impressive engineering isn't the same as a necessary solution, and
  the simplest possible alternative deserves to be tested before you scale around the complicated one
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this might be the single funniest, most
  self-contained collapse I've covered, start to finish"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you remember the hand-squeeze video before watching this — or did you only
  know the punchline? And did you know what the founder built next? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the funding-rounds bar chart and the full-arc timeline, then works through the launch,
the video, and the shutdown as supporting detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a
genuine case study in engineering-versus-necessity risk rather than pure comedy.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Doug Evans building Organic Avenue in a NYC loft in 2002, then cuts hard to the 2025 Shark Tank
pitch before rewinding to fill in everything in between. Slower pacing, holds the Bloomberg video reveal
until Segment 6, resolving with the redemption coda as the closing beat rather than a footnote.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Comedy Style
Structured as "one hand, ninety seconds, four years of consequences," leading into rapid-fire beats:
founding, funding, launch specs (four tons of force! WiFi! QR codes!), price cut, the video, the refund
offer, layoffs, shutdown — landing on the redemption coda as the final, unexpectedly warm beat. Strong
Shorts-extraction potential, especially the hand-vs-machine demonstration and the Doug Chertok quote.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the funding-rounds segment (2:20–3:30) if it feels like a dry list of dollar figures
- **Fix:** frame each round as "and still nobody asked the simplest question" — connects forward to the
  Bloomberg reveal's payoff
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general Silicon-Valley-hype context if audience familiarity with
  the general story is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the redemption coda even more explicitly in the cold open if
  early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 10 (9:15)
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the hand-vs-machine split screen from the cold open as a recurring
  visual motif, replaying the full demonstration with real numbers at Segment 6 for maximum payoff
