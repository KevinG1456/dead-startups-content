# Blog Article: $120 Million Raised. Your Bare Hands Could Do What the Machine Did.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, Bloomberg, Fortune, CNN Business, Forbes, and Crunchbase News — see
`sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Total funding is stated as ~$118.5 million, commonly
rounded to ~$120 million, rather than forced to a single exact figure. The unverified "$32 million R&D
for the packets alone" figure some retellings repeat is not used here.*

## Introduction
Juicero is one of the most frequently cited "worst startup ideas" in Silicon Valley history. Most
versions of the story stop at the punchline: a company raised roughly $120 million to build a machine
that a Bloomberg reporter beat with their bare hands. That punchline is real — but the complete story,
including who built it, why it made a strange kind of sense at the time, and what its founder did next,
is more interesting than the joke alone.

## The Founder Who'd Done This Before
Doug Evans wasn't a first-time founder chasing a trend. In 2002, he co-founded Organic Avenue, a cold-
pressed organic juice bar chain that grew to roughly 10 retail stores in New York City before selling in
2012. When Organic Avenue sold, Evans lost access to the fresh-pressed juice he'd built his daily habit
around — and that gap, by his own later account, is what led directly to Juicero, founded in San
Francisco in 2013.

## The Funding Rocket
Juicero's pitch attracted serious money. A $4 million seed round in October 2013 grew to a $15.8 million
Series A in April 2014, then a $70 million Series B in March 2016 — led by Artis Ventures, with Kleiner
Perkins, Google Ventures, Campbell Soup Company, Thrive Capital, and even NBA star Carmelo Anthony's
investment fund all participating. Total lifetime funding reached roughly $118.5 million, commonly
rounded to about $120 million, at a valuation reported above $270 million.

## What $120 Million Actually Built
The Juicero Press launched the same month as the Series B closed: March 2016, priced at $699. It was a
genuinely sophisticated piece of engineering — a WiFi-connected countertop machine exerting four tons of
force to squeeze proprietary, single-serving "Produce Packs" of pre-chopped fruit and vegetables. Each
pack carried a QR code the machine had to verify over an active internet connection before it would
dispense, officially framed as a food-safety measure to prevent expired produce from being used. Packs
sold on subscription, $5-7 each.

## The First CEO Change
Seven months after launch, on October 31, 2016, Doug Evans stepped down as CEO. Jeff Dunn, previously
president of Coca-Cola North America, took over — a signal that Juicero's leadership believed the
company needed a different kind of operator to scale.

## The Price Cut That Wasn't Enough
On January 17, 2017, Juicero cut the Press's price from $699 to $399 — reportedly 12 to 18 months ahead
of its originally planned schedule, after a Black Friday 2016 test showed that pricing under $400 doubled
new signups in a single day.

## The Ninety Seconds That Ended It
Then, on April 19, 2017, Bloomberg published the story that made Juicero a permanent fixture of startup-
failure lore. Reporters squeezed a Produce Pack entirely by hand — no machine — and got 7.5 ounces of
juice in about 90 seconds. The $399 machine produced 8 ounces. Nearly identical results, with nothing
more sophisticated than a human hand required.

Two of Juicero's own investors told Bloomberg they were surprised to learn this. One, Doug Chertok, is
quoted directly and unambiguously: "There is no doubt the packs can be squeezed without the machine."

## The Response
CEO Jeff Dunn publicly defended the machine, pointing to its WiFi-based freshness-verification function
as a real value it still provided. Within days, Juicero announced a 30-day refund window: "if you send
us your Press, we'll refund the money you paid for it." Nobody at Juicero had lied about what the machine
did — it really did exert four tons of force, it really did verify freshness over WiFi. The problem was
that none of that turned out to be necessary to get juice out of the packet.

## The Layoffs, and the End
On July 14, 2017, Juicero cut 25% of its staff, mostly in sales and marketing, alongside plans for a
cheaper second-generation machine reportedly targeted around $200 (never officially confirmed before the
company closed). It wasn't enough. On September 1, 2017 — sixteen months after launch — Juicero suspended
sales of both the machine and the packs entirely, offered to repurchase remaining machines from
customers, and searched for a buyer for the company and its intellectual property. No buyer was found.
The company wound down completely.

## The Redemption Coda
Most retellings of the Juicero story end at the shutdown. Doug Evans' story didn't. He later founded The
Sprouting Company, a subscription business built around fresh, nutrient-dense home-grown sprouts. In
October 2025, he pitched it on Shark Tank — and per contemporaneous business press, the company grew from
roughly $200,000 in monthly revenue before the episode aired to about $540,000 a month by December, and
was reportedly tracking over $1 million a month by around March 2026, a roughly $12 million annualized
run rate. This time, the subscription model was built in from day one, rather than layered onto a piece
of hardware nobody strictly needed.

## The Actual Lesson
Juicero's engineering wasn't dishonest, and it wasn't even bad. The machine did exactly what it claimed
to do. The mistake was building an enormously sophisticated solution — four tons of force, WiFi
connectivity, QR-code verification — for a problem that already had an almost trivially simple existing
solution: a hand. Sophistication is not the same thing as necessity, and the more impressive the
engineering behind a product, the more important it becomes to test the simplest possible alternative
before scaling spending, hiring, and manufacturing around the complicated one.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **Test the "dumb" alternative before you fund the sophisticated one** — nobody at Juicero, across three
  funding rounds and multiple rounds of due diligence, appears to have systematically tested whether the
  core mechanical function was even necessary
- **Impressive engineering can be a distraction from the actual question** — four tons of force and WiFi
  connectivity are genuinely hard engineering problems to solve well; solving them well doesn't answer
  whether they needed solving at all
- **A CEO change five months before a crisis doesn't insulate a company from it** — Jeff Dunn inherited
  the product decisions that led to the Bloomberg video, and had to manage the fallout regardless
- **A public failure doesn't have to be a founder's last chapter** — Doug Evans' Sprouting Company,
  reportedly on a ~$12 million run rate by 2026, is a genuinely well-documented second act

## The Lesson
Juicero's story is usually told as a punchline: $120 million, beaten by two hands. The fuller, more
useful version is about what happens when brilliant engineering gets applied to a question nobody
actually asked first — and about the founder who, years later, applied the lesson to something much
simpler.
