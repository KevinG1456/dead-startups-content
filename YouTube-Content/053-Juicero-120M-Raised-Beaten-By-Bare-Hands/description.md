## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Juicero —
one of the most mocked startups in Silicon Valley history, and a company whose complete story most
retellings never actually finish.

Doug Evans had already built one juice business: Organic Avenue, a cold-pressed juice bar chain he
co-founded in New York in 2002, which grew to roughly 10 retail stores before selling in 2012. In 2013,
he founded Juicero in San Francisco with a much more ambitious pitch — a WiFi-connected countertop press,
exerting four tons of force, that would squeeze proprietary single-serving "Produce Packs" of pre-chopped
fruit and vegetables. Each pack carried a QR code the machine had to verify over an active internet
connection before it would dispense, officially framed as a freshness and expiration safeguard.

Investors believed in it. A $4 million seed round in October 2013 grew to a $15.8 million Series A in
2014, then a $70 million Series B in March 2016 — led by Artis Ventures, with Kleiner Perkins, Google
Ventures, Campbell Soup Company, Thrive Capital, and even NBA star Carmelo Anthony all participating.
Total funding reached roughly $118.5 million, commonly rounded to about $120 million, valuing the
company at over $270 million. The Juicero Press launched the same month, March 2016, at $699.

On October 31, 2016, Doug Evans stepped down as CEO; Jeff Dunn, previously president of Coca-Cola North
America, took over. In January 2017, the price was cut to $399 after a Black Friday test showed a
sub-$400 price point doubled signups in a single day.

Then, on April 19, 2017, Bloomberg published a video that ended the company's credibility almost
overnight: reporters squeezed a Produce Pack entirely by hand, getting 7.5 ounces of juice in about 90
seconds — nearly identical to the 8 ounces the $399 machine produced. Two of Juicero's own investors told
Bloomberg they were surprised. One, Doug Chertok, said plainly: "There is no doubt the packs can be
squeezed without the machine." CEO Jeff Dunn defended the machine's freshness-verification function, then
offered 30-day refunds to any customer who mailed their Press back.

It wasn't enough. In July 2017, Juicero cut 25% of its staff. On September 1, 2017 — sixteen months after
launch — the company suspended sales entirely, offered to repurchase remaining machines, searched for a
buyer for the company and its intellectual property, found none, and shut down for good.

Here's the part most retellings skip: Doug Evans didn't disappear. He later founded The Sprouting
Company, a subscription home-sprouting business, and pitched it on Shark Tank in October 2025 —
reportedly growing from about $200,000 a month in revenue before the episode aired to a roughly $12
million annualized run rate by early 2026, building the subscription model in from the start this time.
