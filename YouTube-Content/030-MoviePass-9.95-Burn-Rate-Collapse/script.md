# Script Package

Anchored on MoviePass/Helios and Matheson — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST
IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS SCRIPT: Stacy Spikes (co-founded MoviePass 2011, fired January 2018 by
Helios and Matheson, never charged, later bought the brand back) must be kept completely and
repeatedly distinct from Ted Farnsworth (Helios and Matheson CEO), Mitch Lowe (installed as
MoviePass CEO), and Khalid Itum (MoviePass business-development executive named in the SEC's civil
action) throughout — never name Spikes in the same breath as the fraud-charged executives without
explicitly marking the separation. "Fraud" language is factually appropriate for Farnsworth/Lowe
specifically (Lowe has an actual guilty plea) but must never attach to Spikes. On Lowe's 2024 guilty
plea: state only the securities fraud conspiracy charge and the plea's stated maximum exposure (5
years) — do not state a specific sentence for Lowe or Farnsworth, and note Farnsworth's case status
was not confirmed as resolved in this research pass. On the 2018 "changed subscriber passwords"
detail: present as a well-corroborated, widely-reported pattern rather than citing one single
precise date. On the 2023-2024 relaunch figures ($14M ARR, first profitable year, 2.8M trips):
attribute explicitly as Spikes' own public statements about his own company, and note they're the
most recent figures found in research, not necessarily current. Cross-reference package 025
(Lyric/Black Swan Holdings) explicitly as the channel's other "brand survives the company" story —
note this one is the more complete, redemptive version since the ORIGINAL founder is who returned.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 15–18 minutes (founding, the $9.95-era collapse, the criminal case, and the
comeback all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "MoviePass charged $9.95 a month for unlimited movies. For over a year, it paid theaters close
  to full price for every single ticket its subscribers used. That's not a mistake. That was the
  strategy."
- Visual: large "$9.95/MONTH" text, hard cut to a stack of ticket-price icons visibly taller,
  a red "LOSS" stamp landing between them
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "The founder didn't make this
  decision. He was fired seven months before it launched."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is MoviePass — co-founded in 2011 by Stacy Spikes. In 2017, a data-analytics firm called
  Helios and Matheson bought a controlling 51% stake in the company for $25 million. In January
  2018, Spikes was fired by the new owners after disagreeing with the pricing direction they wanted
  to take. What came next — the $9.95 unlimited plan, the almost-3-million subscribers, the $329
  million-a-year losses, and eventually federal fraud charges against two of the executives who ran
  it — all happened after Spikes was already gone."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how this company lost money on
  purpose at scale, exactly who was later criminally charged and why — and exactly why that list
  does not include the person who started the company. Then we'll get to the part almost nobody
  covers: what happened when that same founder bought the wreckage back."

### Introduction (1:00–1:50)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, SEC filings (Helios and Matheson's 2017 424B3/
  424B5 forms and later financial disclosures), Inc.com (Spikes' own account), IndieWire, NBC News,
  Deadline, Bloomberg, Variety, Hollywood Reporter, SEC.gov litigation release LR-25525, and Fortune
  — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit who's-who framing, stated early and plainly: "Two groups of people ran MoviePass, at two
  different times, and they are not the same people. Stacy Spikes founded the company and was fired
  in January 2018. Ted Farnsworth and Mitch Lowe — installed by the new owners — ran the company
  through its collapse, and were later criminally charged. Keep those two groups separate as you
  watch this, because most coverage of this story doesn't."
- Cross-reference tease: "This channel has covered a brand surviving its own company's death before
  — Lyric, in package 025. MoviePass's version of that story is the more complete one, because here,
  the original founder is the one who came back."

### Main Content — The Rise, the Ouster, and the Burn (1:50–9:00)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:50–2:40):** MoviePass co-founded in 2011 by Stacy Spikes. Early
  years positioned as a niche subscription service for frequent moviegoers. Visual: founder graphic
  (generic avatar, not a rendered real likeness), early-era subscription-card mockup.
- **Segment 2 — The Acquisition and the Ouster (2:40–4:00):** 2017, Helios and Matheson Analytics
  acquired a 51% controlling stake in MoviePass for $25 million. January 2018: Spikes was fired by
  Helios and Matheson after disagreeing with the direction the new owners wanted to take the pricing
  model. On-screen framing card: "Spikes was OUT of the company before the $9.95 strategy was locked
  in and before the worst of the collapse happened." Visual: acquisition graphic, then a clean
  "FOUNDER OUT — JANUARY 2018" title card before the next segment begins.
- **Segment 3 — The $9.95 Plan (4:00–5:45):** Per Spikes' own later account, the $9.95-a-month price
  originated as what was initially framed as a short-term promotional move tied to the Helios and
  Matheson acquisition — not something Spikes himself intended to become the permanent price. Spikes
  has stated on record that he knew from the start the $9.95 rate would never be profitable at that
  price. Under Helios and Matheson's leadership — CEO Ted Farnsworth, with Mitch Lowe installed as
  MoviePass CEO — the $9.95 unlimited plan launched and was aggressively marketed starting August
  2017, driving explosive growth to almost 3 million subscribers at its peak. Visual: subscriber
  count climbing steeply, "$9.95 UNLIMITED — LAUNCHED AUGUST 2017" title card.
- **Segment 4 — The Math That Never Worked (5:45–7:00):** because MoviePass paid theaters close to
  full price for every ticket while charging subscribers a flat $9.95 regardless of how many movies
  they saw, heavy users generated massive losses per-subscriber — the core, deliberate unit-economics
  flaw. Helios and Matheson reported an estimated net loss of $329.2 million for the fiscal year
  covering the worst of the burn. Visual: side-by-side "$9.95 charged" vs. "actual per-ticket cost"
  comparison graphic, loss figure climbing to $329.2M.
- **Segment 5 — The Desperate Measures (7:00–8:00):** as losses mounted through 2018, the company
  took increasingly desperate measures to survive — reportedly changing subscriber passwords to block
  heavy users from using the service, alongside repeated outages. On-screen note: presented here as a
  well-established, widely-corroborated pattern from contemporaneous 2018 reporting, not tied to one
  single precise date. Subscriber count collapsed from almost 3 million at peak to approximately
  225,000 by April 2019. Visual: subscriber-count graph collapsing sharply, generic "PASSWORD
  CHANGED" login-lock icon.
- **Segment 6 — Shutdown and Bankruptcy (8:00–9:00):** September 14, 2019, MoviePass notified
  remaining subscribers it would shut down, stating its "efforts to recapitalize MoviePass have not
  been successful to date." January 28, 2020, Helios and Matheson Analytics filed a Chapter 7
  bankruptcy petition — full liquidation, not reorganization — in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the
  Southern District of New York. Visual: dated timeline, "CHAPTER 7 — LIQUIDATION" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~8:45): "If you've seen this channel's video on Lyric — package 025 — you already
  know this channel covers brand-outlives-the-company stories. Stay with this one, because MoviePass's
  version is about to get more interesting than most. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### Main Content — The Criminal Case (9:00–11:30)
- **Segment 7 — The Allegations (9:00–10:00):** the SEC and federal prosecutors alleged that from
  August 2017 through at least March 2019, Ted Farnsworth and Mitch Lowe repeatedly made materially
  false or misleading public statements about MoviePass's business model and path to profitability —
  and that when the true, dire finances became undeniable internally, they chose to covertly
  throttle and degrade the service for heavy users rather than disclose the problem publicly. A third
  executive, Khalid Itum — a MoviePass business-development executive — was also named in the SEC's
  civil action. On-screen framing card, held for a beat: "None of this involves Stacy Spikes. He was
  fired before any of this happened." Visual: three-name graphic with Farnsworth and Lowe clearly
  grouped together and Itum marked distinctly as the SEC civil-action defendant, Spikes' name kept
  visually and spatially separate with a "FIRED JAN 2018 — NOT INVOLVED" tag.
- **Segment 8 — The Charges and the Plea (10:00–11:30):** November 2022, Farnsworth and Lowe were
  criminally charged with wire fraud and securities fraud; the government estimated total investor
  and consumer losses from the scheme at approximately $303 million. In 2024, Mitch Lowe pleaded
  guilty to a securities fraud conspiracy charge, admitting he had conspired to deceive the public
  and investors about the sustainability of the $9.95-a-month unlimited plan — he faces a maximum
  sentence of five years in federal prison under the plea. On-screen disclosure card, held clearly:
  "This research did not confirm final sentencing for either man, or the current resolved/unresolved
  status of Farnsworth's case, as of this recording. Do not treat a specific sentence as fact — this
  is the maximum exposure under the plea, not a confirmed outcome." Visual: "GUILTY PLEA — 2024"
  title card with the [VERIFY] disclosure text held on screen for a full beat, not a quick flash.

### Main Content — The Comeback (11:30–15:30)
- **Segment 9 — Buying It Back (11:30–12:30):** 2021, Stacy Spikes — the ORIGINAL founder, fired in
  January 2018 and uninvolved in the $9.95-era executives' later conduct — acquired MoviePass's brand
  and remaining assets out of the Helios and Matheson bankruptcy proceeding. Visual: "2021 — SPIKES
  BUYS BACK THE BRAND" title card, connecting line back to the "FOUNDER OUT — 2018" card from
  Segment 2, visually closing the loop.
- **Segment 10 — Fixing the Flaw (12:30–14:00):** August 2022, Spikes announced MoviePass would
  relaunch under an entirely different model: three subscription tiers, NONE of them unlimited, with
  pricing varying by market — a direct structural correction of the exact flaw that killed the
  original company. Roughly 775,000 people joined a waitlist. September 5, 2022, the relaunched
  MoviePass began a beta rollout in Chicago, Dallas, and Kansas City. Visual: "NO UNLIMITED TIER"
  callout directly beside a small ghosted repeat of the Segment 4 "$9.95 vs. real cost" graphic, to
  visually show the fix landing on the exact original flaw.
- **Segment 11 — Where It Stands (14:00–15:30):** by the most recent reporting found in this
  research pass (early 2024), Spikes stated the relaunched service's users had collectively watched
  over 1 million movies since the relaunch, that 2023 was the company's first profitable year, and
  that the company had grown from $0 to $14 million in annual recurring revenue while powering 2.8
  million movie trips. On-screen attribution card, held clearly: "These are Stacy Spikes' own public
  statements about his own company — not independently audited figures. This is the most recent data
  found in this research pass, not necessarily current as of when you're watching this." Visual:
  growth-figure graphic with the attribution card locked to the same frame, never shown alone.
- Direct cross-reference beat: "This channel already told a version of this story with Lyric —
  package 025 — where the brand survived even though the original company didn't, revived by an
  unrelated buyer, Black Swan Holdings. MoviePass's ending is the more complete version of that same
  pattern: here, the person who came back to fix it is the same person who built it in the first
  place, and got fired trying to warn everyone what would happen if the price stuck."

### The Actual Lesson (15:30–16:30)
- Plain-language comparison: a company can grow explosively on a strategy that's actively losing it
  money, collapse under the weight of that same strategy, generate real federal fraud charges for
  the executives who ran it that way — and still, separately, have a real, positive, corrected
  second act under the person who tried to prevent the first one from happening.
- The core irony, stated directly: the person removed for objecting to the pricing decision that
  destroyed the company was later proven right by that exact collapse — and is the same person who
  eventually fixed it.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2011) → ouster (2018) → $9.95 launch (Aug 2017,
  shown as happening in parallel/just after) → collapse (2019) → bankruptcy (2020) → criminal
  charges (2022) → guilty plea (2024) → comeback (2021-2022) → current standing (2023-2024) — with
  Spikes' timeline thread visually distinct in color from the Farnsworth/Lowe/Itum thread throughout.

### Final CTA + Outro (16:30–18:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a founder can be right, get fired for it, watch the company
  collapse exactly as warned, and still get a real second chance to prove it
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you haven't seen the Lyric video, package
  025, that's the direct companion piece to this one's brand-survives-the-company ending"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you ever have a MoviePass card? What year did you cancel — before the
  password-changing started, or after? And do you think Farnsworth and Lowe deserved what's coming
  to them? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 025 (Lyric) + most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed unit-economics comparison graphic ($9.95 charged vs. real per-ticket
cost, subscriber growth curve, $329.2M loss figure), then works backward into the founding, the
ouster, the criminal case, and the comeback. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a
genuine case study in deliberately unsustainable growth economics rather than a nostalgia piece.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Stacy Spikes' January 2018 firing itself — the disagreement over pricing direction, stated
plainly, before any of the $9.95-era chaos is shown — so the audience learns the founder is already
gone before watching the collapse unfold. Slower pacing, holds the "he was right" reveal until the
criminal charges land in 2022, then resolves with the 2021 buyback and relaunch as the payoff.
Strongest emotional arc of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "the $9.95 math that never worked, in 90 seconds" leading into rapid-fire beats: the
founding, the firing, the launch, the losses, the passwords, the shutdown, the charges, the plea, the
buyback, the fix, the current numbers — landing on the who's-who distinction as the final, clarifying
beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $9.95-vs-ticket-cost math and the
fired-then-bought-it-back arc (keep the SEC/DOJ fraud-allegation language precise and out of any
Shorts cut that doesn't have room for the full, careful attribution).

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the criminal-case segment (9:00–11:30) if the SEC/DOJ material becomes a dry
  legal recitation, or if the [VERIFY] sentencing disclosure feels like a disclaimer dump
- **Fix:** keep the disclosure card visually clean and brief in wording even though it's held on
  screen for a full beat; ground the fraud allegations in the single throttling/password-change
  detail already established in Segment 5 so it feels like a continuation, not a new topic
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general 2017-era "subscription economy" context if audience
  familiarity with the basic MoviePass story is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the "he came back" twist in the cold open framing more
  explicitly if early-retention data shows drop-off before the comeback segment (11:30) — many
  viewers assume this story has no second act
- **Recommended visual change:** keep the Spikes-timeline-thread color distinct from the
  Farnsworth/Lowe/Itum-timeline-thread color visible in a corner graphic throughout Segments 2
  through 11, so the who's-who separation stays visually reinforced across the entire runtime
