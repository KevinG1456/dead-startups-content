# Blog Article: MoviePass Charged $9.95 a Month for Unlimited Movies. It Lost Money on Almost Every Ticket. Then Its Fired Founder Bought It Back and Fixed It.

*All figures sourced to Wikipedia, SEC filings (Helios and Matheson's 2017 424B3/424B5 forms and
later financial disclosures), Inc.com (Stacy Spikes' own account), IndieWire, NBC News, Deadline,
Bloomberg, Variety, Hollywood Reporter, SEC.gov litigation release LR-25525, and Fortune — see
`sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. The single most important framing rule in this article:
Stacy Spikes (co-founder, fired January 2018, never charged with anything) is kept completely and
repeatedly distinct from Ted Farnsworth, Mitch Lowe, and Khalid Itum (the Helios and Matheson-era
executives who were later criminally charged). "Fraud" language applies only to Farnsworth, Lowe,
and Itum — never to Spikes. The 2023-2024 relaunch performance figures are presented throughout as
Spikes' own public statements about his own company, not as independently audited fact.*

## Introduction
Most startup-failure stories on this channel follow a familiar shape: one team, one bad bet, one
collapse. MoviePass breaks that shape into two distinct halves with two distinct casts of
characters. The person who founded the company was fired before its most reckless decision was ever
made. The people who made that decision — and then allegedly lied about the consequences — were not
him, and two of them are now convicted or charged federal criminals. And then, almost nobody's
version of this story includes the ending: the original, fired founder bought the wreckage back and
built something that, per his own account, actually works. This is that full, sourced arc, with the
who's-who kept clean throughout.

## The Founding
MoviePass was co-founded in 2011 by Stacy Spikes, positioned in its early years as a niche
subscription service for frequent moviegoers — pay a flat monthly fee, see a capped or modest number
of movies in theaters. It existed in this smaller, more conservative form for years before the event
that would eventually define — and destroy — the brand.

## The Acquisition and the Ouster
In 2017, data-analytics firm Helios and Matheson Analytics acquired a 51% controlling stake in
MoviePass for $25 million. This is the hinge point of the entire story: new majority ownership,
with its own ideas about pricing and growth.

In January 2018, Stacy Spikes was fired from MoviePass by Helios and Matheson after disagreeing with
the direction the new owners wanted to take the company's pricing model. This is worth stating as
plainly as the sourcing allows: **the founder was out of the company before the reckless $9.95
unlimited-plan strategy was locked in and before the worst of the financial collapse happened.** He
is not the executive responsible for the burn-rate catastrophe that follows in this article, and he
was not one of the individuals later criminally charged. Keeping this distinction clean is the
single most important accuracy requirement in this whole piece — conflating Spikes with the Helios
and Matheson-era executives who came after him would be a factual error.

## The $9.95 Plan
Per Spikes' own later account, the $9.95-a-month unlimited-movies plan originated as what was
initially framed as a short-term promotional move to mark Helios and Matheson's acquisition of the
company — not something Spikes himself intended to become the company's permanent price point.
Spikes has stated on record that he knew from the start the $9.95 rate was never going to be
profitable for the company at that price.

Under Helios and Matheson's leadership — CEO Ted Farnsworth, with Mitch Lowe installed as MoviePass
CEO — the $9.95 unlimited plan was launched and aggressively marketed starting in August 2017,
driving explosive subscriber growth. MoviePass grew to almost 3 million subscribers at its peak.

## The Math That Never Worked
Here is the mechanism, stated plainly: MoviePass was paying theaters close to full price for every
single ticket a subscriber used, while charging that subscriber a flat $9.95 per month no matter how
many movies they saw. A subscriber who saw one movie a month cost the company far less than they
paid in. A subscriber who saw eight movies a month generated a loss that dwarfed their entire annual
subscription fee. This wasn't a rounding error in the business model — it was the business model,
scaled to almost 3 million people. Helios and Matheson reported an estimated net loss of $329.2
million for the fiscal year covering the worst of the burn.

## The Desperate Measures
As losses mounted through 2018, the company took increasingly desperate measures to survive,
including reportedly changing subscriber passwords to block heavy users from using the service, and
experiencing repeated outages. This is presented here as a well-established, widely-corroborated
pattern from contemporaneous 2018 reporting and retrospective coverage, rather than tied to one
single, precise date and source. Subscriber count collapsed from almost 3 million at peak to
approximately 225,000 by April 2019, as service quality degraded and the company scrambled to
survive.

## Shutdown and Bankruptcy
On September 14, 2019, MoviePass notified its remaining subscribers it would shut down, stating its
"efforts to recapitalize MoviePass have not been successful to date." On January 28, 2020, Helios
and Matheson Analytics — MoviePass's parent company — filed a Chapter 7 bankruptcy petition, meaning
full liquidation, not reorganization, in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern District of New
York.

## The Criminal Case
This is the section where the who's-who distinction matters most. **None of what follows involves
Stacy Spikes.** He was fired in January 2018, well before the conduct described below.

The SEC and federal prosecutors alleged that from August 2017 through at least March 2019, Ted
Farnsworth (Helios and Matheson CEO) and Mitch Lowe (MoviePass CEO) repeatedly made materially false
or misleading public statements about MoviePass's business model and path to profitability — and
that when the company's true, dire finances became undeniable internally, they chose to covertly
throttle and degrade the service for heavy users rather than disclose the problem publicly. A third
executive, Khalid Itum, a MoviePass business-development executive, was also named in the SEC's
civil action.

In November 2022, Farnsworth and Lowe were criminally charged with wire fraud and securities fraud.
The government estimated total investor and consumer losses from the scheme at approximately $303
million. In 2024, Mitch Lowe pleaded guilty to a securities fraud conspiracy charge, admitting he had
conspired to deceive the public and investors about the sustainability of the $9.95-a-month unlimited
plan. He faces a maximum sentence of five years in federal prison under the plea.

This research did not fully confirm final sentencing for either Farnsworth or Lowe, or the current
resolved/unresolved status of Farnsworth's case, as of this article's research pass. This article
does not state a specific sentence for either man beyond the plea's stated maximum exposure — that
detail should be reconfirmed against current reporting before being treated as settled.

## The Comeback
In 2021, Stacy Spikes — the original founder, fired in January 2018 and entirely uninvolved in the
conduct described above — acquired MoviePass's brand and remaining assets out of the Helios and
Matheson bankruptcy proceeding.

In August 2022, Spikes announced MoviePass would relaunch under an entirely different pricing model:
three subscription tiers, none of them unlimited, with pricing varying by market — a direct
structural correction of the exact flaw that killed the original company. Roughly 775,000 people
joined a waitlist. On September 5, 2022, the relaunched MoviePass began a beta rollout in Chicago,
Dallas, and Kansas City.

By the most recent reporting found in this research pass (early 2024), Spikes stated the relaunched
service's users had collectively watched over 1 million movies since the relaunch, that 2023 was the
company's first profitable year, and that the company had grown from $0 to $14 million in annual
recurring revenue while powering 2.8 million movie trips. **These figures are presented here as
Spikes' own public statements about his own company — not as independently audited facts** —
consistent with how this channel handles founder self-reported figures elsewhere. This is the most
recent data point found in this research pass, not necessarily current performance as of when you're
reading this.

## The Other Brand-Survives-the-Company Story
This channel has covered a version of this pattern before: Lyric, a companion segment in package 025
(anchored on Sonder). Lyric's operating company effectively ended in July 2020, but its
dynamic-pricing software spun out and survives today as Wheelhouse, and its brand was later bought
and revived by an unrelated Florida real estate investment firm, Black Swan Holdings, in 2023.

MoviePass's version of this "brand outlives the company" pattern is more complete and more positive
than Lyric's. In Lyric's case, the brand was revived by a third party with no connection to the
original founders. In MoviePass's case, the person who came back to fix the company is the same
person who built it in the first place — and the person who was fired for objecting to the exact
pricing decision that later destroyed it. This is the more redemptive version of the same structural
beat.

## The Actual Lesson
A company can grow explosively on a strategy that is actively losing it money, collapse under the
weight of that same strategy, generate real federal fraud charges for the executives who ran it that
way — and still, separately, have a real, corrected second act under the person who tried to prevent
the first disaster from happening. The core irony is worth sitting with: the person removed for
objecting to the pricing decision that destroyed the company was later vindicated by that exact
collapse, and is the same person who eventually fixed it.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A flat-fee-for-unlimited-usage model is only as sound as its heaviest users' actual cost** —
  MoviePass's $9.95 plan lost money precisely because it didn't account for how heavy users would
  actually behave at scale
- **Who leaves a company, and when, matters as much as what the company did** — Spikes' departure
  seven months before the reckless pricing decision fully launched is the single fact that
  determines everything else about how to read this story
- **"Fraud" is a specific, legally meaningful word — apply it only to the people actually charged**
  — Farnsworth, Lowe, and (civilly) Itum, never Spikes
- **A founder's self-reported turnaround numbers deserve the same attribution discipline as any other
  claim** — present them as the founder's own statements, not as verified fact, until independently
  confirmed
- **A structurally broken pricing model can be fixed by removing the exact mechanism that broke it**
  — MoviePass's relaunch didn't reinvent the business, it just removed the unlimited tier

## The Lesson
MoviePass is the rare startup story with two distinct outcomes for two distinct groups of people: a
real federal fraud case for the executives who ran the company into the ground, and a real,
self-reported comeback for the founder who was fired for warning them not to. Keep the two groups
separate, and the story becomes not just a cautionary tale about unit economics, but a genuinely rare
example of a founder getting the ending the story originally denied him.
