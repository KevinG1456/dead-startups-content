## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week:
MoviePass — arguably the single most viral, most quotable unit-economics catastrophe in this entire
genre, paired with a genuinely rare happy ending.

MoviePass was co-founded in 2011 by Stacy Spikes. In 2017, data-analytics firm Helios and Matheson
Analytics acquired a 51% controlling stake in the company for $25 million. In January 2018, Spikes
was fired by the new owners after disagreeing with the pricing direction they wanted to take. This
is the single most important fact in this video: everything that follows — the reckless pricing
strategy, the historic losses, the federal fraud charges — happened after Spikes was already gone.

Under Helios and Matheson's leadership — CEO Ted Farnsworth, with Mitch Lowe installed as MoviePass
CEO — the company launched and aggressively marketed an unlimited-movies-for-$9.95-a-month plan
starting August 2017. Per Spikes' own later account, the $9.95 price originated as a short-term
promotional move tied to the acquisition, not something he intended to become permanent — and he has
said on record that he knew from the start it would never be profitable at that price. Subscribers
grew to almost 3 million at peak. Because MoviePass paid theaters close to full price for every
ticket while collecting a flat $9.95 fee regardless of how many movies a subscriber saw, heavy users
generated massive losses per subscriber — a structure that lost money on purpose, as a growth
strategy. Helios and Matheson reported an estimated net loss of $329.2 million for the fiscal year
covering the worst of the burn.

As losses mounted through 2018, the company took increasingly desperate measures to survive,
reportedly including changing subscriber passwords to block heavy users and experiencing repeated
outages — a well-corroborated, widely-reported pattern from contemporaneous coverage. Subscribers
collapsed from almost 3 million at peak to approximately 225,000 by April 2019. On September 14,
2019, MoviePass notified remaining subscribers it would shut down, citing unsuccessful efforts to
recapitalize. On January 28, 2020, Helios and Matheson Analytics filed a Chapter 7 bankruptcy
petition — full liquidation, not reorganization — in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the Southern
District of New York.

The SEC and federal prosecutors alleged that from August 2017 through at least March 2019, Farnsworth
and Lowe repeatedly made materially false or misleading public statements about MoviePass's business
model and path to profitability, and that they covertly throttled the service for heavy users rather
than disclose the company's true finances. A third executive, Khalid Itum, was also named in the
SEC's civil action. In November 2022, Farnsworth and Lowe were criminally charged with wire fraud and
securities fraud; the government estimated total investor and consumer losses at approximately $303
million. In 2024, Mitch Lowe pleaded guilty to a securities fraud conspiracy charge, admitting he had
conspired to deceive the public and investors about the plan's sustainability — he faces a maximum
sentence of five years in federal prison under the plea. This research pass confirmed the guilty plea
itself but did not confirm final sentencing for either man, or the resolved status of Farnsworth's
case — this video does not state a specific sentence beyond the plea's stated maximum exposure.

None of this involves Stacy Spikes. In 2021, Spikes acquired MoviePass's brand and remaining assets
out of the Helios and Matheson bankruptcy proceeding. In August 2022, he announced a relaunch under
an entirely different model: three subscription tiers, none of them unlimited, with pricing varying
by market — a direct structural correction of the flaw that killed the original company. Roughly
775,000 people joined a waitlist. The relaunched MoviePass began a beta rollout on September 5, 2022,
in Chicago, Dallas, and Kansas City. By the most recent reporting found in this research pass (early
2024), Spikes stated the relaunched service's users had collectively watched over 1 million movies,
that 2023 was the company's first profitable year, and that the company had grown from $0 to $14
million in annual recurring revenue while powering 2.8 million movie trips. These are Spikes' own
public statements about his own company, presented here as such — not independently audited figures
— and they represent the most recent data found in this research pass, not necessarily current
performance.

This channel has covered a brand surviving its own company's death before — Lyric, in package 025,
whose brand was later revived by an unrelated buyer, Black Swan Holdings. MoviePass's version of that
same pattern is the more complete, more redemptive one: here, the person who came back to fix the
company is the same person who built it in the first place, and who was fired for objecting to the
exact decision that later destroyed it.
