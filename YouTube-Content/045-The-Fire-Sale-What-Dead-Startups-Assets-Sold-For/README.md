# 045 — The Fire Sale: What a Dead Startup's Assets Actually Fetch, Line by Line

**Package status:** Filled in and sourced — built as a COMPILATION, not cold research. Every core
figure in this package was already fact-checked and sourced in its own package elsewhere on this
channel; this package's only new contribution is the Katerra asset-sale total (freshly sourced this
session) and the comparative framing itself. Replaces retired dead-saas-30 batch title #16 ("the
remote-first company that quietly stopped shipping," which never found a real sourceable anchor after
two research passes); fills the slot instead with batch title #24 ("The fire sale: what a startup's
assets fetch at auction, line by line"), Cluster 4. Same multi-company comparator format as packages
007, 015, 025, and 034 on this channel.

**The five real cases, each already an anchor or component elsewhere on this channel:**
1. **Quibi** — raised $1.75B, shut down after 6 months (Oct 2020). Its ~75-show content library sold
   to Roku for a reported price under $100M, announced January 2021. Re-verified from
   `002-Quibi-1.75B-6-Months-Zero-Pivots/README.md`.
2. **Bird** (scooters) — peaked at a $2.3–2.5B SPAC-era valuation. After Chapter 11 (Dec 20, 2023),
   assets sold April 5, 2024 for ~$145M to Third Lane Mobility — Bird's OWN lenders (led by an Apollo
   affiliate) converting debt to ownership, not an outside acquirer. Re-verified from
   `028-Bird-Scooter-SPAC-To-Bankruptcy/sources-log.txt`.
3. **Virgin Orbit** — peaked at a $3.7B SPAC valuation on ~$1B invested pre-IPO. After Chapter 11
   (Apr 4, 2023), assets were auctioned May 22–23, 2023 for ~$36M total, split three ways: Rocket Lab
   ($16.1M, HQ building), Stratolaunch ($17M, the Cosmic Girl 747 carrier plane), and Vast/Launcher
   ($2.7M, Mojave facility) — plus a separate $3.8M to Firefly Aerospace in June 2023. Re-verified from
   `031-Virgin-Orbit-3.7B-Valuation-Bankrupt-In-16-Months/sources-log.txt`.
4. **Friendster** — sold outright to MOL Global for $39M (Dec 2009, a normal acquisition, NOT a
   bankruptcy fire sale — no Chapter 11 was ever filed, flagged explicitly as a different mechanism
   from the other four). Then, separately, its patent portfolio sold to Facebook for $40M (Jun 2010) —
   the patents alone sold for MORE than the whole company had less than a year earlier. Re-verified
   from `040-Friendster-The-Founder-Who-Wouldnt-Sell/sources-log.txt`.
5. **Katerra** — raised ~$2B, mostly from SoftBank. Filed Chapter 11 June 7, 2021 (previously covered
   in `034-The-Bridge-Round-Death-Spiral-4-Companies/sources-log.txt` for its rescue-financing history,
   NOT for its final asset-sale total, which wasn't sourced in that package). FRESHLY SOURCED THIS
   SESSION: Katerra's combined asset sales and post-bankruptcy income totaled approximately $99M —
   including its Tracy, CA facility ($21.25M), its Spokane, WA factory ($50M), and its renovation
   business (sold to an Onx Homes-affiliated group for $1M cash plus $17M in assumed liabilities).
   Sources: [Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/bankruptcy-law/softbank-backed-katerra-seeks-liquidation-after-selling-assets),
   [Levelset](https://www.levelset.com/news/after-bankruptcy-katerra-still-faces-liens/).

**Framing requirement:** Every figure is presented as a ratio against the company's peak valuation or
lifetime funding — this is what makes the comparison visceral and legible across five very different
asset types (media content, a scooter fleet, aerospace hardware, software patents, construction-tech
factories). Friendster's sale is explicitly flagged on screen as NOT a bankruptcy fire sale (a
voluntary M&A deal instead) — its patent sale is the "line item" that earns it a place in this lineup,
not the company sale itself. No fraud or criminal allegations apply to any of the five companies —
keep the tone analytical, not accusatory, throughout.

the actual dollar figures the wreckage fetched, sourced to bankruptcy filings, SEC 8-Ks, and deal
press releases, and presenting them as one continuous ledger across five otherwise-unrelated
companies.

## 2. Viewer Psychology
- **Curiosity triggers:** how small the final sale prices are relative to what these companies were
  once "worth"; which specific physical or legal assets (a factory, a cargo plane, a patent portfolio)
  fetched real money when the company itself couldn't be saved
- **Emotional triggers:** shock at the scale of value destruction, dark fascination with liquidation
  mechanics, mild irony when a company's own lenders end up as the buyer
- **Viewer objections:** "this is just a recap of five different bankruptcies" — pre-empt by keeping
  the ratio-to-peak-value framing constant across all five, making the comparison itself the point
- **Motivation to watch:** founders/operators/investors studying distressed-asset and liquidation
  mechanics; general audience drawn to concrete numbers behind abstract "the company went bankrupt"
  headlines
- **Expected comments:** viewers naming other fire-sale examples, debate over which ratio is the most
  shocking, questions about what happens to smaller/less headline-worthy startups' assets

## 3. Competitor Analysis
- **Similar creators:** individual company bankruptcies are covered elsewhere (including four of these
  five on this very channel); no creator found in this research pass compiles multiple companies'
  actual liquidation dollar figures into one direct, ratio-framed comparison
- **Content gap:** most coverage stops at "the company filed for bankruptcy" — few follow through to
  the actual, dated, sourced dollar amounts the physical and legal assets fetched
- **How to outperform:** a consistent visual template per company (peak value bar → auction/sale price
  bar, drawn to scale) so the disproportion becomes immediately, visually obvious by the second
  comparison
