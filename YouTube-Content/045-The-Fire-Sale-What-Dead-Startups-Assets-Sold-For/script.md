# Script Package

Anchored on five companies — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR
THIS SCRIPT: Friendster's 2009 sale was a VOLUNTARY acquisition, not a bankruptcy fire sale — no
Chapter 11 was ever filed for Friendster. Never imply otherwise; its patent sale (2010) is what earns
it a place in this comparator, not the company sale itself. Keep every ratio (sale price as % of peak
valuation/lifetime funding) precisely as sourced — never round up to make a number more dramatic.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 15–17 minutes (pattern setup, five company segments, and the shared lesson, all
covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Five companies. Five real bankruptcies — well, four bankruptcies and one straight sale. Here's
  exactly what the wreckage actually sold for."
- Visual: five small "PEAK VALUE" bars of wildly different heights, each collapsing down to a tiny
  "SOLD FOR" bar beside it
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't about why these companies
  died. It's about what was actually left when they did."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This video covers five real companies — a streaming app, a scooter company, a rocket company, a
  social network, and a construction-tech company — and one question none of their individual failure
  stories usually answer: once the company is gone, what does the actual wreckage sell for?"
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll know the real dollar figures for all five, and you'll
  see exactly how small a fraction of peak value each one turned out to be."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: four of these five companies are already full autopsies on this channel — this
  package re-verifies and re-cites those figures directly, plus one newly sourced this session
  (Katerra's actual asset-sale total) — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "One of these five isn't a bankruptcy fire sale at all —
  it's a normal sale that gets interesting because of what happened to it a year later. I'll flag which
  one, and why it's still worth including."

### Company 1 — Quibi (1:40–3:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Raise (1:40–2:15):** founded by Jeffrey Katzenberg and Meg Whitman, Quibi raised
  $1.75 billion before launching a short-form premium mobile video app in April 2020. Visual: "$1.75
  BILLION RAISED" title card.
- **Segment 2 — The Collapse (2:15–2:45):** shutdown announced October 21, 2020 — roughly six months
  after launch. Visual: "SHUTDOWN — 6 MONTHS LATER" title card.
- **Segment 3 — What Sold (2:45–3:30):** January 2021, Quibi's content library — roughly 75 shows —
  sold to Roku for a reported price under $100 million. On-screen framing card: "Under $100 million,
  against $1.75 billion raised — under 6% of what went in." Visual: "$1.75B → UNDER $100M" ratio bar
  graphic.

### Company 2 — Bird (3:30–6:00)
- **Segment 4 — The Peak (3:30–4:15):** founded 2017 by Travis VanderZanden, Bird's dockless-scooter
  business went public via SPAC in November 2021 at a reported $2.3-2.5 billion valuation. Visual:
  "$2.3–2.5 BILLION PEAK VALUATION" title card.
- **Segment 5 — The Collapse (4:15–5:00):** an SEC-disclosed accounting restatement, an NYSE delisting,
  and Chapter 11 filed December 20, 2023. Visual: "CHAPTER 11 — DECEMBER 2023" title card.
- **Segment 6 — What Sold (5:00–6:00):** April 5, 2024, Bird's assets sold for approximately $145
  million — to Third Lane Mobility, a new company formed by Bird's OWN lenders, led by an Apollo Global
  Management affiliate, converting debt into ownership. On-screen framing card: "The buyer wasn't an
  outsider. It was the people Bird already owed money to." Visual: "$2.3–2.5B → $145M" ratio bar
  graphic.

### Company 3 — Virgin Orbit (6:00–9:15)
- **Segment 7 — The Peak (6:00–6:45):** founded 2017 as a Virgin Galactic spin-off under Richard
  Branson, Virgin Orbit went public via SPAC in December 2021 at a $3.7 billion valuation, on roughly
  $1 billion invested pre-IPO. Visual: "$3.7 BILLION VALUATION" title card.
- **Segment 8 — The Collapse (6:45–7:30):** a failed UK launch in January 2023 (traced to a dislodged
  fuel filter), mass layoffs, and Chapter 11 filed April 4, 2023 — about 16 months after going public.
  Visual: "CHAPTER 11 — 16 MONTHS AFTER IPO" title card.
- **Segment 9 — What Sold (7:30–9:15):** May 2023, Virgin Orbit's assets were auctioned for roughly $36
  million total, split three ways — Rocket Lab paid $16.1 million for the Long Beach headquarters,
  Stratolaunch paid $17 million for the Cosmic Girl 747 carrier plane, and Vast/Launcher paid $2.7
  million for the Mojave facility. On-screen framing card: "$36 million, against a $3.7 billion peak
  valuation — about 1%." Visual: "$3.7B → $36M" ratio bar graphic, the sharpest contrast in the video.
- Mid-video CTA (~9:00): "Two down, three to go — including the one where the patents outsold the
  entire company. Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### Company 4 — Friendster (9:15–11:30)
- **Segment 10 — The Difference (9:15–9:45):** on-screen framing card, stated clearly before the
  segment begins: "This one isn't a bankruptcy fire sale — Friendster was never in Chapter 11. It's
  here because of what happened to one specific piece of it, a year after the company itself sold."
  Visual: "NOT A BANKRUPTCY — A VOLUNTARY SALE" framing card.
- **Segment 11 — The Sale (9:45–10:30):** December 2009, Malaysian payments company MOL Global bought
  100% of Friendster for approximately $39 million. Visual: "SOLD FOR $39 MILLION — DECEMBER 2009"
  title card.
- **Segment 12 — The Patents (10:30–11:30):** June 2010, MOL separately sold Friendster's patent
  portfolio to Facebook for approximately $40 million — more than the whole company had sold for less
  than a year earlier. On-screen framing card: "The patents alone sold for more than the entire
  company." Visual: "$39M COMPANY → $40M PATENTS ALONE" comparison graphic.

### Company 5 — Katerra (11:30–14:00)
- **Segment 13 — The Peak (11:30–12:00):** founded 2015 by Michael Marks, Katerra raised roughly $2
  billion, mostly from SoftBank, building a vertically integrated construction company. Visual: "~$2
  BILLION RAISED" title card.
- **Segment 14 — The Collapse (12:00–12:45):** two separate $200 million SoftBank rescue infusions in
  2020, then Chapter 11 filed June 7, 2021, after Greensill Capital's unrelated collapse cut off
  Katerra's working-capital lifeline. Visual: "CHAPTER 11 — JUNE 2021" title card.
- **Segment 15 — What Sold (12:45–14:00):** Katerra's combined asset sales and post-bankruptcy income
  totaled approximately $99 million — its Tracy, California facility sold for $21.25 million, its
  Spokane, Washington factory for $50 million, and its renovation business for $1 million cash plus $17
  million in assumed liabilities. On-screen framing card: "$99 million, against roughly $2 billion
  raised — about 5%." Visual: "~$2B → ~$99M" ratio bar graphic.

### The Actual Lesson (14:00–15:30)
- Plain-language comparison: five companies, five industries, five very different asset types — and in
  every single case, what the wreckage actually sold for was a small fraction of what the company was
  once believed to be worth. Four of the five went through real bankruptcy fire sales; the fifth
  (Friendster) shows the same principle a different way — even a voluntary sale can be dwarfed by what
  a single piece of the company turns out to be worth later.
- The core lesson, stated directly: a company's peak valuation measures belief. A liquidation measures
  what buyers will actually pay, in cash, once belief is no longer part of the price. The gap between
  those two numbers is the real cost of a failed bet — and it's almost always bigger than people expect.
- Visual: five-row side-by-side ratio ledger — Virgin Orbit (~1%), Katerra (~5%), Quibi (under 5.7%),
  Bird (~6%), Friendster (patents alone exceeded the company sale).

### Final CTA + Outro (15:30–17:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — valuation measures belief, liquidation measures cash, and the gap
  between them is the real, final cost of a failed company
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want the full individual stories behind

- Engagement prompt: "Which of these five ratios surprised you most — the 1% rocket company, the

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the five-row ratio ledger from The Actual Lesson, then works through each company in
ratio order (Virgin Orbit's ~1% first, Friendster's irony last), using the ledger as a recurring visual
anchor after each segment. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a genuine liquidation-value
case study.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on Friendster's patents-outsold-the-company detail as the most viscerally specific and
counterintuitive moment, then works through the other four chronologically by bankruptcy-filing date
(Katerra 2021 → Virgin Orbit 2023 → Bird 2023/2024 → Quibi 2020/2021, reordered for narrative effect),
closing with the shared-ratio reveal. Strongest tonal build of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "five companies, five fire sales" with rapid-fire beats per company (peak value → what
happened → what sold → the ratio), landing on the shared-lesson reveal as the final, clarifying beat.
Strong Shorts-extraction potential — each company's ratio reveal can stand alone as its own Short.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the transition between companies if the pacing flags before the next company's
  ratio-reveal lands, especially heading into Friendster's segment where the "not a bankruptcy"
  framing card could feel like a slow-down
- **Fix:** end each company segment on its ratio bar graphic reveal immediately before cutting to the
  next company, so momentum carries across the transition; keep Friendster's framing card to one
  sentence before moving straight into its own numbers
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general company-history context (Quibi's founding story, Bird's
  business model) if audience is more interested in the liquidation numbers themselves
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the Virgin Orbit ~1% ratio and the Friendster patents-
  outsold-the-company detail in the cold open if early-retention data shows drop-off before those
  segments
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running ratio-ledger corner graphic visible from Segment
  3 onward, filling in each company's percentage as its segment concludes, so viewers always see how
  many comparisons remain
