# Blog Article: The Fire Sale — What a Dead Startup's Assets Actually Fetch, Line by Line

*All figures re-verified against this channel's own already-sourced packages (002, 028, 031, 040),
plus one freshly-sourced figure for Katerra — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map.*

## Introduction
Startup-failure coverage almost always ends at the shutdown announcement or the bankruptcy filing.
What happens after that — what the actual office, equipment, content, patents, and fleet turn out to
be worth once nobody believes in the company anymore — usually goes untold. This article tracks down
five real, dated, sourced answers.

## Quibi: $1.75 Billion In, Under $100 Million Out
Jeffrey Katzenberg and Meg Whitman's short-form video app raised $1.75 billion before launching in
April 2020 and shutting down just six months later, in October 2020. In January 2021, its content
library — roughly 75 shows — sold to Roku for a reported price under $100 million. That's under 5.7%
of what went in.

## Bird: Bought by Its Own Lenders
Bird's dockless-scooter business went public via SPAC in November 2021 at a reported $2.3-2.5 billion
valuation. After an SEC-disclosed accounting restatement and an NYSE delisting, Bird filed Chapter 11
in December 2023. In April 2024, its assets sold for approximately $145 million — not to an outside
acquirer, but to Third Lane Mobility, a new company formed by Bird's OWN lenders, led by an Apollo
Global Management affiliate, converting the debt Bird already owed them into ownership of what was
left.

## Virgin Orbit: About 1% of Peak Value
Richard Branson's small-satellite launch company went public via SPAC in December 2021 at a $3.7
billion valuation. A failed UK launch in January 2023, traced to a dislodged fuel filter, triggered
mass layoffs and a Chapter 11 filing in April 2023 — about 16 months after going public. In May 2023,
its assets were auctioned for roughly $36 million total, split three ways: Rocket Lab paid $16.1
million for the Long Beach headquarters, Stratolaunch paid $17 million for the Cosmic Girl 747 carrier
aircraft, and Vast/Launcher paid $2.7 million for the Mojave facility. That's about 1% of the company's
peak valuation — the sharpest ratio in this comparison.

## Friendster: Not a Bankruptcy, But the Sharpest Irony
Friendster is different from the other four companies in this article — it never filed for bankruptcy.
In December 2009, it sold outright to Malaysian payments company MOL Global for approximately $39
million, a normal, voluntary acquisition. What earns it a place here is what happened next: in June
2010, MOL separately sold Friendster's patent portfolio to Facebook for approximately $40 million —
more than the entire company had sold for less than a year earlier.

## Katerra: $2 Billion Raised, $99 Million Recovered
Katerra, a vertically integrated construction-tech company backed mostly by SoftBank, raised roughly
$2 billion before filing Chapter 11 in June 2021, after two prior $200 million SoftBank rescue
infusions failed to save it. Its combined asset sales and post-bankruptcy income totaled approximately
$99 million — its Tracy, California facility sold for $21.25 million, its Spokane, Washington factory
for $50 million, and its renovation business for $1 million cash plus $17 million in assumed
liabilities. That's roughly 5% of what was raised.

## The Actual Lesson
Line these five up and a pattern emerges that has nothing to do with industry: media, hardware fleets,
aerospace, software patents, and construction tech all produced the same shape of outcome. A peak
valuation measures belief — what investors, at a specific moment, thought a company's future was
worth. A liquidation measures something completely different: what real buyers, in cash, right now,
with no story left to believe in, will actually pay for what remains. The gap between those two
numbers is the real, final cost of a failed bet, and in every one of these five cases, it was larger
than most people would guess.

Friendster's story adds one more wrinkle worth remembering: sometimes a single piece of a company — a
patent portfolio, in this case — turns out to be worth more than the whole company was, on its own,
just months earlier. Value doesn't always live where a business's own name and brand lived. Sometimes
it's sitting in a filing cabinet the whole time.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **Peak valuation and liquidation value are answering two different questions** — one measures
  belief, the other measures what cash buyers will actually pay once belief is gone
- **A company's own lenders are often the most likely buyer of its wreckage** — they already know the
  assets, already have a claim on them, and are often best positioned to bid
- **Physical and legal assets can retain value independent of the business built around them** — a
  factory, a headquarters, a cargo plane, or a patent portfolio can outlive the company that owned it
- **Not every distressed sale is a bankruptcy** — a company can be sold for a fraction of what a single
  piece of it is later worth without ever filing Chapter 11
- **The ratio, not the raw dollar figure, is what actually tells the story** — $145 million sounds
  large in isolation; against a $2.5 billion peak valuation, it's a very different number

## The Lesson
Every one of these five companies was, at some point, believed to be worth billions. What they actually
sold for, line by line, tells a more honest story than any valuation ever did — and it's a story that
almost never gets told, because most coverage stops well before the auction.
