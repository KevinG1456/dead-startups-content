# Blog Article: Customer Concentration Risk — Startups That Died Because One Customer Was Most of Their Revenue

## Introduction
Landing a massive customer can feel like a company's defining validation moment — proof that the product works and the business is real. But when one customer comes to represent the overwhelming majority of a company's revenue, that same relationship becomes a structural vulnerability. This article breaks down customer concentration risk using a real, documented example, along with a practical framework for managing it.

## What Is Customer Concentration Risk?
Customer concentration risk is the danger that a company's financial health becomes so dependent on one customer (or a small handful of customers) that losing that relationship threatens the survival of the business itself. It's a well-established concept in corporate finance and is a standard line item in investor and lender diligence — but it's discussed far less often in startup media than funding, product, or team risk.

## Why This Risk Is Easy to Miss
Customer concentration risk is easy to overlook precisely because it looks like success while it's building. Revenue is growing, the company has validation from a marquee customer, and the immediate financial picture looks strong. The risk only becomes visible in retrospect, once the relationship changes or ends — which is exactly what makes it dangerous.

## What a "Safe" Concentration Level Looks Like
There's no single universal threshold, but common diligence and lending practice often treats customer concentration above roughly 10-20% of revenue from a single customer as worth close scrutiny, with concentration above 50% frequently treated as a significant, explicit risk factor requiring a clear mitigation plan `[VERIFY specific industry-standard thresholds before citing as authoritative]`. The right threshold varies significantly by industry, contract structure, and switching costs.

## How Founders Can Manage This Risk
- Actively pursue revenue diversification even while a dominant customer relationship is healthy, rather than waiting until it's at risk
- Negotiate contract terms (length, exclusivity, notice periods) that provide some cushion if the relationship changes
- Be transparent with your board and investors about concentration levels rather than letting a strong topline number obscure the underlying risk
- Build organizational and product flexibility that doesn't assume the dominant customer relationship is permanent

## The Lesson
A single dominant customer relationship isn't automatically a mistake — for many companies, especially in enterprise and industrial contexts, it can be a rational and even necessary path to scale. But it should be evaluated and managed as an explicit, named risk, not treated as pure validation. The companies most vulnerable to customer concentration risk are the ones that never planned for what happens if the relationship changes.
