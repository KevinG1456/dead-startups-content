THE MEMO — precisely sourced
- Stephen Elop became Nokia's CEO in September 2010, moving directly from his prior role as
  head of Microsoft's Business Division (responsible for the Office suite, among other
  products) — the first non-Finnish CEO in Nokia's history.
- February 8, 2011: Elop sent an internal memo, roughly 1,300 words, to all Nokia staff. It was
  leaked/reported publicly within about a day.
- The memo opens with an extended metaphor: a worker on a burning North Sea oil rig who wakes to
  an explosion and, surrounded by flames with the ocean 30 meters below, has an impossible
  choice — "stand on the platform, and inevitably be consumed by the burning flames. Or, he
  could plunge 30 meters in to the freezing waters." The man jumps.
- On Apple's rise: "In 2008, Apple's market share in the $300+ price range was 25 percent; by
  2010 it escalated to 61 percent." On Android: "In about two years, Android created a platform
  that attracts application developers" — and, in the memo's own words about that same week,
  Android had just taken "our leadership position in smartphone volumes."
- Closing call to action: "Together, we can choose to define our future." Elop explicitly framed
  the memo as urgent justification for a major strategic announcement scheduled 3 days later,
  February 11, 2011.
- Nokia's board chairman reportedly gave "bitter feedback" on the memo at a subsequent board
  meeting — [VERIFY]: this is reported via Alexander Jarvis's retrospective piece; no primary
  board-minutes source was found in this pass, present as reported, not confirmed record.
Source: Engadget (engadget.com/2011-02-08-nokia-ceo-stephen-elop-rallies-troops-in-brutally-
honest-burnin.html); Forbes (Eric Savitz, Feb 9, 2011); Alexander Jarvis retrospective (full memo
text); Fortune (Sept 10, 2010, on Elop's hiring).

THE "TROJAN HORSE" THEORY — DISPUTED, HANDLE WITH CARE
- After the eventual 2013-2014 Microsoft acquisition, some employees, former Nokia executives,
  and outside commentators speculated that Elop had been placed at Nokia specifically to steer
  it toward a Microsoft acquisition — the "Trojan horse" theory.
- Elop has explicitly and directly denied this, on record: "I have only ever worked on behalf of
  and for the benefit of Nokia shareholders while at Nokia," adding that "all fundamental
  business and strategy decisions were made with the support and approval of the Nokia board of
  directors, of which I was a member."
- A later book (Finnish-language, reported via Yle and Neowin) reportedly concludes Elop was NOT
  a Trojan horse, but was instead simply an ineffective CEO — a different, also-unflattering, but
  distinct claim.
- HANDLING REQUIREMENT: never state the "Trojan horse" theory as fact. Always attribute it
  explicitly ("some employees and critics speculated," "the theory holds that..."), and always
  pair it with Elop's own on-record denial and the board-approval fact in the same breath or the
  same segment. This mirrors this channel's established handling of disputed/unadjudicated claims
  about a named, living individual (see package 025's Marriott-allegation handling, package 022's
  Fast/NPR handling). Never use this theory as if it were confirmed motive.
Source: Neowin ("New book claims Microsoft's Stephen Elop wasn't a 'Trojan horse' at Nokia - just
a bad CEO"); Yle (Finnish public broadcaster, same book coverage); The Register forums
(contemporaneous "I'm not a Trojan horse" pushback, Feb 2011).

THE PIVOT — precisely sourced
- February 11, 2011 (3 days after the memo): Nokia and Microsoft announced a strategic
  partnership — Nokia would adopt Windows Phone as its primary smartphone platform, abandoning
  its own Symbian and the still-unreleased MeeGo operating systems.
- This was the drastic action the memo was written to justify — not a warning that was later
  ignored, but the explicit rationale for the pivot that followed almost immediately.
Source: Forbes; Engadget; widely corroborated contemporaneous 2011 tech press coverage.

THE OUTCOME — precisely sourced
- The Windows Phone bet did not reverse Nokia's decline. Windows Phone never achieved durable
  market share against iOS and Android.
- September 3, 2013: Microsoft announced it would acquire substantially all of Nokia's Devices &
  Services business. Deal structure: approximately €3.79 billion (~$5 billion) for the D&S
  business itself, plus approximately €1.65 billion (~$2.17 billion) to license Nokia's patents
  — a combined total commonly reported as approximately $7.2 billion (a figure of $7.17 billion
  also appears in some contemporaneous reporting — use "approximately $7.2 billion").
  - [VERIFY]: exact final combined total varies slightly across sources ($7.17B vs $7.2B) — use
    the hedged "approximately $7.2 billion," never a falsely precise single figure.
- The deal closed in 2014; approximately 30,000 employees transferred to Microsoft. The new unit
  was named "Microsoft Mobile Oy."
- July 8, 2015: under CEO Satya Nadella (who had succeeded Steve Ballmer — the CEO who actually
  did the 2013 deal — in Feb 2014), Microsoft announced a $7.6 billion write-down/impairment
  charge on the Nokia acquisition — nearly the entire original purchase price — alongside 7,800
  layoffs (primarily in the phone hardware business) and an approximately $800 million
  restructuring charge. This was, at the time, the largest write-down in Microsoft's corporate
  history, surpassing its prior record ($6.2 billion, the 2012 aQuantive write-down).
- Throughout 2016, Microsoft slowed Lumia production; only nine Lumia phones were released that
  year before the line was fully discontinued.
Source: Computerworld; TechCrunch (Jul 8, 2015, "Microsoft Writes Down $7.6B Of Its Nokia
Acquisition, Announces 7,800 Layoffs"); Forbes (Adam Hartung, Jul 8, 2015); The Register;
GSMArena; Wikipedia ("Microsoft Lumia").
