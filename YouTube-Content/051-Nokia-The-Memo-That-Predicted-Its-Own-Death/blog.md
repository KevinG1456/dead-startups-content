# Blog Article: He Warned Everyone the Platform Was Burning. They Listened. It Still Burned Down.

*All figures sourced to Engadget, Forbes, Alexander Jarvis's retrospective compilation, Computerworld,

## Introduction
"Burning platform" is one of the most borrowed phrases in business strategy. Almost everyone who
uses it knows it came from a leaked internal memo. Almost nobody who uses it knows how the actual
story ends. It doesn't end with an ignored warning. It ends with a warning that was heeded almost
immediately — and a company that died anyway.

## The New CEO
In September 2010, Nokia hired Stephen Elop as CEO — the first non-Finnish CEO in the company's
history. He arrived directly from running Microsoft's Business Division, which had overall
responsibility for products including the Office suite.

## The Memo
On February 8, 2011, Elop sent Nokia's entire staff an internal memo, roughly 1,300 words long.
It opened with an extended metaphor: a worker on a burning North Sea oil rig, waking to an
explosion, facing an impossible choice. In Elop's own words, the man could "stand on the platform,
and inevitably be consumed by the burning flames. Or, he could plunge 30 meters in to the freezing
waters." The man jumps.

The memo backed the metaphor with real numbers. On Apple: "In 2008, Apple's market share in the
$300+ price range was 25 percent; by 2010 it escalated to 61 percent." On Android, which had just
overtaken Nokia in smartphone sales volume that same week: "In about two years, Android created a
platform that attracts application developers." The memo leaked publicly within about a day,
becoming one of the most widely covered internal corporate documents in tech history.

## It Was Not Ignored
Here is the detail most retellings skip. Three days after the memo leaked — February 11, 2011 —
Nokia announced the drastic pivot the memo was written to justify: abandoning its own Symbian and
the still-unreleased MeeGo operating systems in favor of Windows Phone. This was not a warning
that sat ignored on a shelf. It was acted on almost immediately.

## The Trojan Horse Theory
After everything that followed, a theory emerged among some employees, former executives, and
outside commentators: that Elop had been placed at Nokia specifically to engineer a future
Microsoft acquisition — a "Trojan horse." Elop has directly and repeatedly denied this on record:
"I have only ever worked on behalf of and for the benefit of Nokia shareholders while at Nokia,"
adding that "all fundamental business and strategy decisions were made with the support and
approval of the Nokia board of directors, of which I was a member." A later retrospective book
reportedly concludes he wasn't a plant at all — just an ineffective CEO, a different and still
unflattering, but distinct, claim. This article presents the theory as disputed, not as fact.

## The Bet Doesn't Work
Despite the fast, decisive pivot, Windows Phone never achieved durable market share against iOS
and Android. Nokia's position kept deteriorating through 2011, 2012, and 2013.

## Sold
On September 3, 2013, Microsoft agreed to acquire substantially all of Nokia's Devices & Services
business — roughly $5 billion for the business itself, roughly $2.17 billion to license Nokia's
patents, a combined total commonly reported as approximately $7.2 billion. The deal closed in
2014; about 30,000 employees transferred to a new subsidiary, Microsoft Mobile Oy.

## The Write-Off
On July 8, 2015 — under new CEO Satya Nadella, who had succeeded Steve Ballmer, the CEO who did
the original deal — Microsoft wrote off $7.6 billion, nearly the entire purchase price, and laid
off 7,800 people, most from the former Nokia phone business. At the time, this was the largest
write-down in Microsoft's corporate history, surpassing its prior record of $6.2 billion from a
2012 write-down. Through 2016, Lumia production wound down to just nine phones released that year
before the entire line was discontinued.

## The Actual Lesson
Most stories about internal warnings follow the same shape: leadership ignores a memo, the
predicted disaster happens, and the lesson is "listen next time." This story doesn't let you off
that easily. Nokia's leadership did listen. They acted within three days, betting the company's
future on exactly the kind of drastic pivot the memo called for. The market still didn't reward
it.

The lesson isn't that warnings don't matter. It's that heeding a warning and acting on it fast is
necessary — but it isn't sufficient. The response also has to actually work, and sometimes, even
with the right diagnosis and the right speed, it just doesn't.

## What Founders and Leaders Should Take From This
- **A correct diagnosis doesn't guarantee a correct cure** — Elop's read on the smartphone market
  was accurate; the specific bet Nokia made in response still didn't pay off
- **Speed of response isn't the same as quality of response** — Nokia moved in three days, faster
  than almost any comparable corporate pivot, and still lost
- **Disputed theories about a living person's motives deserve careful handling** — the "Trojan
  horse" theory is widely repeated, but it remains disputed and directly denied on record; treat
  it as such, not as settled fact
- **Public-company outcomes are still real case studies for founders** — Nokia isn't VC-funded,
  but the mechanics of a heeded-but-failed pivot apply just as directly to any funded startup
  making a bet-the-company decision
- **The gap between a warning and an ending is rarely as clean as the popular version suggests** —
  this story is often compressed to "2 years"; the real gap is closer to four and a half to five

## The Lesson
The "Burning Platform" memo is usually cited as a case study in radical corporate honesty. The
more precise, more useful version is about what honesty alone can't do: it can get a company to
jump at exactly the right moment, and the water can still be too cold.
