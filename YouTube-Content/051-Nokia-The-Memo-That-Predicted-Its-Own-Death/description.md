## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct outcomes using primary sources. This week: a
deliberate exception to our usual funded-startup format — Nokia, and the most famous internal
memo in modern tech history.

Stephen Elop became Nokia's CEO in September 2010, arriving directly from running Microsoft's
Business Division — the first non-Finnish CEO in the company's history. On February 8, 2011, he
sent all Nokia staff an internal memo, roughly 1,300 words, built around an extended metaphor: a
worker on a burning North Sea oil rig, facing an impossible choice between certain death by fire
or a 30-meter plunge into freezing water. The memo backed the metaphor with real numbers: Apple's
share of the $300+ phone market had gone from 25% in 2008 to 61% by 2010, and that same week,
Android had overtaken Nokia in smartphone sales volume. It leaked publicly within about a day.

Here's the part most retellings skip: this warning was not ignored. Three days later, on February
11, 2011, Nokia announced the pivot the memo was written to justify — abandoning its own Symbian
and the unreleased MeeGo operating systems for Windows Phone. Some employees and critics later
floated a "Trojan horse" theory, suggesting Elop had been placed at Nokia to engineer a future
Microsoft sale. Elop has directly and repeatedly denied this on record; a later retrospective book
reportedly concludes he wasn't a plant, just an ineffective CEO.

The Windows Phone bet did not work. Market share kept falling through 2011-2013. On September 3,
2013, Microsoft agreed to acquire substantially all of Nokia's Devices & Services business for
approximately $7.2 billion; the deal closed in 2014 with about 30,000 employees transferring over.
On July 8, 2015 — under new CEO Satya Nadella — Microsoft wrote off $7.6 billion, nearly the
entire purchase price, and laid off 7,800 people, most from the former Nokia phone business. At
the time, this was the largest write-down in Microsoft's corporate history. Through 2016, Lumia
production wound down to just nine phones before the entire line was discontinued.

The real lesson: heeding a warning and acting on it fast is necessary, but it isn't sufficient.
The response also has to actually work.
