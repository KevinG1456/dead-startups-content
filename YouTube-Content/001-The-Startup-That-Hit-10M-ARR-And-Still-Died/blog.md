# Blog Article: Sprig Hit a $22M Run Rate. It Still Lost $850K a Month.

*All figures sourced to public reporting and founder interviews — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Sprig's revenue is presented as "roughly $21-22M," per co-founder/CEO Gagan Biyani's own words, not as a false-precision single number.*

## Introduction
Most startup-failure stories have to be reconstructed secondhand — press coverage, leaked documents, LinkedIn posts from former employees. Sprig is different. Its co-founder and CEO, Gagan Biyani, gave two separate, detailed, on-record interviews after the company shut down, walking through exactly what went wrong in his own words. That makes Sprig one of the best-documented "real revenue, still died" cases available, and a genuine unit-economics teaching case rather than just another cautionary tale.

## The Raise
Sprig was founded in 2013 by CEO Gagan Biyani, building an on-demand meal-delivery service with one structural difference from most competitors: it built and operated its own kitchens rather than partnering with existing restaurants. The company raised a $10 million Series A from Greylock Partners in March 2014, followed by a $45 million round in April 2015 led by Social+Capital and Greylock, with Battery Ventures and Accel participating. Total lifetime funding is reported as roughly $56-58 million across sources that cluster tightly but don't agree to the exact dollar.

## The Run Rate
By around February 2016, Sprig had reached real, meaningful revenue. Biyani himself, in a later Mixergy interview, described it this way: "we were on a run rate of $22 million, so or 21, or something like that." That's not a company limping along — it's a startup with genuine commercial traction. And it still wasn't enough.

## The Cost Structure
Here's the mechanism most retellings of startup failure skip: Sprig's decision to own its own kitchens meant it bore the full cost of food production and delivery directly, with no restaurant partner absorbing any of that cost or risk the way a marketplace model would. As Biyani put it, describing what happened as growth slowed: "when your business is declining... your gross margin gets harder and harder... their margins are already so thin, that overhead is cutting a greater percentage." Per Bloomberg's reporting, Sprig was losing roughly $850,000 a month at its worst — even while pulling in a $21-22 million annual run rate.

## The Trigger
Around February 2016, Uber Eats launched its third major product iteration, competing directly for the same on-demand meal customers Sprig depended on. Biyani described the effect precisely: "instead of going up by 2%, we were going down by 2%" — a clean, dated inflection point where Sprig's own weekly growth rate flipped from positive to negative.

## The Shutdown
Sprig shut down on May 26, 2017. Roughly 200 staff were affected at the time of the shutdown, according to Fortune — a smaller number than the company's peak operational headcount, reported by Wikipedia at over 1,300 across its kitchens and delivery operations at its largest scale. These are two different measures — peak operational scale versus final shutdown headcount — not a contradiction, and this article keeps them clearly distinguished rather than conflating them. Roughly $8 million was returned to investors out of the approximately $56-58 million raised.

## The Founder's Own Hindsight
What sets Sprig apart from most autopsies is that Biyani didn't just accept press coverage of the shutdown — he explained it himself, in detail, more than once. In a separate interview with The Full Ratchet, he covered a second, compounding factor beyond pure unit economics: declining food quality as the company scaled and cut costs — food arriving cold, presentation and taste declining — which drove churn independent of the cost-structure problem. And in reflecting on the whole arc, he offered one of the more candid lines available in startup-failure journalism: "I would have shut it down within three months." That's not a literal claim about Sprig's 2013 launch date — it refers to the period once the negative unit-economics trend had become clear. Presented in that context, it's a genuinely instructive statement about founder decision-making under sunk-cost pressure.

## The Actual Lesson
Sprig's story isn't really about running out of money — plenty of startups do that and it's rarely interesting on its own. It's about a specific structural choice — owning the full supply chain rather than operating as a marketplace — that made growth itself dangerous once the market got more competitive. A marketplace model spreads production risk across restaurant partners; Sprig's owned-kitchen model concentrated all of it on the company itself. That's not automatically the wrong choice (it can offer more quality control and margin upside when things go well), but it's a fundamentally different risk profile than most founders building "on-demand X" companies were signing up for at the time, and Sprig's numbers show exactly what that risk profile costs when growth reverses.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **Understand exactly who bears the cost in your model** — a marketplace and a vertically-integrated operator have fundamentally different risk profiles, even if the customer-facing product looks similar
- **Watch your fixed-cost-to-revenue ratio as growth slows**, not just while it's climbing — Biyani's own quote about overhead "cutting a greater percentage" as growth declines is the whole mechanism in one sentence
- **Take a single competitor's product launch seriously if your growth rate visibly reacts to it** — Sprig's dated Uber Eats inflection point shows how fast a trend can flip
- **Consider acting on a negative trend earlier than sunk-cost thinking suggests** — Biyani's own hindsight is the clearest available data point on this

## The Lesson
Real revenue at meaningful scale — $21-22 million a year, by the founder's own account — doesn't automatically mean a viable business if the cost of producing what you sell is structurally too high, especially once growth slows and fixed costs have fewer orders to spread across. Sprig is a real, well-documented, founder-narrated proof of exactly that.

**16. What is a vertically integrated startup?**
One that owns and operates more of its own supply chain — in Sprig's case, its own kitchens — rather than relying on partners to absorb some of the production cost and risk.

**17. What is the difference between a marketplace model and Sprig's model?**
A marketplace connects supply (restaurants) and demand (customers) without owning production; Sprig owned production itself, concentrating all food-cost risk on the company.

**18. What is customer acquisition cost vs. lifetime value?**
CAC is what it costs to acquire a customer; LTV is what that customer is worth over time. When CAC consistently outpaces LTV, or margins on each sale are too thin, growth itself can become unprofitable.
