## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week:
Sonder — founded in 2014 in Montreal (originally as Flatbook, later rebranded), relocated to San
Francisco, and co-founded by CEO Francis Davidson alongside Lucas Pellan and Martin Picard. Sonder
leased and managed its own short-term rental units directly, reaching 9,000+ units across 40+
cities at its peak.

Sonder raised more than $550 million in private venture funding before doing something almost no
other company on this channel has done: merging with a SPAC (Gores Metropoulos II) to go public
on Nasdaq under ticker SOND in January 2022. It never turned a profit in over a decade. Combined
net losses of $240 million in 2023-2024 alone pushed its cumulative deficit to $1.6 billion by the
end of 2024 — even with strong 2024 occupancy (80.9%) and healthy nightly rates ($196 average).
This was not a demand-collapse story; revenue of $621M still fell short of $803M in costs, largely
because of fixed master-lease obligations averaging 6.8 years remaining.

In August 2024, Sonder signed a licensing deal putting 10,500+ of its rooms on Marriott Bonvoy —
widely covered at the time as a legitimacy win. Fifteen months later, that same deal ended the
company. Marriott terminated the agreement on November 7, 2025, citing default; Sonder notified
Marriott of a planned abrupt liquidation days later; Marriott made its own termination public on
November 9, 2025 — a separate, later date. On November 10, Sonder abruptly ceased operations,
telling guests at its properties to vacate with roughly a day's notice in some cases. On November
14, 2025, Sonder Holdings and 12 affiliated entities filed for Chapter 7 — liquidation, not
reorganization — in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware.

According to Marriott's own court filing, Sonder threatened to lock guests out of their rooms
"without regard to whether those rooms contained medication, passports, personal effects" unless
Marriott financed its wind-down. Sonder's own public explanation was softer, citing "unanticipated
delays and costs" from the Marriott integration itself. Both accounts are presented, attributed,
in this video — along with analyst Daniel Lesser's counterpoint that the ending was largely baked
in well before Marriott ever entered the picture.

This video also covers Sonder's two smaller companions in this category: Lyric ($185M raised,
Airbnb-led round), which was nearly wiped out by COVID in 2020 but whose pricing software survives
today as Wheelhouse, with its brand later revived by Black Swan Holdings in 2023 — and Zeus Living
($150M raised, also Airbnb-backed), which survived the COVID shock that killed Lyric, then wound
down slowly, handing its book of business to competitor Blueground in November 2023.

All three companies ran a version of the same master-lease model as this channel's own video on
Stay Alfred (package 021) — cross-referenced explicitly throughout. The core thesis: going public
didn't fix the underlying math. It just gave the same structural flaw more time and a bigger stage
to fail on.
