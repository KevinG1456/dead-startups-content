# Blog Article: The Bridge Round Death Spiral: 4 Companies, 4 "One Last Raise" Stories

*All figures sourced to Bloomberg, CNBC, TechCrunch, Construction Dive, Forbes, Bloomberg Law, The
Information, and Retail Dive — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Each company's specific
"bridge" mechanism is stated precisely and kept distinct throughout — these are not four versions of
the same story, but four different structures that failed the same way. No individual across any of
the four companies was charged with fraud or any crime.*

## Introduction
Most of this channel's videos follow one company from rise to collapse. This one follows four —
Katerra, Veev, Plastiq, and Enjoy Technology — chosen specifically because they operate in unrelated
industries (construction tech, prefab housing, B2B payments, mobile retail) and still hit the exact
same moment: real financial distress, followed by an attempt at "one more round," specifically framed
as a bridge to survival. In every case, the bridge either didn't close, or closed and still didn't
save the company.

## Katerra: Rescued Twice, Still Gone
Katerra was founded in 2015 by Michael Marks, the former CEO of Flextronics and former interim CEO of
Tesla, aiming to vertically integrate construction — design, manufacturing, and building — under one
company. In January 2018, an $865 million round led by SoftBank Vision Fund valued Katerra above $3
billion; SoftBank's cumulative investment eventually reached roughly $2 billion.

As distress set in, SoftBank stepped in twice. In May 2020, it provided a $200 million emergency
infusion alongside a new CEO brought in to fix the company's finances. Just seven months later, in
December 2020, SoftBank provided a second $200 million infusion — reported at the time as its final
cash infusion — and took a majority stake; its lender, Greensill Capital, canceled $435 million of
Katerra's debt for a 5% equity stake.

Then, in early 2021, Greensill Capital itself collapsed in an unrelated financial scandal, eliminating
the working-capital lifeline Katerra depended on. On June 7, 2021, Katerra filed for Chapter 11
bankruptcy — and received a third infusion, a $35 million loan from SoftBank, explicitly to fund a
60-day asset sale rather than to save the company.

## Veev: The Bridge Loan Nobody Would Give
Veev started in 2008 as a real estate developer, pivoting around 2018 toward prefabricated, tech-
forward home construction under founder Amit Haller. In March 2022, a $400 million round pushed Veev
to unicorn status, bringing its total funding to roughly $600-650 million.

By late 2023, Veev tried to raise a new round. It failed. Veev then specifically tried to secure a
bridge loan as a fallback — not a vague "search for funding," but a named, structured attempt at
bridge financing. That failed too. On November 27, 2023, Veev told staff it was shutting down,
citing an inability to secure additional funding given market conditions. Roughly 250 employees were
laid off.

## Plastiq: The SPAC as a Public-Market Bridge
Plastiq, founded in 2012 by Eliot Buchanan and Dan Choi, built a B2B bill-payment platform letting
businesses pay bills by credit card even when the recipient didn't accept cards directly. In January
2022, a funding round valued the company at $940 million.

Plastiq's "big last raise" attempt looked different from the others: a SPAC merger, announced in
August 2022 with Colonnade Acquisition Corp II, at an implied $480 million valuation, expected to add
more than $300 million to Plastiq's balance sheet. In September 2022, in the middle of pursuing that
deal, Plastiq acquired Nearside, a digital-banking product for small businesses, for $130 million. By
late 2022, Plastiq determined the SPAC deal wasn't viable and backed out — triggering a breach-of-
agreement threat from Colonnade in February 2023.

Then, in March 2023, Silicon Valley Bank — Plastiq's own primary bank — collapsed, compounding an
already dire cash position. On May 24, 2023, Plastiq filed Chapter 11, having missed a $2 million
interest payment on a $43 million loan despite multiple extensions. It survived the bankruptcy process
on $7.1 million in debtor-in-possession financing before its assets sold to Priority Technology
Holdings for $27.5 million.

## Enjoy Technology: The Buyer as the Bridge
Enjoy Technology was founded in 2014 by Ron Johnson, formerly Apple's SVP of Retail and later CEO of
JCPenney, built around mobile "retail-as-a-service" — vans and staff bringing a store experience to
customers, with AT&T as a major retail partner. On October 15, 2021, Enjoy completed a SPAC merger at
a roughly $1.2 billion implied valuation, raising up to $454 million.

Early 2022 brought a fast-moving crisis: a shortage of Apple's latest iPhones gutted Enjoy's AT&T-
driven sales just as the company expanded staffing and warehouses for entry into roughly 100 new
markets. On June 30, 2022 — barely eight months after going public — Enjoy filed for Chapter 11 with
just $523,000 in cash on hand, not enough to make its next weekly payroll.

To survive the bankruptcy process, Enjoy took a $2.5 million bridge loan and a $55 million financing
commitment from Asurion, a technology-insurance company — which became the stalking-horse bidder and
ultimately bought Enjoy's mobile-retail business for $110 million. The company that bridged Enjoy
through its final weeks is the same company that ended up owning what was left of it.

## The Actual Lesson
Four companies. Four industries. Four differently structured bridges — repeat emergency equity, a
named bridge loan, a SPAC merger functioning as a public-market bridge, and bankruptcy financing
supplied by an eventual buyer. All four failed to keep the company alive as an independent business.

The pattern worth taking away isn't "these companies made bad decisions" — a bridge round is a
reasonable bet when a company's situation is genuinely likely to improve before the bridge runs out.
The pattern is that a bridge round is only as good as what's waiting on the other side of it, and in
all four of these cases, nothing was. In two of the four — Enjoy and, in a different form, Katerra's
bankruptcy-stage financing — the party providing the bridge ended up as the one holding the assets
when it was over.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A bridge round is a bet on what happens after it closes, not a solution in itself** — in all four
  of these cases, the underlying problem (funding-market conditions, industry-specific demand shocks,
  a failed public listing) didn't resolve before the bridge ran out
- **Repeat rescues from the same investor are a warning sign, not a safety net** — Katerra's second
  $200 million infusion didn't fix what the first one hadn't fixed
- **A SPAC merger can function as a company's "bridge," and it carries the same risk as any other one**
  — Plastiq's failed de-SPAC behaved exactly like a failed private bridge round, just in public-market
  form
- **When a bridge lender also becomes the eventual buyer, that's not a coincidence** — a lender
  financing a distressed company through its final weeks is often positioning to acquire its assets
  cheaply, as happened with both Enjoy Technology and, in a modified form, Katerra
- **The size of a company's prior funding says nothing about its ability to raise a bridge** — Katerra
  had raised roughly $2 billion and still couldn't raise a fourth round; Veev had just reached unicorn
  status and still couldn't secure a bridge loan

## The Lesson
Bridge rounds are common enough in startup finance that the term itself sounds routine — a normal,
manageable step on the way to a bigger raise or a return to stability. These four companies show what
happens when the bridge doesn't lead anywhere: the underlying distress that made the bridge necessary
in the first place doesn't go away just because the round closes, or because the company tries hard
enough to make one close.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What is a "bridge round"?**
A round of financing meant to carry a company through a period of distress until it can raise a
larger round, get acquired, or reach stability — a temporary solution, not a permanent one.

**2. What companies does this video cover?**
Katerra (construction tech), Veev (prefab home construction), Plastiq (B2B bill payments), and Enjoy
Technology (mobile retail).

**3. What do these four companies have in common?**
Each hit real financial distress and tried "one more round," specifically framed as a bridge — and in
every case, the bridge either didn't close or didn't actually save the company.

**4. How much did Katerra raise before it collapsed?**
Roughly $2 billion, mostly from SoftBank Vision Fund, including an $865 million round that valued it
above $3 billion.

**5. How many times did SoftBank rescue Katerra?**
Two separate $200 million emergency infusions (May 2020 and December 2020) before Katerra's June 2021
bankruptcy — plus a third, smaller $35 million loan after the bankruptcy filing, specifically to fund
an asset sale.

**6. What ended Katerra's ability to raise more money?**
The collapse of Greensill Capital, a SoftBank-backed lender that had been financing Katerra's working
capital, in an unrelated financial scandal in early 2021.

**7. When did Katerra file for bankruptcy?**
June 7, 2021.

**8. What happened to Veev?**
It reached unicorn status with a $400 million round in March 2022, then failed to raise a new round or
secure a bridge loan by late 2023, and shut down on November 27, 2023.

**9. How much had Veev raised in total?**
Roughly $600-650 million.

**10. What specifically did Veev try before shutting down?**
A named, structured bridge loan from investors, as a fallback after a new funding round failed to
materialize.

**11. What happened to Plastiq?**
It tried to go public via a SPAC merger as its "big last raise" in 2022; the deal collapsed, its own
bank (Silicon Valley Bank) failed in March 2023, and it filed Chapter 11 in May 2023.

**12. How much had Plastiq raised in total?**
Approximately $141.8 million, per Crunchbase.

**13. What was Plastiq's peak valuation?**
$940 million, in a January 2022 funding round.

**14. Why did Plastiq's SPAC deal fall apart?**
Plastiq determined the merger with Colonnade Acquisition Corp II was no longer viable and backed out
in late 2022; Colonnade then threatened litigation over an alleged breach of the merger agreement.

**15. How did Silicon Valley Bank's collapse affect Plastiq?**
SVB was Plastiq's primary bank; its March 2023 failure compounded Plastiq's already dire cash
position in the lead-up to its own bankruptcy filing two months later.

**16. What happened to Plastiq's assets?**
They sold to Priority Technology Holdings Inc. for $27.5 million, in a sale completed August 1, 2023.

**17. What happened to Enjoy Technology?**
It went public via SPAC in October 2021, faced a cash crisis from an iPhone supply shortage, and filed
Chapter 11 in June 2022 with just $523,000 in the bank.

**18. Who founded Enjoy Technology?**
Ron Johnson, formerly Apple's SVP of Retail and later CEO of JCPenney.

**19. What was Enjoy Technology's business model?**
"Retail-as-a-service" — mobile vans and staff bringing a retail-store experience to customers, with
AT&T as a major partner selling phones this way.

**20. How much cash did Enjoy Technology have when it filed for bankruptcy?**
$523,000 — not enough to cover its next weekly payroll.

**21. How did Enjoy Technology survive its bankruptcy process?**
On a $2.5 million bridge loan and a $55 million financing commitment from Asurion, a technology-
insurance company.

**22. What happened to Enjoy Technology's business afterward?**
Asurion, its bridge lender, became the stalking-horse bidder and ultimately bought Enjoy's mobile-
retail business for $110 million.

**23. Was any of this caused by fraud?**
No — all four are financing-mechanism and distress-financing stories, not fraud stories. No individual
was charged with any crime in connection with these events.

**24. Are any of these four companies still operating independently today?**
No — all four ceased operating as independent businesses, either through liquidation, asset sale, or
acquisition of their remaining assets.

**25. What's the difference between Katerra's and Veev's "bridges"?**
Katerra's bridges were repeat emergency equity infusions from its single dominant investor (SoftBank).
Veev's bridge was a specifically named, structured bridge loan that ultimately failed to materialize.

**26. What's the difference between Plastiq's and Enjoy Technology's "bridges"?**
Plastiq's bridge was effectively a failed SPAC merger — a public-market bridge — followed by real
bankruptcy DIP financing. Enjoy's bridge was DIP financing supplied directly by the company that ended
up buying it.
