# Blog Article: Dropbox Paid $100 Million for an App That Was 37 Days Old. Under Three Years Later, It Was Gone.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, Forbes, Engadget, Fortune, CNN Business, and a 2023 YourStory
retrospective — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. The $100 million price and the "37
days" launch-to-acquisition figure are both reported numbers from press coverage, never confirmed
exactly by Dropbox — this article treats them as such throughout.*

## Introduction
Some acquisition stories are about a company running out of runway and getting bought as a rescue.
Mailbox's story is the opposite: a genuinely loved product, acquired at the height of its hype, for real
money, with real enthusiasm — and still, within a few years, gone. That gap between the announcement and
the ending is the actual story.

## The Founding
Gentry Underwood and Scott Cannon originally built Orchestra, a shared to-do-list app meant to get people
to stop using their email inbox as a to-do list. Orchestra raised a $5.3 million Series A round in
November 2011, led by Charles River Ventures, with SV Angel, Kapor Capital, and CrunchFund
participating. In 2012, the team pivoted from Orchestra to a new idea entirely: Mailbox, an iOS email
app built around swipe gestures and a "snooze" feature that let you defer a message to reappear later.

## The Video and the Waitlist
In December 2012, Mailbox released a demo video showing off its gestures — swipe right to archive, swipe
further right to delete, swipe left to snooze a message for later. The video drew 100,000 views within
four hours. Rather than releasing the app to everyone immediately, Mailbox used a "velvet rope" virtual
waitlist: download the app, and you'd see your live position in a queue, along with how many people were
ahead of and behind you. Over 800,000 people signed up.

## The Launch
The app opened to the public App Store in February 2013, with the virtual queue continuing to admit
waitlisted users gradually rather than all at once. Within about a month, reported figures put Mailbox
at 1.3 million reservations and as many as 60 million messages processed per day.

## The Acquisition
Here's the number that makes this story worth telling precisely: on March 15, 2013, Dropbox announced it
was acquiring Mailbox. Multiple retrospectives — most directly a 2023 YourStory piece — report that only
37 days had passed between Mailbox's public launch and the announced deal. The reported price was around
$100 million, though Dropbox has never confirmed an exact figure. All 13 Mailbox/Orchestra employees
joined Dropbox, and Mailbox kept running as a standalone app at first. Dropbox co-founder Drew Houston's
blog post framed the acquisition enthusiastically, describing the two companies' goals as closely
aligned — though the exact wording varies across secondary coverage, and this article doesn't put an
unverified direct quote in his mouth.

## What $100 Million Actually Bought
Gentry Underwood became head of product and design for Dropbox's collaboration products. Scott Cannon
became head of engineering for Dropbox's Paper product. In other words: the founders were folded into
Dropbox's broader product organization, not kept running Mailbox as a discrete, protected team. That
detail matters more than it might seem at the time — it's the first quiet signal of what was actually
being bought.

## The Promises
In 2014, Dropbox announced plans for an Android version of Mailbox and a Mac desktop app. Both were
real, publicly stated commitments.

## The Copy
And then, according to retrospective coverage, real development on Mailbox itself slowed sharply. No
on-record Dropbox statement explains the internal reasons why — this article presents that as reported
analysis, not a company admission. What's better documented is what happened around Mailbox while it
stalled: Yahoo Mail, Microsoft Outlook, and Google's own new "Inbox by Gmail" all adopted swipe-and-
snooze gestures similar to Mailbox's own signature feature. The idea that made Mailbox famous kept
spreading. Mailbox itself did not.

## The Quiet Years
By 2015, Mailbox had gone roughly two years with no major new features, even as the products that had
copied its ideas kept shipping updates of their own.

## The Shutdown
On December 7, 2015, Dropbox announced it was shutting down both Mailbox and its separate Carousel photo
app, to refocus company resources on collaboration and file-sharing products — most notably the newer
Paper app. This was Dropbox's own stated strategic reasoning, not attributed to any outside platform or
competitor action. Mailbox actually closed on February 26, 2016; Carousel closed separately on March 31,
2016 — two distinct products, two distinct dates.

From the March 2013 acquisition to the app's actual February 2016 closure: just under three years.

## The Actual Lesson
The enthusiasm in Dropbox's 2013 announcement wasn't necessarily insincere. But enthusiasm at the moment
of signing doesn't predict what resources, attention, and roadmap priority a product actually gets
afterward — especially once its founders are folded into other parts of the acquiring company's org
chart, working on other products entirely.

Getting acquired fast and getting acquired well are two different things. The real test of an
acquisition isn't the announcement — it's what the product looks like two or three years later.

## What Founders and Product Leaders Should Take From This
- **A fast, enthusiastic deal doesn't guarantee a roadmap** — the speed and warmth of an acquisition
  announcement says little about what actually happens to the product afterward
- **Watch where the founders end up, not just what gets said in the press release** — Underwood and
  Cannon moving into other Dropbox product roles was a real, visible signal about what was actually
  being prioritized
- **A copied feature can outlive the product that invented it** — Mailbox's gestures spread to Yahoo
  Mail, Outlook, and Google's Inbox while Mailbox itself sat un-updated
- **A stated strategic pivot is a real reason, even if it's a hard one to hear** — Dropbox's own
  explanation (refocusing on collaboration products) is the actual documented cause here, not a platform
  action or a competitor's move
- **The real timeline of an acquisition's outcome is measured in years, not headlines** — the true
  story of this deal isn't the March 2013 announcement, it's everything that did and didn't happen in
  the roughly three years after it

## The Lesson
Mailbox's story is usually told as the launch hype: the viral video, the waitlist, the fast $100 million
deal. The more precise, more useful version treats the acquisition as the midpoint, not the ending — and
asks what actually happened to a genuinely loved product once the announcement enthusiasm had to turn
into sustained investment.
