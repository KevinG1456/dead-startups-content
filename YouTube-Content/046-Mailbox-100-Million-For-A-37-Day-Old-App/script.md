# Script Package

Anchored on Mailbox — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS
SCRIPT: present the ~$100 million price and the "37 days" launch-to-acquisition gap as reported,
attributed figures (TechCrunch; YourStory retrospective) — never as unqualified exact facts. Do not put
an unverified direct "we're thrilled" quote in Drew Houston's mouth on screen. Keep Mailbox's Feb 26,
2016 shutdown date distinct from Carousel's separate Mar 31, 2016 date. This is NOT a "both companies
died" story — Dropbox is alive and independently operating; never imply otherwise.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 11–13 minutes (the founding, the launch hype, the acquisition, the stall, and the
shutdown all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Dropbox paid a reported $100 million for an app that had existed for 37 days. Under three years
  later, it was gone."
- Visual: a small "37 DAYS" tag next to a large "$100 MILLION" numeral, then both fading to gray
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a story about running out of
  money. It's a story about what happens after the check clears."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Mailbox — an iOS email app so hyped in early 2013 that people waited in a live virtual
  line just to try it. Dropbox bought it barely a month after launch."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how fast this deal happened, what
  Dropbox actually did with what it bought, and exactly how it ended."

### Introduction (1:00–1:35)
- Method statement: reconstructed from TechCrunch, Forbes, Engadget, Fortune, CNN Business, and a 2023
  YourStory retrospective — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "One honesty note up front: the $100 million price and
  the '37 days' figure are both reported numbers from press coverage, not numbers Dropbox has ever
  confirmed exactly — I'll flag that every time they come up."

### Main Content — The Rise (1:35–4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:35–2:10):** Gentry Underwood and Scott Cannon originally built Orchestra,
  a shared to-do-list app, raising a $5.3 million Series A in November 2011 (Charles River Ventures, SV
  Angel, Kapor Capital, CrunchFund). The team pivoted to Mailbox — an email-triage app — in 2012. Visual:
  generic early-2010s startup-office graphic.
- **Segment 2 — The Video and the Waitlist (2:10–3:15):** a December 2012 demo video showing swipe-to-
  archive, swipe-to-delete, and "snooze" gestures gets 100,000 views in four hours. Instead of releasing
  immediately, Mailbox uses a "velvet rope" virtual waitlist — download the app, see your live position
  in line. Over 800,000 people sign up. Visual: "800,000+ ON THE WAITLIST" title card with a mock live-
  queue-counter graphic.
- **Segment 3 — The Launch (3:15–4:30):** the app opens to the public App Store in February 2013,
  admitting waitlisted users gradually. Reported figures at the time: 1.3 million reservations, up to 60
  million messages a day. Visual: "FEBRUARY 2013 — PUBLIC LAUNCH" title card.

### Main Content — The Deal (4:30–7:00)
- **Segment 4 — The Acquisition (4:30–6:00):** March 15, 2013 — reported as just 37 days after launch —
  Dropbox announces it's acquiring Mailbox. Reported price: around $100 million. All 13 employees join
  Dropbox; Mailbox keeps running as a standalone app at first. Drew Houston's blog post frames the deal
  enthusiastically, saying the two companies' goals aligned closely. On-screen framing card: "The exact
  wording of Dropbox's announcement varies across retrospectives — this video uses the best-sourced
  paraphrase, not an unverified direct quote." Visual: "$100 MILLION — REPORTED PRICE, 37 DAYS AFTER
  LAUNCH" title card.
- **Segment 5 — What $100 Million Actually Bought (6:00–7:00):** Gentry Underwood becomes head of
  product and design for Dropbox's collaboration products; Scott Cannon becomes head of engineering for
  Dropbox's Paper product. The founders are folded into Dropbox's broader org — not kept running Mailbox
  as a standalone unit. Visual: simple org-chart graphic showing the founders moving into new Dropbox
  roles.

### Main Content — The Stall (7:00–9:30)
- **Segment 6 — The Promises (7:00–8:00):** in 2014, Dropbox announces plans for an Android version and
  a Mac desktop app. Visual: "ANDROID + MAC — PROMISED, 2014" title card.
- **Segment 7 — The Copy (8:00–9:00):** meanwhile, real development on Mailbox itself slows sharply.
  Competing products — Yahoo Mail, Microsoft Outlook, and Google's own new "Inbox by Gmail" — adopt
  swipe-and-snooze gestures similar to Mailbox's own signature feature. On-screen framing card: "No
  on-record Dropbox statement explains the internal reasons development slowed — this is retrospective
  analysis, not a company admission." Visual: side-by-side generic app-icon graphic, Mailbox's gesture
  icon appearing to "spread" into three competitor icons.
- **Segment 8 — The Quiet Years (9:00–9:30):** by 2015, Mailbox has gone roughly two years with no major
  new features, even as the products that copied its ideas keep shipping updates. Visual: a simple
  "LAST MAJOR UPDATE" vs. "TODAY" date-gap graphic.

### Main Content — The Ending (9:30–11:00)
- **Segment 9 — The Shutdown Announcement (9:30–10:15):** December 7, 2015, Dropbox announces it's
  shutting down both Mailbox and its separate Carousel photo app, to refocus resources on collaboration
  products — most notably the newer Paper app. This is Dropbox's own stated strategic reasoning, not
  blamed on any outside platform or competitor. Visual: "SHUTDOWN ANNOUNCED — DECEMBER 7, 2015" title
  card.
- **Segment 10 — The Actual Closure (10:15–11:00):** Mailbox closes for good on February 26, 2016.
  Carousel closes separately, March 31, 2016 — two distinct products, two distinct dates. Just under
  three years after the $100 million acquisition that made headlines for its enthusiasm and speed.
  Visual: "MAILBOX CLOSED — FEBRUARY 26, 2016" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:45): "This channel usually covers companies that ran out of money or got cut off by
  a platform. This is the rare one where getting bought is the thing that actually ended it. Subscribe
  for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson (11:00–12:15)
- Plain-language comparison: the enthusiasm in Dropbox's 2013 announcement wasn't necessarily insincere
  — but enthusiasm at the moment of acquisition doesn't predict what resources, attention, and roadmap
  priority a product actually gets afterward, especially once its founders are folded into other parts
  of the acquiring company's org chart.
- The core lesson, stated directly: getting acquired fast and getting acquired well are two different
  things — and the real test of an acquisition isn't the announcement, it's what the product looks like
  two or three years later.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — Orchestra founded (2011) → pivot to Mailbox (2012) → viral demo
  video (Dec 2012) → public launch (Feb 2013) → Dropbox acquisition (Mar 2013) → Android/Mac promised
  (2014) → competitors adopt the gestures (2014-15) → shutdown announced (Dec 2015) → Mailbox closes
  (Feb 2016).

### Final CTA + Outro (12:15–13:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a fast, enthusiastic acquisition is not the same thing as a
  product's future being secured, and the real story is always what happens in the years after the
  announcement, not the announcement itself
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and this is the first one centered on getting
  acquired as the actual cause, not a rescue"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you use Mailbox back in 2013? Do you think Dropbox ever really had a plan for
  it, or was this always going to end this way? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the $100M/37-days numeral graphic and the full-arc timeline, then works through the
founding, the launch, and the acquisition as supporting detail. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the
video as a genuine case study in post-acquisition product-integration risk.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the December 2012 viral demo video and the virtual waitlist itself — the genuine excitement —
before cutting hard to the December 2015 shutdown announcement, then rewinding to fill in everything in
between, including the acquisition. Slower pacing, holds the "37 days" reveal until the acquisition
segment, resolving with the Feb 2016 closure as the final beat. Strongest tonal-irony arc of the three
versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "one acquisition, three years of quiet decline" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
viral video, waitlist, launch, acquisition, promised platforms, competitors copying the gestures,
shutdown announcement, actual closure — landing on the acquisition-integration lesson as the final,
clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $100M/37-days contrast and the
"competitors copied the gesture, the app still died" detail.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the "promises" segment (7:00–8:00) if the Android/Mac detail feels like a dry
  product-roadmap footnote
- **Fix:** frame the unshipped Android/Mac promise explicitly as "the first visible sign the enthusiasm
  from the acquisition announcement wasn't translating into resources," connecting it directly forward
  to the competitors-copying-the-gestures segment
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general early-2010s email-app context if audience familiarity with
  the period is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the "under three years later, it was gone" contrast even
  more explicitly in the cold open if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 9 (9:30)
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "time since acquisition" corner counter visible
  from Segment 5 onward, ticking forward as the timeline advances toward the Feb 2016 closure
