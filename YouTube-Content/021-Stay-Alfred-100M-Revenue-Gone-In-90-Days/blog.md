# Blog Article: Stay Alfred Hit $100M a Year. It Announced Reopening. 13 Days Later, It Was Gone.

*All figures sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Where a specific figure is inconsistently reported (peak unit/city count, the exact Series A month), it's flagged inline rather than stated as one precise number.*

## Introduction
Most "COVID killed my startup" stories treat the pandemic as a blanket explanation. Stay Alfred's story is more specific and more instructive: a real, $100-million-a-year company with a particular cost structure — one that guaranteed landlords got paid no matter what — that turned a temporary demand shock into an immediate, total collapse. The company didn't just eventually run out of money. It publicly announced a reopening plan, then reversed that decision entirely just thirteen days later.

## The Founder and the Build
Stay Alfred was co-founded by CEO Jordan Allen, a former U.S. Army Ranger Captain with eight years of service including deployments to Iraq and Afghanistan, alongside co-founder Conrad Manfred — the company's name, "Alfred," is a portmanteau of both founders' surnames. Sources place the founding around 2011, with operations beginning in 2012. Allen bootstrapped the company for years before seriously considering outside capital around 2016. His original 2016 positioning line captures the model's distinguishing idea clearly: "Control of the entire guest experience is a major advantage for us. At the end of the day, most of our 'competitors' are marketplaces for someone else's widget. We're the marketplace and the widget."

## The Raise
Stay Alfred raised $15 million in a Series A, followed by a $47 million Series B in October 2018 led by Nine Four Ventures, a Chicago real-estate-tech fund, with Concentric Equity Partners participating — $62 million total. By the time of the Series B, the company had grown to roughly 2,000 apartments across 28 markets with 230 employees. By FY2019, revenue reached approximately $100 million, independently confirmed by CB Insights and Bisnow.

## The Model
Stay Alfred's business was master-lease arbitrage: it signed leases directly with landlords and developers — individual units, partial buildings, full buildings, and national developer partnerships, some with exclusivity clauses — guaranteeing the property owner fixed rent regardless of occupancy, then re-let the units as furnished short-term rentals to travelers. Allen's own pitch to landlords framed this as reducing the property owner's risk: it would "eliminate vacancy loss for the lease's duration and provide profit by increasing net operating income." The flip side of that pitch is the mechanism that later proved fatal — Stay Alfred itself absorbed 100% of the demand risk. If bookings fell, the lease payments to landlords didn't. At its approximate peak, the company operated somewhere between 2,000 and 2,500 apartments across roughly 30 cities — sources vary slightly by exact snapshot date, so this article presents it as a range rather than one precise figure.

## The Shock
In March 2020, COVID collapsed travel demand nationwide almost overnight. Stay Alfred was simultaneously working to close a rescue financing round of up to $30 million — investors pulled out at the last minute. On March 26, 2020, the company temporarily closed all of its properties. Employee count fell from over 230 to 221 (146 based in Spokane) before furloughs began in April, ultimately reducing the team to a skeleton crew of roughly 50 to help wind down operations.

## The Reversal
Here is the case's most underused, most dramatic beat. On May 7, 2020, Stay Alfred publicly announced a plan to resume bookings on August 1, with a smaller national footprint and reduced central staff. A company spokeswoman said: "We intend to resume normal operations as quickly and safely as possible." Thirteen days later, on May 20, 2020, founder Jordan Allen reversed that decision entirely and announced permanent closure. His own words: "amazing to think that a $100 million company in Spokane, Washington, could be wiped out by a virus from Wuhan, China."

## The Founder's Own Words
In a later reflection, Allen called the master-lease structure itself a "foundational crack," and said that in hindsight he would have leaned more toward an ownership-based model rather than concentrating so heavily on leases. That's a rare, direct, on-record admission of the specific structural choice that made the company's collapse so fast — not a vague appeal to "unprecedented circumstances," but a named mechanism.

## An Honest Complication
Not every part of Stay Alfred's collapse is a clean story. In Pennsylvania, the company attempted to remove tenants from a unit during the state's COVID shutdown; the tenants argued they were sheltering in place and entitled to eviction protections, and a judge ruled Stay Alfred could not remove them. The company's own spokesperson framed the attempt as compliance with a state order, not predation. Separately, Trammell Crow Residential sued Stay Alfred over $79,343 in unpaid April rent on one building — a case later dismissed — while Stay Alfred walked away entirely from other fully-controlled properties, including a tower in downtown Nashville and a set of apartments in downtown Savannah. These are the messy edge cases of a fast, real collapse, not evidence of intentional wrongdoing.

## The Actual Lesson
Stay Alfred's story isn't really a story about a pandemic — plenty of companies faced the same external shock and survived it, some barely, some by cutting deep. It's a story about a specific structural choice: guaranteeing a fixed obligation (rent to landlords) regardless of a variable input (occupancy from travelers) works fine as long as demand stays roughly steady, and becomes catastrophic the moment demand doesn't. Real revenue at real scale — $100 million a year, independently confirmed — did nothing to protect the company once that structural assumption broke.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **Map every fixed obligation in your model honestly** — not just debt, but leases, minimum commitments, and guarantees that don't flex with demand
- **Stress-test against a genuine demand collapse, not just a slowdown** — Stay Alfred's model could likely have absorbed a gradual downturn; it could not absorb demand going to near-zero within weeks
- **Watch what a founder says after the fact** — Allen's own "foundational crack" language is more instructive than any outside analysis, because it names the specific choice rather than blaming circumstances alone
- **Understand that reversing a public commitment is sometimes the right call, however painful** — the 13-day reversal from Stay Alfred's own reopening plan wasn't indecision; it was new information (the failed rescue raise, continued demand collapse) changing the right answer quickly

## The Lesson
A $100 million company disappeared in roughly eight weeks because its core business model guaranteed a fixed cost that couldn't flex when demand did the one thing the model wasn't built to survive. Real revenue at real scale is not the same thing as resilience.

# FAQ (30 Questions)

**1. What was Stay Alfred?**
A short-term-rental management startup founded in Spokane, WA, that leased apartments directly from landlords and re-let them to travelers as furnished short-term rentals.

**2. Who founded Stay Alfred?**

**3. Where does the name "Stay Alfred" come from?**
"Alfred" is a portmanteau of the two founders' surnames, Allen and Manfred.

**4. How much revenue did Stay Alfred have?**
Roughly $100 million in FY2019, independently confirmed by CB Insights and Bisnow.

**5. How much did Stay Alfred raise?**
$62 million total — a $15 million Series A and a $47 million Series B (October 2018, led by Nine Four Ventures).

**6. Why did Stay Alfred shut down?**
Its master-lease business model guaranteed landlords fixed rent regardless of occupancy, meaning Stay Alfred itself absorbed all the demand risk. When COVID collapsed travel demand in March 2020, that fixed-cost structure turned a temporary shock into an immediate, total collapse — and a last-minute rescue financing round fell through.

**7. What is master-lease arbitrage?**
A business model where a company signs fixed-rent leases with property owners, then re-lets those properties (often furnished, often short-term) to generate revenue that may or may not exceed the fixed lease cost.

**8. When did Stay Alfred shut down?**
It temporarily closed all properties on March 26, 2020, and permanently closed on May 20, 2020.

**9. Did Stay Alfred try to reopen?**
Yes — on May 7, 2020, it publicly announced a plan to resume bookings August 1. Thirteen days later, on May 20, it reversed that decision and shut down permanently instead.

**12. How many employees did Stay Alfred have?**

**13. How many properties did Stay Alfred operate?**

**14. What happened to the rescue funding round?**

**18. What did Jordan Allen do after Stay Alfred?**
He launched Ruumr Inc., an online residential real-estate offer/bidding platform, live by June 2021, which appears to have later rebranded to Doorsey Inc.

**19. Were other short-term-rental startups affected by COVID the same way?**
Yes — Lyric (raised $160M) also collapsed quickly in mid-2020. Zeus Living (raised $150M) survived the initial shock but wound down more slowly, in November 2023. Sonder, the category's largest player, went public via SPAC and later faced its own struggles.

**20. Was Stay Alfred's model unique in this industry?**
No — master-lease arbitrage was common among short-term-rental startups of this era; Stay Alfred's story is a clear, well-documented example of the risk that model carries.

**21. Was COVID the only cause of Stay Alfred's collapse?**
COVID was the trigger, but the specific reason the collapse was immediate and total (rather than gradual) was the fixed-lease-obligation structure — this video's central point is distinguishing the trigger from the structural mechanism.

**22. How is this different from a typical "startup ran out of money" story?**
Most "ran out of money" stories involve a gradual burn against a finite runway. Stay Alfred had real, substantial revenue right up until the shock — the story is about structural fragility, not slow decline.

**23. What should founders take from this if they're not in real estate?**
Any fixed obligation that doesn't flex with demand — not just leases, but minimum commitments, guarantees, or long-term contracts — carries this same structural risk.

**24. Is master-lease arbitrage inherently a bad business model?**
Not inherently — it can work well when demand is stable — but it carries a specific, undiversifiable exposure to sudden demand collapse that more variable-cost models don't.
