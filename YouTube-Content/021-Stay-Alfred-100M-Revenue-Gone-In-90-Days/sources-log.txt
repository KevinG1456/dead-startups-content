BUSINESS MODEL — master-lease arbitrage, precisely

- Stay Alfred used four lease structures: individual-unit leases, partial-building
  leases, full-building leases, and national developer partnerships — several
  including exclusivity clauses preventing other short-term-rental operators from
  the same building.
- The fatal mechanism: Stay Alfred bore the FULL fixed lease obligation to
  landlords regardless of its own occupancy — landlords got guaranteed rent no
  matter what; Stay Alfred absorbed 100% of the vacancy/demand risk.
- Allen's own pitch to landlords/developers (pre-collapse) framed this as
  risk-REDUCING for the property owner: it "eliminat[es] vacancy loss for the
  lease's duration and provid[es] profit by increasing net operating income (NOI)"
  — i.e., the entire value proposition to landlords was that Stay Alfred, not them,
  held the risk.
- Scale trajectory (use for an on-screen growth graphic, note the approximation):
  2016 — 13 cities, ~$13M revenue through mid-year, projecting >$25M for the full
  year, 60 HQ + 6 remote staff. October 2018 (Series B) — ~2,000 units, 28 markets,
  230 employees. Later/peak figures vary by source and snapshot date: Spokesman-
  Review reports "2,500 apartments... in 33 cities"; a Bisnow bio separately cites
  "30 downtown neighborhoods... 1.5M+ sq ft under management" and "32 U.S. cities."
  [Present peak scale as an approximate range — "roughly 2,000-2,500 units, ~30
  cities" — not a single precise peak figure, since sources disagree by a small
  margin depending on exact date.]
- Named competitors in the same master-lease category: Sonder (described as "the
  next largest venture-backed firm" in this space, ahead of Stay Alfred), The Guild,
  Domio, Lyric, Zeus Living.
- Minor regulatory friction noted in passing: Seattle's short-term-rental tax rules
  restricted entire-unit rentals, complicating hotel-style operations there — a
  real but secondary headwind, not the core structural risk.
Source: shorttermrentalz.com ("The Master Lease Model: Has The Bubble Burst?"),
GeekWire, Bisnow, Spokesman-Review.

THE COVID COLLAPSE — precisely dated sequence

1. Early March 2020 (before the shutdown): Allen and leadership were meeting with
   investors to close a rescue round of up to $30M. Investors pulled the funding
   "at the last minute."
2. March 26, 2020: Stay Alfred temporarily closes properties nationwide.
3. April 2020: Furloughs begin. Employee count immediately before furloughs: 221
   total, 146 based in Spokane (down from 230+ pre-COVID). Workforce reduced to a
   skeleton crew of roughly 50 to help wind down operations.
4. May 7, 2020: Company PUBLICLY ANNOUNCES a plan to resume bookings August 1,
   2020, with a smaller national footprint and reduced Spokane/central staff.
   Company spokeswoman: "We intend to resume normal operations as quickly and
   safely as possible."
5. May 20, 2020 — just 13 days after that reopening announcement — Allen reverses
   course entirely and announces PERMANENT closure. Quote, widely carried:
   "amazing to think that a $100 million company in Spokane, Washington, could be
   wiped out by a virus from Wuhan, China."
   [THIS 13-DAY REVERSAL IS THE VIDEO'S STRONGEST, MOST UNDERUSED DRAMATIC BEAT —
   the company publicly committed to reopening, then killed itself off less than
   two weeks later. Lead the shutdown segment with this whiplash, not just the
   final closure date.]
Source: Spokesman-Review (multiple dated articles), Spokane Journal of Business.

FOUNDER'S OWN ACCOUNT

- The "foundational crack" quote, in full context: Allen acknowledged the
  master-lease structure itself was a "foundational crack," and said that in
  hindsight he would have leaned more toward an OWNERSHIP-based model rather than
  concentrating so heavily on master leases.
- The "wiped out by a virus" quote (see above) directly underscores the video's
  actual thesis: real scale ($100M revenue) does not equal resilience when the
  underlying cost structure is fixed regardless of demand.
Source: shorttermrentalz.com, Spokane Journal of Business.

AFTERMATH

- Landlord litigation: Trammell Crow Residential (owner of the Alexan 20th Street
  Station building) sued Stay Alfred in early May 2020 over $79,343 in unpaid April
  rent — the case was later dismissed. Stay Alfred walked away entirely from
  buildings it had fully controlled, including the 505 Tower in downtown Nashville
  and 17 apartments in downtown Savannah under a master long-term lease.
Source: creditorsrights101.com.
- A separate, messier legal wrinkle in Pennsylvania: Stay Alfred attempted to
  remove tenants from a unit during the state's COVID shutdown; the tenants (who'd
  moved in March 20, contacted Stay Alfred April 2) argued they were now
  sheltering-in-place residents entitled to eviction protections; a PA judge ruled
  Stay Alfred could not oust them. Stay Alfred's spokesperson framed the removal
  attempt as simply "trying to comply with the governor's order," not predation.
  [Use as a brief complicating wrinkle showing the collapse's messy edge cases —
  not as a core narrative beat, and not framed as corporate villainy given the
  company's own stated compliance rationale.]
Source: Law360 (law360.com/articles/1261844).
- Founder's next act: Jordan Allen launched Ruumr Inc., an online residential
  real-estate offer/bidding platform, live by June 2021 (first listing June 10,
  2021, a three-bedroom Liberty Lake townhouse), reporting roughly $4M in "soft
  commitments" from investors at that stage. Team included former OddJobbers CEO
  Nick McLain and ex-Stay Alfred finance staffer Matt Melville. The company appears
  to have later rebranded to Doorsey Inc. (WA Dept of Revenue filing, likely 2021 —
  [VERIFY exact date] before stating on screen).
Source: Spokane Journal of Business, Spokesman-Review.
