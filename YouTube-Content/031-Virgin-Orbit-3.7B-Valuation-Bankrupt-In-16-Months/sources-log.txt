Sources Log — Package 031: Virgin Orbit
Filled in: 2026-08-15

WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Virgin Orbit is the anchor for this channel's "Money Mechanics" batch title #1: "The startup that
IPO'd and died within 24 months." It's the cleanest, fastest, most famous-name example of that
pattern found in this research pass — SPAC IPO close Dec 30, 2021 to Chapter 11 filing Apr 4, 2023
is roughly 15-16 months, well inside the 24-month window (FlightGlobal's own headline states "16
months after going public"). Compared to this channel's other SPAC casualties — Bird (028, ~25
months IPO-to-Chapter-11), 23andMe (027, ~3.5 years), WeWork (026, whose 2021 SPAC listing didn't
collapse the company at all, only its 2019 IPO attempt did) — Virgin Orbit is the fastest, and the
first true "IPO'd and died inside 24 months" case on this channel. Bonus real twist not found in any
prior package: an unrelated third party (Matthew Brown, a Texas man) submitted a fabricated $200
million rescue offer for Virgin Orbit days before the bankruptcy filing and was later sued by the
SEC for it — a rare case where a *bystander's* fraud, not the company's own conduct, becomes part of
the story.

CRITICAL ACCURACY NOTE — VIRGIN ORBIT vs. VIRGIN GALACTIC
Virgin Orbit and Virgin Galactic are TWO SEPARATE public companies, both founded under Richard
Branson's Virgin Group, both SPAC-era space companies — easily confused and must never be conflated.
Virgin Galactic (space tourism, ticker SPCE) had its own, well-documented 2021 shareholder securities
class-action lawsuit (Case No. 1:21-cv-03070). This research pass found NO equivalent named
shareholder class action specifically against Virgin Orbit (ticker VORB) — if a later pass finds one,
verify it names Virgin Orbit specifically before citing it; do not assume the Virgin Galactic
litigation applies here. Every file in this package must refer to "Virgin Orbit" precisely and never
interchange the two companies' facts, executives, tickers, or litigation.

FOUNDING AND BUSINESS MODEL
- Virgin Orbit was founded March 2, 2017, as a spin-off from Virgin Galactic, under Richard Branson's
  Virgin Group.
- Business model: LauncherOne, a small-satellite orbital rocket carried to launch altitude by a
  modified Boeing 747-400 named "Cosmic Girl," then air-dropped and ignited — a horizontal, mobile
  "air-launch" approach aimed at flexible, rapid-response small-satellite deployment, differentiated
  from vertical ground-launch competitors like Rocket Lab.
- Dan Hart became CEO in 2017 after 34 years at Boeing, most recently as VP of Government Satellite
  Systems overseeing Boeing's DoD/NASA/NOAA satellite programs.
Source: Wikipedia ("Virgin Orbit"); The CEO Magazine (Dan Hart profile).

THE SPAC MERGER AND IPO — precisely dated
- August 23, 2021: Virgin Orbit announced plans to go public via a SPAC merger with NextGen
  Acquisition Corp II, at a reported $3.7 billion valuation.
- December 30, 2021: the merger closed; Virgin Orbit began trading publicly under ticker VORB on
  Nasdaq. Stock opened at $10/share at a January 7, 2022 opening-bell ceremony.
- The deal was expected to raise $483 million (including a $100 million PIPE round from investors
  including Boeing and private equity firm AE Industrial Partners) but, due to SPAC-trust
  redemptions, actually netted only $228 million for the company — under half of what was expected.
  *** Note: one source (CMC Markets) states the deal "netted just $67.8 million" — this is a
  different, more specific net-cash-to-company figure vs. the $228 million gross-proceeds figure
  reported elsewhere (Wikipedia/CNBC). [VERIFY] which figure is being cited before stating a single
  number on screen — likely gross SPAC-trust proceeds ($228M) vs. net cash after fees/redemptions
  actually available to the company ($67.8M); if only one figure fits on screen, use $228 million
  (the more widely corroborated figure) and note it was still "less than half of what was expected." ***
Source: CNBC (Aug 23, 2021); Wikipedia ("Virgin Orbit"); CMC Markets ("From $3.7bn to $150m: what
led to Virgin Orbit's meteoric fall?").

THE LAUNCH RECORD — precisely as sourced
- Virgin Orbit attempted 6 orbital launches total between 2020 and 2023: 4 successes, 2 failures.
- May 25, 2020: maiden LauncherOne flight failed — first-stage engine shut down shortly after
  ignition, rocket did not reach orbit.
- January 17, 2021: first successful orbital launch ("Launch Demo 2").
- 2021: 2 successful flights total for the year.
- 2022: 2 additional successful flights (4 successes total by Q3 2022).
- January 9, 2023: "Start Me Up" mission — Virgin Orbit's first UK orbital launch attempt, from
  Spaceport Cornwall — failed to reach orbit, losing all 9 satellites from 7 different customers
  onboard.
- February 15, 2023: Virgin Orbit's own investigation revealed the root cause — a fuel filter within
  the fuel feedline had become dislodged from its normal position at the start of the second stage's
  first burn, causing the fuel pump to operate at reduced efficiency, under-fueling the engine, which
  overheated; components near the overheated engine then malfunctioned, causing the second-stage
  engine to shut down prematurely. Ground tests recreating flight conditions confirmed this as the
  initiating event. Virgin Orbit stated it modified the next rocket with a more robust filter.
Source: Space Explored ("Virgin Orbit – History, launches, and failure"); SpaceNews ("First Virgin
Orbit U.K. launch fails"); Space.com ("Virgin Orbit traces failed UK rocket launch to 'dislodged fuel
filter'"); Engadget; ITV News West Country; BBC ("Faulty fuel filter caused failed rocket launch").

THE FINANCIAL PICTURE AS A PUBLIC COMPANY — the "public market exposure" mechanism, precisely sourced
- Full-year 2021 revenue: $7.4 million (up from $3.8 million in 2020) — the company's first revenue
  figures disclosed under public-company SEC reporting requirements.
- Q3 2022: Virgin Orbit recognized more than $12 million in revenue following its fourth consecutive
  successful launch — but reported a net loss of $42.9 million for that SAME quarter. *** This is the
  core "public-market exposure revealing what private funding hid" mechanism for this package: as a
  private company (2017-2021), this revenue-to-loss ratio was invisible to the public; the SPAC deal's
  mandatory quarterly SEC filings (10-Q/10-K) made it visible for the first time, roughly a year
  before the January 2023 launch failure that triggered the final collapse. ***
Source: BusinessWire (Mar 29, 2022, Virgin Orbit Q4/FY2021 earnings press release); SEC.gov EDGAR
(Virgin Orbit Holdings 8-K, earnings press release exhibit); The Register (Aug 15, 2022, "Virgin
Orbit trims losses, eyes two final launches for 2022"); TechCrunch (Jan 10, 2023, "Virgin Orbit's
botched launch highlights shaky financial future").

THE COLLAPSE — precisely dated
- March 15, 2023: CEO Dan Hart told nearly all staff to pause operations, placing them on a
  week-long furlough while the company sought emergency funding.
- March 19-23, 2023: Matthew Brown, a Texas man with no verified space-industry investment track
  record, submitted an unsolicited $200 million rescue offer to Virgin Orbit, falsely portraying
  himself as an experienced venture capitalist with stakes in "over 13 space companies." He sent
  Virgin Orbit a fabricated screenshot claiming his company held $182 million in a bank account, when
  the real balance was less than $1. After signing an NDA with Virgin Orbit, and after his offer
  leaked to media, Brown appeared on CNBC on March 23, 2023, to publicly tout the offer. Virgin
  Orbit's stock rose approximately 33.1% on the news before the offer was exposed as fraudulent. The
  SEC filed a civil fraud suit against Brown and his company, Matthew Brown Companies, LLC, on June
  17, 2024 (SEC Litigation Release No. 26031). *** Framing note: this fraud is entirely on Brown, an
  unrelated third party — not on Virgin Orbit, Dan Hart, or Richard Branson. Virgin Orbit was the
  victim/target of this specific fraud, not a party to it. ***
- March 30, 2023: Virgin Orbit announced it would cease operations and lay off 675 employees —
  approximately 85% of its workforce — "to reduce expenses in light of the Company's inability to
  secure meaningful funding."
- April 4, 2023: Virgin Orbit filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the U.S. Bankruptcy Court
  for the District of Delaware, listing $243 million in assets against $153.5 million in total debt.
Source: CNBC (Mar 30, 2023, "Virgin Orbit funding plans fail, will stop operations, conduct
layoffs"); CNN Business (Mar 30, 2023); Fortune (Mar 31, 2023); Space.com ("Virgin Orbit files for
bankruptcy"); FlightGlobal (Apr 4, 2023, "Virgin Orbit files for bankruptcy 16 months after going
public"); SEC.gov Litigation Release No. 26031 (Jun 17, 2024, Matthew Brown); Bloomberg (Jun 17,
2024); CNBC (Jun 17, 2024, "SEC sues over alleged bogus bailout of Richard Branson's Virgin Orbit");
Investing.com/Reuters (Jun 17, 2024).

THE BANKRUPTCY AUCTION — precisely dated, sold for parts
- May 22-23, 2023: Virgin Orbit's assets were sold at a bankruptcy auction for approximately $36
  million total, split among three buyers:
  - Rocket Lab: $16.1 million for Virgin Orbit's Long Beach, California headquarters (~140,000 sq
    ft), which Rocket Lab said it would use to advance production of its own Neutron rocket.
  - Stratolaunch: $17 million "stalking horse" bid for the Cosmic Girl 747 carrier aircraft.
  - Vast/Launcher (Launcher, a subsidiary of Vast Space): $2.7 million for Virgin Orbit's Mojave,
    California facility, plus some machinery, equipment, and inventory.
  - Firefly Aerospace separately acquired approximately $3.8 million of additional assets in June
    2023, per Wikipedia.
- Virgin Orbit ceased operations entirely following the asset sale.
Source: CNBC (May 23, 2023, "Virgin Orbit shuts down after selling assets in bankruptcy auction");
NBC Los Angeles; Via Satellite; TechCrunch (May 23, 2023, "Virgin Orbit's launch business sold for
parts to Vast, Stratolaunch and Rocket Lab"); SpaceNews; Aerotech News & Review; Wikipedia.

CATEGORY / CROSS-REFERENCE NOTES
- This is the channel's 4th SPAC-era company (after 026/WeWork, 027/23andMe, 028/Bird) but the FIRST
  one built specifically around the "IPO'd and died within 24 months" speed pattern — the other three
  either took much longer to collapse (23andMe, ~3.5 years) or, in WeWork's case, the 2021 SPAC
  listing didn't cause the collapse at all (that was its failed 2019 IPO attempt; the actual 2023
  Chapter 11 was a reorganization the company emerged from, not a liquidation). Bird is the closest
  comparator in shape (SPAC IPO to Chapter 11) but still ran roughly 25 months — Virgin Orbit is
  faster. Good explicit on-screen comparison: "Bird took 25 months. Virgin Orbit took 16."
- Distinct failure mechanism from all three: not accounting misstatement (028/Bird), not a data
  breach (027/23andMe), not a failed first IPO followed by a later different listing (026/WeWork) —
  here it's a single, dated, root-cause-confirmed technical failure (a $100-part dislodged fuel
  filter) removing the one lever (successful launches → revenue → investor confidence) holding up a
  company whose public filings had already revealed a revenue-to-loss ratio that couldn't survive a
  funding gap.
