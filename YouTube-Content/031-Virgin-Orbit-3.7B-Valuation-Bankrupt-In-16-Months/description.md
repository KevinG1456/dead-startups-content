## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Virgin
Orbit — the fastest IPO-to-bankruptcy timeline this channel has covered so far, with a rocket-failure
detail and a fraud subplot most existing coverage never connects to the ending.

Virgin Orbit was founded March 2, 2017, as a spin-off from Virgin Galactic under Richard Branson's
Virgin Group. This is worth stating plainly up front: Virgin Orbit and Virgin Galactic are two
separate companies, with different tickers, different business models, and different histories —
this video is entirely about Virgin Orbit. Virgin Orbit built LauncherOne, a small-satellite rocket
carried to altitude by a modified Boeing 747 named "Cosmic Girl," then air-dropped and ignited. Dan
Hart became CEO in 2017 after 34 years at Boeing.

For nearly five years, Virgin Orbit operated as a private company, backed by roughly $1 billion from
Virgin Group and Abu Dhabi's Mubadala Investment Company. On August 23, 2021, it announced a SPAC
merger with NextGen Acquisition Corp II at a reported $3.7 billion valuation. The deal closed December
30, 2021; trading began on Nasdaq under ticker VORB, opening at $10 a share. The deal was expected to
raise $483 million but, after SPAC-trust redemptions, actually raised $228 million — less than half
of what was expected.

Going public meant quarterly SEC disclosures for the first time. Full-year 2021 revenue: $7.4
million. By Q3 2022, after a fourth successful launch, Virgin Orbit recognized over $12 million in
revenue — but reported a net loss of $42.9 million for that same quarter. This is the mechanism at
the center of this video: as a private company, this revenue-to-loss ratio was invisible. Going
public made it public record, a full year before the failure that most coverage treats as the cause
of the collapse.

Virgin Orbit attempted six orbital launches between 2020 and 2023 — four successes, two failures. On
January 9, 2023, its first UK orbital launch attempt, "Start Me Up," failed to reach orbit, destroying
all 9 customer satellites onboard. On February 15, 2023, the company's own investigation confirmed the
cause: a dislodged fuel filter caused the fuel pump to run inefficiently, under-fueling the engine,
which overheated and shut down prematurely.

On March 15, 2023, CEO Dan Hart paused operations and furloughed nearly all staff for a week while
seeking emergency funding. Between March 19 and 23, 2023, a Texas man named Matthew Brown submitted
an unsolicited $200 million rescue offer, falsely portraying himself as an experienced venture
capitalist. He sent a fabricated bank-statement screenshot claiming $182 million — his real balance
was less than $1. After the offer leaked, he appeared on CNBC touting it; Virgin Orbit's stock rose
about 33% before the offer was exposed as fake. On June 17, 2024, the SEC filed a civil fraud suit
against Brown (Litigation Release No. 26031). This fraud belongs entirely to Brown — Virgin Orbit was
the target, not a participant, and no one who worked at the company was accused of anything here.

On March 30, 2023, Virgin Orbit announced it would cease operations, laying off 675 employees — about
85% of its workforce. On April 4, 2023, the company filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Delaware, listing
$243 million in assets against $153.5 million in debt — 16 months after going public. On May 22-23,
2023, its assets sold at auction for about $36 million total: Rocket Lab paid $16.1 million for the
Long Beach headquarters, Stratolaunch paid $17 million for the Cosmic Girl 747, and Vast's Launcher
subsidiary paid $2.7 million for the Mojave test site.

In a 2024 interview, former CEO Dan Hart said Virgin Orbit "didn't raise enough money" and that its
"closest competitor had raised over three times what we had" — his own attributed words.

This channel has covered SPAC-era collapses before — Bird, package 028, took about 25 months from IPO
to bankruptcy. Virgin Orbit took 16.
