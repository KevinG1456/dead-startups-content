# Script Package

Anchored on Virgin Orbit — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR
THIS SCRIPT: Virgin Orbit and Virgin Galactic are two separate, easily confused Branson/Virgin Group
companies — never interchange their facts, tickers (VORB vs. SPCE), business models, or litigation
histories. The only fraud in this story belongs to Matthew Brown, an unrelated Texas man who faked a
rescue offer — never imply Virgin Orbit, Richard Branson, or Dan Hart committed or were charged with
fraud; none of them were. On the SPAC-proceeds figure: state $228 million (the more widely
corroborated gross figure) with "less than half of what was expected" framing — do not also state the
$67.8 million net-cash figure unless clearly distinguishing gross-vs-net. On the pre-IPO funding
total: present "about $1 billion invested by Virgin Group and Mubadala" as a reported aggregate, not
an audited figure. On Dan Hart's "didn't raise enough money" quote: attribute it as his own words —
do not insert "Rocket Lab" inside the quote itself, since his excerpted quote doesn't name a
competitor directly.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 14–17 minutes (founding, the SPAC IPO, the financial exposure, the launch
failure, the fake bailout, and the bankruptcy auction all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "This company went public at a $3.7 billion valuation. Sixteen months later, it filed for
  bankruptcy. The part that helped cause it — one specific mechanical failure — cost about $100."
- Visual: large "$3.7 BILLION" text, hard cut to a tiny glowing part icon dwarfed by a huge cracked
  rocket silhouette
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "The rocket wasn't the only thing that
  failed. Going public revealed the rest."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Virgin Orbit — founded in 2017 as a spin-off of Virgin Galactic, under Richard
  Branson's Virgin Group. Not to be confused with Virgin Galactic — that's a separate company, space
  tourism, a different ticker, a different story. Virgin Orbit built LauncherOne: a rocket that gets
  carried up by a modified 747 and dropped mid-air before it ignites. For almost five years, it
  operated privately, backed by roughly a billion dollars from Virgin Group and Abu Dhabi's Mubadala
  fund. Then, in December 2021, it went public."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly what going public revealed about
  this company's real finances a full year before its rocket ever failed — and exactly how a company
  worth $3.7 billion ended up selling its headquarters, its plane, and its test site for about $36
  million combined, sixteen months later. Plus one detail almost no coverage of this story connects
  to the ending: a man who offered $200 million to save the company, while he had less than $1 in his
  own account."

### Introduction (1:00–1:45)
- Method statement: reconstructed from Wikipedia, CNBC, Fortune, CNN Business, Space.com, SpaceNews,
  FlightGlobal, TechCrunch, The Register, BusinessWire/SEC EDGAR filings, and SEC.gov Litigation
  Release No. 26031 — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "Two things happened to this company that most coverage
  treats as separate stories, but they're connected. Going public forced this company to disclose
  numbers that had been invisible for almost five years. And about a year after that disclosure
  started, one rocket failure removed the one thing propping the company up. This video connects
  both."
- Distinction tease: "And keep one thing straight the whole way through: the fraud in this story
  belongs to one man, and it isn't anyone who worked at this company."

### Main Content — The Founding and Going Public (1:45–5:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:45–2:30):** Virgin Orbit founded March 2, 2017, spun off from Virgin
  Galactic under Richard Branson's Virgin Group. Business model: LauncherOne, a small-satellite
  rocket, carried to altitude by a modified 747 named "Cosmic Girl," then air-dropped and ignited.
  Dan Hart became CEO in 2017 after 34 years at Boeing. Visual: generic aerospace-hangar establishing
  shot, no real branding.
- **Segment 2 — Nearly Five Years Private (2:30–3:15):** Virgin Group and Mubadala together invested
  about $1 billion in the company through August 2021. On-screen framing card: "About a billion
  dollars — a reported figure, not a single company-confirmed total." During this period, the
  company's real financial performance was not publicly disclosed. Visual: dollar-figure graphic,
  "PRIVATE — NOT DISCLOSED" stamp.
- **Segment 3 — The SPAC Deal (3:15–4:15):** August 23, 2021, Virgin Orbit announced a SPAC merger
  with NextGen Acquisition Corp II at a reported $3.7 billion valuation. The deal closed December 30,
  2021; trading began on Nasdaq under ticker VORB, opening at $10 a share at a January 7, 2022 bell
  ceremony. The deal was expected to raise $483 million but, after redemptions, actually raised $228
  million — under half of what was expected. Visual: "$3.7 BILLION" valuation card, then "$228M
  RAISED — LESS THAN HALF OF WHAT WAS EXPECTED" card.
- **Segment 4 — What the Filings Showed (4:15–5:30):** as a newly public company, Virgin Orbit was
  now required to disclose quarterly financials. Full-year 2021 revenue: $7.4 million. By Q3 2022,
  after a fourth successful launch, revenue was over $12 million for the quarter — but the SAME
  quarter's net loss was $42.9 million. On-screen framing card, held for a beat: "This is the
  mechanism at the center of this story. As a private company, this ratio was invisible. Going public
  made it public record — a full year before the rocket failure that most coverage treats as the
  cause of the collapse." Visual: side-by-side "$12M revenue" vs. "$42.9M loss," same quarter, same
  company.

### Main Content — The Launch Failure (5:30–7:45)
- **Segment 5 — The Track Record (5:30–6:15):** Virgin Orbit attempted six orbital launches between
  2020 and 2023 — four successes, two failures. The maiden flight failed in May 2020. The first
  success came January 17, 2021. Visual: launch-record scoreboard, 4 checkmarks and 2 red marks.
- **Segment 6 — Start Me Up (6:15–7:45):** January 9, 2023, Virgin Orbit attempted its first UK
  orbital launch, "Start Me Up," from Spaceport Cornwall — its sixth launch overall. The mission
  failed, destroying all 9 customer satellites onboard. On February 15, 2023, the company's own
  investigation revealed the cause: a dislodged fuel filter caused the fuel pump to run inefficiently,
  under-fueling the engine, which overheated and shut down prematurely. Ground tests confirmed the
  root cause. On-screen card: "One part. One failure. It removed the one thing keeping the funding
  gap from becoming visible everyone had to deal with." Visual: technical diagram of the failure
  chain (filter → fuel pump → overheating → engine shutdown), title card "ROOT CAUSE CONFIRMED — FEB
  15, 2023."

### Main Content — The Collapse (7:45–11:30)
- **Segment 7 — The Pause (7:45–8:15):** March 15, 2023, CEO Dan Hart paused operations and furloughed
  nearly all staff for a week while the company searched for emergency funding. Visual: "OPERATIONS
  PAUSED — MARCH 15, 2023" title card.
- **Segment 8 — The Fake Bailout (8:15–10:00):** March 19-23, 2023, a Texas man named Matthew Brown
  submitted an unsolicited $200 million offer to rescue the company, falsely claiming to be an
  experienced venture capitalist with stakes in over 13 space companies. He sent Virgin Orbit a
  fabricated bank-statement screenshot claiming $182 million — his real balance was less than $1.
  Virgin Orbit signed an NDA with him. After the offer leaked, Brown appeared on CNBC on March 23,
  2023, publicly touting it. Virgin Orbit's stock rose about 33% on the news, before the offer was
  exposed as fake. On June 17, 2024, the SEC filed a civil fraud suit against Brown (SEC Litigation
  Release No. 26031). On-screen framing card, held clearly: "This fraud belongs to Matthew Brown. It
  is not Virgin Orbit's fraud, and no one who worked at this company was accused of anything here —
  the company was the target, not a participant." Visual: fabricated "$182M" screenshot graphic
  crossed out, real "$0.87" balance beneath it, stock-price spike-then-crash graph.
- **Segment 9 — The Layoffs and Bankruptcy (10:00–11:30):** March 30, 2023, Virgin Orbit announced it
  would cease operations, laying off 675 employees — about 85% of its workforce. On April 4, 2023, the
  company filed Chapter 11 bankruptcy in Delaware, listing $243 million in assets against $153.5
  million in debt. Sixteen months after going public. Visual: dated timeline graphic, "CHAPTER 11 —
  APRIL 4, 2023 — 16 MONTHS AFTER GOING PUBLIC" title card.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:45): "This channel has covered SPAC-era collapses before — Bird, package 028,
  took about 25 months from IPO to bankruptcy. This one took 16. Subscribe for a new autopsy every
  week."

### Main Content — Sold for Parts (11:30–13:00)
- **Segment 10 — The Auction (11:30–13:00):** May 22-23, 2023, Virgin Orbit's assets were sold at a
  bankruptcy auction for about $36 million total: Rocket Lab paid $16.1 million for the Long Beach
  headquarters, planning to use it for its own Neutron rocket production. Stratolaunch paid $17
  million for the Cosmic Girl 747. Vast's Launcher subsidiary paid $2.7 million for the Mojave test
  site and equipment. Firefly Aerospace separately bought about $3.8 million in additional assets in
  June 2023. Visual: "$3.7 BILLION" valuation card shrinking down to "$36 MILLION — SOLD IN PIECES,"
  three-way asset-split graphic (Rocket Lab / Stratolaunch / Vast).

### The Actual Lesson (13:00–14:15)
- Dan Hart's own words, attributed clearly: in a 2024 interview, the former CEO said Virgin Orbit
  "didn't raise enough money," and that its "closest competitor had raised over three times what we
  had." On-screen attribution card: "Hart's own quoted words — this video does not name the
  competitor he's referring to, because his excerpted quote doesn't name one directly."
- Plain-language lesson: going public doesn't just raise money — it forces disclosure. For almost
  five years, Virgin Orbit's real revenue-to-loss ratio was invisible. The SPAC deal made it public
  record a full year before the rocket failure that ended the company. The failure was the trigger.
  The financial picture, visible in the filings the whole time, was the underlying cause.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2017) → ~$1B private funding (2017-2021) → SPAC close
  (Dec 2021) → first public filings reveal the loss ratio (2022) → launch failure (Jan 2023) →
  furlough (Mar 2023) → fake bailout (Mar 2023) → layoffs (Mar 2023) → Chapter 11 (Apr 2023) → asset
  auction (May 2023).

### Final CTA + Outro (14:15–15:30)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — going public reveals what private funding can keep hidden, and
  a company already running on a bad revenue-to-loss ratio only needs one more failure to end it
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you haven't seen the Bird video, package
  028, that's the direct companion piece to this one's SPAC-to-bankruptcy timeline"
- Engagement prompt: "Did you know about the fake $200 million bailout offer before this video? And
  do you think Virgin Orbit could have survived if the January 2023 launch had succeeded — or was the
  funding gap always going to catch up with it? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 028 (Bird) + most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed $7.4M-revenue-vs-$42.9M-quarterly-loss comparison graphic and the
$3.7B-to-$36M valuation collapse, then works backward into the founding, the SPAC deal, the launch
failure, and the bankruptcy. Measured, graphics-forward, treats the video as a genuine case study in
what public-market disclosure requirements reveal rather than a rocket-failure story.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the January 7, 2022 Nasdaq opening-bell ceremony itself — the $10 share price, the optimism
— before cutting to the April 4, 2023 bankruptcy filing date, then rewinding to tell the full story in
order. Slower pacing, holds the "the numbers were already bad" reveal until Segment 4, then resolves
with the auction and Dan Hart's own quote as the closing beat. Strongest tonal-irony arc of the three
versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "sixteen months, in order" leading into rapid-fire beats: founding, private funding,
SPAC close, first public filings, launch failure, root cause, furlough, fake bailout, layoffs,
bankruptcy, auction — landing on the going-public-reveals-everything lesson as the final, clarifying
beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $100-part-vs-$3.7B-valuation contrast and
the fake-bailout subplot (keep the SEC fraud-allegation language precise and attributed to Matthew
Brown specifically in any Short that touches it).

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the financial-disclosure segment (4:15–5:30) if the SEC-filing material becomes
  a dry numbers recitation without a clear visual payoff
- **Fix:** keep the "$12M revenue / $42.9M loss, same quarter" graphic on screen long enough to land
  as a genuine gut-punch reveal, not a quick flash of numbers
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general 2021-era SPAC-boom context if audience familiarity with
  SPAC mechanics is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the fake-bailout twist more explicitly in the cold open if
  early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 8 (8:15) — most viewers don't expect a fraud
  subplot in a rocket-company story
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "days since IPO" counter visible in a corner
  graphic from Segment 3 onward, landing on "487 DAYS" (~16 months) at the Chapter 11 title card, to
  keep the speed of the collapse visually present throughout
