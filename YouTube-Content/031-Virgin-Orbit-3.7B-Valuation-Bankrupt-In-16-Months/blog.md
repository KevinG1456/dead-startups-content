# Blog Article: Virgin Orbit Went Public at a $3.7 Billion Valuation. It Filed for Bankruptcy 16 Months Later — After One Rocket Failure and a Fake $200 Million Bailout Offer.

*All figures sourced to Wikipedia, CNBC, Fortune, CNN Business, Space.com, SpaceNews, FlightGlobal,
TechCrunch, The Register, BusinessWire/SEC EDGAR earnings filings, and SEC.gov Litigation Release No.
26031 — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. The single most important framing rule in
this article: Virgin Orbit and Virgin Galactic are two separate companies under Richard Branson's
Virgin Group, and are never interchanged here. The fraud described in this article belongs entirely
to Matthew Brown, an unrelated third party — never to Virgin Orbit, Richard Branson, or Dan Hart, none
of whom were charged with anything.*

## Introduction
Most SPAC-collapse stories on this channel take a year or more to fully unwind. Virgin Orbit is the
fastest one yet: from its SPAC merger closing to its Chapter 11 filing, about 16 months passed. This
article covers the full arc — not just the rocket failure that most existing coverage treats as the
cause, but the financial exposure that going public forced a full year earlier, and a fraud subplot,
belonging to someone who never worked at the company, that almost nothing else covering this story
connects to the ending.

## The Founding
Virgin Orbit was founded March 2, 2017, as a spin-off from Virgin Galactic, under Richard Branson's
Virgin Group. **A necessary clarification up front: Virgin Orbit and Virgin Galactic are two separate
companies** — different tickers (VORB vs. SPCE), different business models (small-satellite launch vs.
space tourism), and different histories. This article is entirely about Virgin Orbit.

Virgin Orbit's business was LauncherOne — a small-satellite rocket carried to launch altitude by a
modified Boeing 747 named "Cosmic Girl," then air-dropped and ignited mid-flight, an approach meant to
offer more flexible, rapid-response launch scheduling than traditional ground-launch competitors. Dan
Hart became CEO in 2017 after 34 years at Boeing, most recently overseeing the company's government
satellite programs.

## Nearly Five Years Private
For almost five years, Virgin Orbit operated as a privately held company. Virgin Group and Abu
Dhabi's Mubadala Investment Company — a minority stakeholder — together invested about $1 billion in
the company through August 2021, per contemporaneous press reporting. During this period, the
company's detailed financial performance was not subject to public disclosure requirements.

## The SPAC Deal
On August 23, 2021, Virgin Orbit announced it would go public via a merger with a special purpose
acquisition company, NextGen Acquisition Corp II, at a reported $3.7 billion valuation. The deal
closed December 30, 2021, and Virgin Orbit began trading on Nasdaq under ticker VORB, opening at $10 a
share at a January 7, 2022 bell ceremony.

The deal had been expected to raise $483 million for the company, including a $100 million PIPE round
from investors like Boeing and AE Industrial Partners. After SPAC-trust redemptions — a common
2021-2022 pattern as SPAC-era enthusiasm cooled — it actually raised $228 million, less than half of
what was expected.

## What the Filings Showed
Here is the mechanism at the center of this story, stated plainly: going public didn't just raise
money for Virgin Orbit — it forced disclosure. As a newly public company, Virgin Orbit was now
required to file quarterly financial reports with the SEC. Full-year 2021 revenue: $7.4 million, up
from $3.8 million the year before. By the third quarter of 2022, following a fourth consecutive
successful launch, Virgin Orbit recognized more than $12 million in revenue for the quarter — but
reported a net loss of $42.9 million for that same quarter.

For almost five years as a private company, this revenue-to-loss ratio simply wasn't visible outside
the company and its private backers. Going public made it public record — a full year before the
launch failure that most coverage of Virgin Orbit's collapse treats as the root cause.

## The Track Record
Virgin Orbit attempted six orbital launches between 2020 and 2023: four successes, two failures. The
maiden LauncherOne flight failed in May 2020, when the first-stage engine shut down shortly after
ignition. The first success came January 17, 2021. Two more successful launches followed in 2021, and
two more in 2022 — bringing the total to four successes by the third quarter of 2022.

## Start Me Up — The Launch Failure
On January 9, 2023, Virgin Orbit attempted its first UK orbital launch — "Start Me Up," from
Spaceport Cornwall, its sixth launch overall. The mission failed to reach orbit, destroying all 9
customer satellites from 7 different customers onboard.

On February 15, 2023, Virgin Orbit's own investigation revealed the root cause: a fuel filter within
the fuel feedline had become dislodged from its normal position at the start of the second stage's
first burn. This caused the fuel pump to operate at reduced efficiency, under-fueling the engine,
which overheated; components near the overheated engine then malfunctioned, causing the engine to
shut down prematurely. Ground tests recreating flight conditions confirmed this as the initiating
event. Virgin Orbit stated it had modified the next rocket with a more robust filter.

## The Pause
On March 15, 2023, CEO Dan Hart told nearly all staff to pause operations, placing them on a
week-long furlough while the company searched for emergency funding.

## The Fake Bailout
Between March 19 and 23, 2023, a Texas man named Matthew Brown submitted an unsolicited $200 million
offer to rescue Virgin Orbit, falsely portraying himself as an experienced venture capitalist with
stakes in "over 13 space companies." He sent Virgin Orbit a fabricated bank-statement screenshot
claiming his company held $182 million — his real balance was less than $1. After signing a
non-disclosure agreement with Virgin Orbit, and after his offer leaked to the media, Brown appeared on
CNBC on March 23, 2023, publicly touting the offer. Virgin Orbit's stock rose approximately 33% on the
news before the offer was exposed as fraudulent.

On June 17, 2024, the SEC filed a civil fraud suit against Brown and his company, Matthew Brown
Companies, LLC (SEC Litigation Release No. 26031). **This fraud belongs to Matthew Brown alone.**
Virgin Orbit was the target of his scheme, not a participant in it — no one who worked at the company
was accused of anything in connection with this episode.

## The Layoffs and Bankruptcy
On March 30, 2023, Virgin Orbit announced it would cease operations, laying off 675 employees —
roughly 85% of its workforce — "to reduce expenses in light of the Company's inability to secure
meaningful funding." On April 4, 2023, Virgin Orbit filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy protection in the
U.S. Bankruptcy Court for the District of Delaware, listing $243 million in assets against $153.5
million in total debt — 16 months after the company had gone public.

## Sold for Parts
On May 22-23, 2023, Virgin Orbit's assets were sold at a bankruptcy auction for approximately $36
million total, split among three buyers: Rocket Lab paid $16.1 million for Virgin Orbit's Long Beach,
California headquarters — about 140,000 square feet — which it said it would use to advance production
of its own Neutron rocket. Stratolaunch paid $17 million for the Cosmic Girl 747 carrier aircraft.
Vast's Launcher subsidiary paid $2.7 million for the Mojave, California test site and related
equipment. Firefly Aerospace separately acquired about $3.8 million in additional assets in June 2023.
A company once valued at $3.7 billion ended, in pieces, for about $36 million.

## The Actual Lesson
In a January/February 2024 interview, former CEO Dan Hart offered his own explanation, in his own
words: Virgin Orbit "didn't raise enough money," and its "closest competitor had raised over three
times what we had." That's a useful, attributed data point — but the fuller picture, visible in the
company's own public filings, is that the underlying revenue-to-loss ratio had already been exposed
to public scrutiny for a full year before the January 2023 launch failure that ended the company.

Going public doesn't just raise capital. It forces disclosure. A company that can operate for years on
private funding without showing its real numbers to anyone outside a small circle of backers loses
that option the moment it lists — and if the underlying numbers were already bad, the failure that
finally ends the company isn't really the cause. It's the trigger for something the filings had
already made visible.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **Going public changes what a company is allowed to keep hidden** — Virgin Orbit's real
  revenue-to-loss ratio was invisible for nearly five years as a private company; it became public
  record within a year of the SPAC deal closing
- **A single operational failure can be the trigger without being the underlying cause** — Virgin
  Orbit's January 2023 launch failure ended the company, but the financial picture that made one
  failure fatal was already on the public record
- **SPAC deals often raise far less than the headline number implies** — Virgin Orbit's deal was
  expected to raise $483 million and actually raised $228 million, a gap driven by investor
  redemptions common across the 2021-2022 SPAC wave
- **A distressed company is a target for opportunistic fraud, not just a failure story on its own** —
  Matthew Brown's fabricated rescue offer is a reminder that a company's public vulnerability can
  attract fraud from people who have nothing to do with the company itself
- **Liquidation value and peak valuation are almost never the same number** — Virgin Orbit's assets
  sold for about $36 million, roughly 1% of its reported $3.7 billion SPAC-era valuation

## The Lesson
Virgin Orbit is a startup-failure story with two distinct threads: a real, structural mechanism (going
public exposing what private funding had hidden) and a real, bizarre subplot (an unrelated party's
fabricated rescue offer) that most existing coverage tells as separate stories. Put together, they
show how a company's public listing can start the clock on its own collapse well before any single
dramatic failure — and how a dying company can attract fraud from people who were never part of it in
the first place.
