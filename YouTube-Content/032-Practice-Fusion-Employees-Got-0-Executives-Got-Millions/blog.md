# Blog Article: Practice Fusion Sold for $100 Million. Employees Got $0. Executives Got Millions.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, CNBC, Fortune, Forbes, Wikipedia, DOJ/Oversight.gov settlement and
litigation documents, NPR, Fierce Healthcare, and VTDigger — see `sources-log.txt` for the full
citation map. The single most important framing rule in this article: this is NOT a story about "the
founders" collectively getting $0. Ordinary employees and common stockholders got $0; a management
carve-out paid CEO Tom Langan $7 million and co-founder/executive Jonathan Malek $2.25 million.
Founder Ryan Howard's specific outcome from the 2018 sale is not confirmed in any source found for
this article — it is never stated as $0.*

## Introduction
This channel's title library includes a batch idea built around a familiar startup-finance pattern:
"the $100 million exit where founders got $0." Researching a real anchor for that pattern led to
Practice Fusion — and to a version of the story that's more precise, and more useful, than the
original premise. The people who actually got nothing weren't "the founders" as a group. They were
the ordinary employees and common stockholders who didn't have a pre-negotiated carve-out. One of the
company's own co-founders, who stayed on as an executive, was paid $2.25 million from the same deal.
This article tells the real, sourced version.

## The Founding
Practice Fusion was founded in San Francisco in 2005 by Ryan Howard, Alan Wong, Jonathan Malek, and
Matthew Douglass. The company built free, cloud-based electronic health records software aimed at
small and solo medical practices — a niche the bigger EHR vendors largely ignored. Over its first
decade, Practice Fusion raised roughly $157 million, including a $70 million Series D round led by
Kleiner Perkins Caufield & Byers in 2013.

## The Near-IPO
By 2015, Practice Fusion looked like it might be headed for a genuine public-market outcome. The
company hired JPMorgan Chase to explore an IPO, and JPMorgan's own internal estimate put a possible
2016 listing at a valuation near $1.5 billion, based on projected 2018 revenue of $181 million.

## The Ouster
On August 14, 2015, that trajectory changed. Practice Fusion's board removed founder Ryan Howard as
CEO — a decision announced quietly, via a website statement late on a Friday afternoon rather than a
press release. The board's stated reasoning: the company needed to significantly strengthen its
commercialization efforts, an area where Howard was seen as weaker than on product. Tom Langan, then
the company's chief commercial officer, became interim CEO and was confirmed permanently that
November. Around this same period, Practice Fusion cut roughly a quarter of its workforce in pursuit
of cash-flow positivity.

**A necessary note here, stated as plainly as the sourcing allows: this article does not know, and no
source found in this research addresses, what Ryan Howard was ultimately paid — or not paid — when
Practice Fusion sold three years later.** After leaving, Howard went on to found a new company, iBeat.

## The Contract
In March 2016 — after Howard's departure, under Tom Langan's leadership — Practice Fusion signed a
contract with Purdue Pharma, the maker of OxyContin. Conversations about the arrangement had actually
begun back in 2013, while Howard was still CEO, but the contract itself wasn't signed until after he
was gone. Purdue paid Practice Fusion nearly $1 million between April and December 2016 in exchange
for a clinical-decision-support alert embedded in Practice Fusion's EHR software — an alert, designed
in part by Purdue's own marketing department, that prompted physicians to prescribe more
extended-release opioid medications than were medically necessary, while being presented to doctors as
neutral clinical guidance. That alert fired an estimated 230 million times between 2016 and 2019, and
is estimated to have driven tens of thousands of additional opioid prescriptions.

Ryan Howard has since told FierceHealthcare directly that he was "not in charge of the company when
the opioid contract was signed" — his own on-record statement, consistent with (though not proven
solely by) the March 2016 signing date falling after his August 2015 departure. This article presents
that as his own account, not as independently adjudicated fact about the earlier 2013 conversations.

## The Investigation and the Price Collapse
In March 2017, Practice Fusion received a Criminal Investigative Demand from the U.S. Attorney's
Office for the District of Vermont — the DOJ's investigation into the Purdue arrangement had begun.

Two months later, in May 2017, Allscripts submitted a non-binding letter of intent to acquire Practice
Fusion for between $225 million and $250 million. In June 2017, Allscripts pulled the offer — partly,
reporting suggests, after an unrelated EHR vendor, eClinicalWorks, settled separately with federal
prosecutors for $155 million over a different set of allegations, raising sector-wide concern about
EHR-vendor legal exposure.

On January 8, 2018, Allscripts returned — this time with a cash offer of $100 million, which closed.
Allscripts president Rick Poulton later confirmed to shareholders that the company knew about the DOJ
investigation into Practice Fusion before it acquired the business. The deal that closed was less than
half the size of the offer on the table seven months earlier, and represented roughly two-thirds of
Practice Fusion's total lifetime funding.

## The Waterfall
Here is the mechanism at the center of this story, in plain terms. Practice Fusion had raised roughly
$157 million over its life, most of it as preferred stock carrying liquidation preferences —
contractual terms that guarantee those investors get repaid first, ahead of anyone holding ordinary
common stock, when a company is sold. A $100 million sale, set against $157 million in stacked
preferences, means the preferred stack alone could absorb the entire proceeds before the waterfall
ever reaches common stock.

## The Carve-Out
But the actual outcome wasn't purely mechanical. CNBC's reporting, published January 23, 2018 — just
over two weeks after the sale closed — found that Practice Fusion had maintained a separate,
pre-arranged management bonus pool, paid directly to select executives out of the acquisition
proceeds. CEO Tom Langan received $7 million. Co-founder and executive Jonathan Malek received $2.25
million. Other, unnamed executives received millions more.

Practice Fusion's own statement to CNBC defended the arrangement: "In the Bay Area, startup companies
pursue financings with terms that vary in their complexity, such as preferred stock liquidation
preferences," and that "bonus pools for key employees (which include founders and current executives)
align their incentives with company stakeholders to achieve value upon an exit."

**This is the detail that makes "the founders got $0" inaccurate as a headline for this story.**
Jonathan Malek co-founded Practice Fusion. He was also one of the executives paid from the carve-out.
The real dividing line in this story isn't "founders" versus "everyone else" — it's "people with a
pre-negotiated protection" versus "people without one."

## The Employees
Meanwhile, ordinary employees and common stockholders received $0 from the sale. CNBC spoke to three
named former employees who had each lost between $40,000 and over $100,000 — money they had already
spent exercising their stock options, and paying the resulting tax bill, in earlier years, only to
receive nothing when the company finally sold.

## The DOJ Settlement
On January 27, 2020 — two years after the sale had closed — the Department of Justice announced a
$145 million settlement with Practice Fusion, by then an Allscripts subsidiary: a $25.4 million
criminal fine, forfeiture of nearly $1 million in illegal proceeds, and a $118.6 million civil
settlement. Practice Fusion admitted criminal responsibility for conspiring with Purdue Pharma to
violate the federal Anti-Kickback Statute — the first-ever criminal case filed against an electronic
health records vendor. Allscripts later disclosed that the underlying conduct predated its own
acquisition of the company.

## The Obstruction Case
Separately, Steven Mack — Practice Fusion's Director of National Accounts during 2015-2016, and the
company's principal contact with Purdue Pharma on this arrangement — was not charged with the
underlying kickback conspiracy in the material found for this article. He was charged with
obstruction: in late 2019, after learning the DOJ was investigating, he deleted files relevant to the
investigation from his company-issued laptop. He pleaded guilty in March 2021 and was sentenced in May
2024 to one year of probation, a $20,000 fine, and community service. Mack was a sales director, not a
founder and not part of the executive carve-out described above — a third, separate category in this
story.

## The Actual Lesson
Three people, three outcomes, one $100 million sale. Ryan Howard, the founder who was removed before
the company's defining decisions were made, has an outcome from the eventual sale that no available
reporting addresses. Tom Langan and Jonathan Malek, executives with a pre-arranged carve-out, were
paid millions. Ordinary employees and common stockholders, without that protection, got nothing.

The lesson isn't really about liquidation preferences in the abstract — it's about who has the
leverage, and the paperwork, to protect themselves before a sale happens, and who doesn't. A term
sheet's fine print decides that answer years before anyone signs an acquisition agreement.

## What Founders and Employees Should Take From This
- **A liquidation preference stack determines the outcome long before an exit happens** — by the time
  Practice Fusion sold, the math (roughly $157M raised against a $100M sale) had already made a common-
  stock wipeout close to mechanical
- **A management carve-out is a separate negotiation from the underlying cap table** — Tom Langan and
  Jonathan Malek's payouts came from a pre-arranged bonus pool, not from surviving the preference
  waterfall on the same terms as ordinary common stockholders
- **"Founder" and "executive" are not always the same category at exit** — Jonathan Malek was both,
  and it mattered which one determined his payout
- **Exercising stock options early doesn't protect you from a wipeout** — the three named former
  employees in this story had already paid real money and real taxes on options that ultimately
  produced $0
- **A collapsing deal price can have a specific, documentable cause** — Practice Fusion's price didn't
  just erode with market sentiment; a federal investigation is the direct reason it fell from
  $225-250M to $100M

## The Lesson
Practice Fusion is a case study in what a term sheet's fine print actually does when a company sells
for real money but not enough money — and in how easily a headline like "founders got $0" can obscure
who actually walked away with something and who didn't. The precise version is less tidy than the
original premise, and more useful: protection at exit comes from what you negotiated, not from what
you built.
