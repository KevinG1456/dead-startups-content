## LinkedIn
"A real, fully-sourced case study in what a liquidation preference stack actually does — and doesn't
do — to the people who build a company:

Practice Fusion, a free cloud EHR company, raised roughly $157 million and was pitched for a possible
$1.5 billion IPO in 2015. That year, its board removed founder Ryan Howard as CEO. In 2016, under new
leadership, the company signed a deal accepting nearly $1 million from Purdue Pharma to build an
opioid-prescribing alert into its software — triggering a federal investigation in 2017 that
collapsed an initial $225-250 million acquisition offer down to $100 million by the time the deal
closed in January 2018.

CNBC's reporting weeks later found that ordinary employees and common stockholders received $0 from
the sale, due to stacked liquidation preferences. But a separate, pre-arranged management carve-out
paid CEO Tom Langan $7 million and co-founder/executive Jonathan Malek $2.25 million. Three named
former employees told CNBC they'd each lost $40,000 to over $100,000 — money already spent exercising
options years earlier — on stock that ended up worth nothing.

Two years later, Practice Fusion admitted criminal responsibility and paid a $145 million DOJ
settlement — the first-ever criminal case against an EHR vendor.

Full breakdown, precise about exactly who was paid what: [video link]
