WHY THIS FILE EXISTS
Practice Fusion is the anchor for this channel's "Money Mechanics" batch title #3: "Liquidation
preferences: the $100M exit where founders got $0." The $100M acquisition price is an exact,
precisely-sourced match to the title. But this research pass found the literal "founders got $0"
premise does NOT hold up: CNBC's own January 2018 reporting shows ordinary employees and common
stockholders got $0, while a pre-arranged management carve-out paid CEO Tom Langan $7 million and
co-founder/executive Jonathan Malek $2.25 million. This package's framing was adjusted accordingly —
see "CRITICAL FRAMING CORRECTION" below. This is the same kind of honest pivot this channel made on
001 (Sprig's real ARR), 003 (platform-risk categories), and others: the title's premise doesn't
survive contact with the sourcing, so the framing changes rather than the facts being bent to fit.

CRITICAL FRAMING CORRECTION — READ BEFORE WRITING ANY FILE IN THIS PACKAGE
The batch title says "founders got $0." What is actually sourced: ORDINARY EMPLOYEES AND COMMON
STOCKHOLDERS got $0, while MANAGEMENT (including one co-founder who stayed on as an executive) had a
pre-arranged carve-out that paid them millions. Founder Ryan Howard (ousted as CEO in Aug 2015, gone
from the company by the 2018 sale) is NOT confirmed in this research pass to have received $0 — no
source found states his specific payout either way. Do not state or imply he got $0; state plainly
that this research did not find his individual outcome reported. Co-founder Jonathan Malek, who
remained an executive through the sale, is confirmed to have received $2.25 million — the opposite of
"$0." The accurate, sourced framing for this package is: "employees and common stockholders got $0;
executives (including a co-founder who stayed on as one) got millions via a carve-out" — sharper and
more cynical than the original title, and still built on the same liquidation-preference mechanism.

FOUNDING — [VERIFY] founding year discrepancy
- Practice Fusion, a San Francisco-based cloud EHR company for small/solo medical practices, was
  founded by Ryan Howard, Alan Wong, Jonathan Malek, and Matthew Douglass. *** [VERIFY]: Wikipedia
  states 2005; at least one other source found in this pass says 2006 (and TechCrunch's 2018 piece
  calls it "12-year-old," which would point to 2005-2006 depending on exact founding vs. launch

- Business model: free cloud-based EHR software for independent physician practices, monetized via a
  data/advertising-adjacent model rather than subscription fees from doctors.
Source: Wikipedia ("Practice Fusion"); TechCrunch (Jan 8, 2018).

FUNDING HISTORY — [VERIFY] total raised varies by aggregator, use $157M as primary

- September 2013: $70 million Series D led by Kleiner Perkins Caufield & Byers.
- December 2013: additional $15 million Series D extension from Qualcomm Ventures.
- Total lifetime funding: reported as "approximately $157 million" by TechCrunch (the most-

Source: TechCrunch (Jan 8, 2018, "Practice Fusion, once poised for a blockbuster IPO, sells at a loss

THE NEAR-IPO — precisely sourced
- In 2015, Practice Fusion hired JPMorgan Chase to explore a potential IPO. JPMorgan's internal
  estimate: if the company went public in 2016, it could be valued at approximately $1.5 billion,
  based on projected 2018 revenue of $181 million.
Source: TechCrunch (Jan 8, 2018); Fortune (Aug 19, 2015).

THE OUSTER — precisely dated, do NOT describe as a "second" firing (unconfirmed detail, dropped)
- August 14, 2015: founder and CEO Ryan Howard was removed from the CEO role by Practice Fusion's
  board — a board decision, announced quietly via a website statement/blog post late on a Friday
  afternoon, not a press release. Howard retained the board chairman title at the time of the
  announcement. The board's stated reason: the company "needed to significantly enhance its
  commercialization efforts" — Howard was viewed as stronger on product than sales/commercialization.
- Tom Langan, previously Practice Fusion's chief commercial officer, was named interim CEO on
  Howard's departure, and was confirmed as permanent CEO in November 2015.
- Around this period, the company laid off approximately 25% of its workforce, aiming for cash-flow
  positivity. Peak headcount exceeded 400 employees; by the time of the 2018 sale, headcount was down
  to approximately 200.
- After his departure, Ryan Howard went on to found a new startup, iBeat (a health-tech/wearable
  company), by 2016.
Source: Fortune (Aug 19, 2015, "Behind the Practice Fusion CEO switch"); TechCrunch (Jan 8, 2018);
Forbes (May 24, 2016, "Practice Fusion Founder Ryan Howard Makes A Comeback With iBeat").

THE OPIOID KICKBACK SCHEME — precisely sourced, context for the price collapse below
- Conversations between Practice Fusion and Purdue Pharma L.P. (the maker of OxyContin, referred to
  as "Pharma Co. X" in DOJ documents before being publicly identified as Purdue) about a paid
  clinical-decision-support (CDS) alert began in 2013 — while Ryan Howard was still CEO. The actual
  contract for the pain-related CDS alert was signed in March 2016 — after Howard's August 2015
  departure, under CEO Tom Langan.
- Ryan Howard told FierceHealthcare directly, in his own words, that he was "not in charge of the
  company when the opioid contract was signed." *** Present this as Howard's own on-record statement
  to a reporter, not as this channel's independently verified fact — though it is consistent with the
  March 2016 contract-signing date, which is after his confirmed August 2015 departure. Do not extend
  this to claim Howard had no knowledge of or role in the 2013 conversations that preceded the
  contract, since this research pass found no direct statement addressing that period specifically. ***
- Purdue Pharma paid Practice Fusion nearly $1 million (payments made April-December 2016) in
  exchange for Practice Fusion designing and embedding a clinical alert in its EHR software that
  prompted physicians to prescribe more extended-release opioid medications (OxyContin, Butrans,
  Hysingla) than were medically necessary — Purdue's own marketing department helped design the
  alert, which was presented to physicians as objective clinical guidance.
- The alert was triggered in clinical practices an estimated 230 million times between 2016 and 2019,
  and is estimated to have resulted in tens of thousands of additional extended-release opioid
  prescriptions.
- Separately, Practice Fusion struck 14 similar paid-alert deals with other pharmaceutical companies
  between November 2013 and August 2017 — the Purdue deal was the one DOJ specifically targeted in
  its criminal case.
Source: NPR (Feb 1, 2020); Fierce Pharma; DOJ/Oversight.gov (Jan 27, 2020, settlement announcement);
CNBC (Jan 28, 2020, "Practice Fusion took kickbacks to push opioids to doctors, DOJ finds"); Journal
of Bioethical Inquiry (2022 academic case analysis).

WHY THE DEAL PRICE COLLAPSED — precisely sourced, direct causal link to the DOJ investigation
- March 2017: Practice Fusion received a Criminal Investigative Demand (CID) — a federal grand jury
  subpoena-equivalent — from the U.S. Attorney's Office for the District of Vermont, opening the DOJ's
  investigation into the Purdue Pharma arrangement.
- May 2017: Allscripts submitted a non-binding letter of intent to acquire Practice Fusion for
  between $225 million and $250 million — this was AFTER the DOJ investigation had already begun.
- June 2017: Allscripts pulled its offer, in part after another EHR vendor, eClinicalWorks, settled
  separately with federal prosecutors for $155 million over unrelated allegations of falsifying EHR
  certification requirements — an event that raised sector-wide concern about EHR-vendor legal
  exposure.
- January 8, 2018: Allscripts acquired Practice Fusion for $100 million in cash (subject to working-
  capital and net-debt adjustments) — roughly $125-150 million less than the original May 2017 offer,
  and about two-thirds of the company's total lifetime funding raised.
- Allscripts president Rick Poulton later told shareholders (per company disclosures): "As you know
  from our previous SEC filings, DOJ began investigations into certain practices of Practice Fusion
  before we acquired the business" — confirming Allscripts knew of the DOJ investigation before
  closing the reduced-price deal.
Source: Fierce Healthcare ("Allscripts offered to buy Practice Fusion for $250M. A DOJ investigation
changed everything"); TechCrunch (Jan 8, 2018); DOJ settlement disclosures; Allscripts shareholder
communications (per Fierce Healthcare reporting).

WHO GOT WHAT — the core liquidation-preference story, precisely sourced
- CNBC (Jan 23, 2018, "Employees at Practice Fusion expected IPO riches, but got nothing as execs
  pocketed millions"): ordinary employees and common stockholders received $0 from the $100 million
  sale. Because of stacked liquidation preferences across multiple funding rounds, preferred
  shareholders were paid first in the acquisition waterfall, and by the time the payout reached common
  stock, nothing remained.
- CNBC interviewed three named former employees who each lost between $40,000 and over $100,000 —
  money they had already spent exercising stock options (and paying the associated tax bill) in
  earlier years, only to receive nothing when the company sold.
- Despite the common-stock wipeout, a pre-arranged management carve-out/bonus pool paid select
  executives directly from the deal: CEO Tom Langan received $7 million; co-founder and executive
  Jonathan Malek received $2.25 million; other unnamed executives received "millions more" per CNBC's
  reporting.
- Practice Fusion's own statement to CNBC in response: "In the Bay Area, startup companies pursue
  financings with terms that vary in their complexity, such as preferred stock liquidation
  preferences," and that "bonus pools for key employees (which include founders and current
  executives) align their incentives with company stakeholders to achieve value upon an exit." ***
  Present this as the company's own defense/framing, attributed directly, not as this channel's
  characterization. ***
- *** [VERIFY]/NOT FOUND: this research pass found NO reporting on what founder Ryan Howard
  specifically received (or didn't receive) from the 2018 sale — he had left the company in 2015 and
  was not part of the 2018 executive carve-out. Do not state he received $0; state plainly that his
  individual outcome was not found in available reporting. ***
Source: CNBC (Jan 23, 2018); Becker's Hospital Review (analysis/summary of the CNBC reporting,
Jan 2018).

THE DOJ SETTLEMENT — precisely dated, two years after the sale
- January 27-28, 2020: the DOJ announced a $145 million settlement with Practice Fusion (by then an
  Allscripts subsidiary), resolving parallel criminal and civil investigations. Breakdown: a $25.4
  million criminal fine, forfeiture of nearly $1 million in illegal proceeds, and a $118.6 million
  civil settlement (of which $113.4 million went to the federal government and up to $5.2 million to
  participating states).
- Practice Fusion admitted criminal responsibility for conspiring with Purdue Pharma to violate the
  federal Anti-Kickback Statute. This was the first-ever criminal action brought against an EHR
  vendor.
- Allscripts disclosed in August 2020 that the underlying conduct predated its acquisition of Practice
  Fusion (i.e., occurred under prior ownership/leadership, before the Jan 2018 sale closed).
Source: DOJ/Oversight.gov (Jan 27, 2020); Healthcare Dive; Fierce Healthcare; NPR (Feb 1, 2020);
Medscape.

THE OBSTRUCTION CASE — one named individual, precisely dated, NOT the CEO or a founder
- Steven Mack, Practice Fusion's Director of National Accounts during 2015-2016 and the company's
  principal point of contact with Purdue Pharma on this matter, was NOT charged with the underlying
  kickback conspiracy itself in the material found in this research pass. He was charged separately
  with obstruction: in late 2019, after learning the DOJ was investigating, he intentionally deleted
  files relevant to the investigation from his company-issued laptop.
- March 8, 2021: Mack pleaded guilty to one count of attempting to obstruct a federal investigation.
- May 13, 2024: Mack was sentenced to one year of probation, a $20,000 fine, and community service.
*** Framing note: Mack was a sales director, not a founder, CEO, or member of the executive carve-out
group described above — keep him categorically distinct from both Ryan Howard and Tom Langan/Jonathan
Malek in every file. ***
Source: DOJ/Oversight.gov (Mar 8, 2021, guilty plea announcement); VTDigger (Mar 8, 2021; May 16,
2024); DOJ/Oversight.gov (May 13, 2024, sentencing announcement); Vermont Business Magazine.
