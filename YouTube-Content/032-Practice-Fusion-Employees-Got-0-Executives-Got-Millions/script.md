# Script Package

Anchored on Practice Fusion — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR
THIS SCRIPT: never state or imply that "the founders" collectively got $0 from the sale. What's
sourced: ordinary employees and common stockholders got $0; a management carve-out paid CEO Tom
Langan $7 million and co-founder/executive Jonathan Malek $2.25 million. Founder Ryan Howard's
specific outcome from the 2018 sale is NOT confirmed in available reporting — state plainly that his
individual result wasn't found, never that he got $0. Keep three groups distinct throughout: Howard
(ousted founder, outcome unconfirmed), Langan/Malek (executives who received the carve-out), and
Steven Mack (sales director, later convicted of obstruction — categorically separate from the other
two groups, not part of the carve-out story). On the opioid material: attribute Howard's "not in
charge when the contract was signed" defense as his own on-record statement to FierceHealthcare,
consistent with but not fully proven by the March 2016 contract date. Do not include opioid/pill
imagery — keep that material text- and document-led.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 14–17 minutes (founding, near-IPO, the ouster, the opioid deal, the price
collapse, the payout split, and the DOJ settlement all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Practice Fusion sold for $100 million. The employees who built the product got zero dollars.
  The CEO got seven million. Same company. Same sale. Here's exactly how that happens."
- Visual: large glowing "$7M" numeral next to a small flat "$0," hard cut to "SAME COMPANY. SAME
  SALE." text card
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a hypothetical about
  liquidation preferences. This actually happened."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Practice Fusion — a free, cloud-based electronic health records company for small
  medical practices, founded in San Francisco by Ryan Howard, Alan Wong, Jonathan Malek, and Matthew
  Douglass. It raised roughly $157 million. In 2015, it was exploring an IPO that could have valued it
  near $1.5 billion. In 2018, it sold to Allscripts for $100 million in cash."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand exactly how a $100 million sale can pay one
  executive $7 million and pay the people who actually built the product nothing at all — and you'll
  understand the real reason the price collapsed from $250 million to $100 million in the first
  place, which involves a federal investigation into an opioid company most people have never
  connected to this story."

### Introduction (1:00–1:45)
- Method statement: reconstructed from TechCrunch, CNBC, Fortune, Forbes, Wikipedia, DOJ/Oversight.gov
  settlement and litigation documents, NPR, Fierce Healthcare, and VTDigger — sourced on screen
  throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "Three different people matter in this story, and they
  are not the same people, and they did not have the same outcome. The founder who was fired before
  the biggest decisions were made. The executives who negotiated a carve-out that paid them millions.
  And a sales director who later pleaded guilty to a completely separate crime. Keep those three
  groups straight, because this video does."

### Main Content — The Rise and the Ouster (1:45–4:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:45–2:15):** Practice Fusion founded 2005 in San Francisco by Ryan
  Howard, Alan Wong, Jonathan Malek, and Matthew Douglass — free cloud EHR software for small,
  independent medical practices. Visual: generic startup-office establishing shot, no real branding.
- **Segment 2 — The Near-IPO (2:15–2:45):** by 2015, Practice Fusion had raised roughly $157 million
  and hired JPMorgan to explore an IPO. JPMorgan's own estimate: a 2016 public listing could value the
  company near $1.5 billion. Visual: "$1.5 BILLION" projected-valuation card.
- **Segment 3 — The Ouster (2:45–4:00):** August 14, 2015, Practice Fusion's board removed founder
  Ryan Howard as CEO — a quiet Friday-afternoon announcement, not a press release. The board's stated
  reason: the company needed stronger commercialization, not just product development. Tom Langan,
  the chief commercial officer, became interim CEO, confirmed permanent that November. Around this
  period, the company cut about 25% of its workforce chasing cash-flow positivity. On-screen framing
  card: "Howard's outcome from the eventual 2018 sale is not confirmed in any reporting found for this
  video. This video does not claim he received nothing — only that no source states what he received."
  Visual: "FOUNDER REMOVED — AUGUST 14, 2015" title card.
- **Segment 4 — What Howard Did Next (4:00–4:30):** after his departure, Howard founded a new
  company, iBeat. Visual: brief transition graphic, no real branding.

### Main Content — The Opioid Deal and the Price Collapse (4:30–8:30)
- **Segment 5 — The Contract (4:30–6:00):** conversations between Practice Fusion and Purdue Pharma —
  the maker of OxyContin — about a paid software alert began in 2013, while Howard was still CEO. The
  actual contract was signed in March 2016, after Howard's departure, under CEO Tom Langan. Purdue
  paid Practice Fusion nearly $1 million between April and December 2016 to build a clinical-decision
  alert into its software that pushed doctors toward prescribing more extended-release opioids — an
  alert Purdue's own marketing team helped design, presented to physicians as neutral medical
  guidance. That alert fired an estimated 230 million times between 2016 and 2019, and is estimated to
  have led to tens of thousands of additional opioid prescriptions. On-screen attributed quote: Ryan
  Howard told FierceHealthcare he was "not in charge of the company when the opioid contract was
  signed" — his own words, consistent with the March 2016 signing date being after his August 2015
  departure. Visual: dated timeline graphic (2013 conversations → Aug 2015 Howard departs → Mar 2016
  contract signed), Howard's quote card held clearly on screen.
- **Segment 6 — The Investigation Opens (6:00–6:45):** March 2017, federal prosecutors in Vermont sent
  Practice Fusion a Criminal Investigative Demand, opening the DOJ's investigation. Visual: "DOJ
  INVESTIGATION OPENS — MARCH 2017" title card.
- **Segment 7 — The Price Collapse (6:45–8:30):** May 2017, Allscripts offered $225-250 million for
  Practice Fusion — after the investigation had already begun. June 2017, Allscripts pulled the offer,
  partly spooked by an unrelated EHR-industry fraud settlement (eClinicalWorks, $155 million). January
  8, 2018, Allscripts returned with a $100 million cash offer, which closed. Allscripts' own president
  later told shareholders the company knew about the DOJ investigation before it acquired the
  business. On-screen framing card: "The DOJ investigation is the direct, documented reason this deal
  collapsed by more than half." Visual: "$250M" numeral shrinking to "$100M," dated timeline.

### Main Content — Who Got Paid (8:30–11:30)
- **Segment 8 — The Waterfall (8:30–9:30):** here's the mechanism. Practice Fusion had raised
  roughly $157 million through multiple funding rounds, most of it as preferred stock with
  liquidation preferences — contractual terms guaranteeing those investors get paid back first, before
  anyone holding ordinary common stock sees a dollar. A $100 million sale, against $157 million in
  stacked preferences, means the preferred stack alone could absorb the entire sale price before
  common stock is ever reached. Visual: waterfall/cascade graphic, preferred tiers filling first, an
  empty pool at the bottom labeled "COMMON STOCK."
- **Segment 9 — The Carve-Out (9:30–10:45):** but the payout wasn't purely mechanical. CNBC's
  reporting found that Practice Fusion had a separate, pre-arranged management bonus pool — paid
  directly to select executives from the deal. CEO Tom Langan received $7 million. Co-founder and
  executive Jonathan Malek received $2.25 million. Other unnamed executives received millions more.
  Practice Fusion's own statement to CNBC defended this: "bonus pools for key employees... align their
  incentives with company stakeholders to achieve value upon an exit." On-screen framing card: "Malek
  co-founded this company. He is also one of the executives who was paid from the carve-out — this is
  why this video never says 'the founders got $0.'" Visual: "$7M" and "$2.25M" numeral cards, company
  statement quote card.
- **Segment 10 — The Employees (10:45–11:30):** meanwhile, ordinary employees and common stockholders
  received $0. CNBC spoke to three named former employees who had each lost between $40,000 and over
  $100,000 — money they'd already spent exercising stock options, and paying the resulting tax bill,
  in earlier years, on stock that ultimately paid them nothing. Visual: "$0" flat numeral, three
  generic employee-silhouette icons.
- Mid-video CTA (~11:15): "If you hold or are negotiating startup equity, this is exactly the kind of
  term-sheet mechanic worth understanding before you sign anything. Subscribe for a new autopsy every
  week."

### Main Content — The DOJ Settlement (11:30–13:30)
- **Segment 11 — The Settlement (11:30–12:30):** January 27, 2020 — two years after the sale closed —
  the DOJ announced a $145 million settlement with Practice Fusion, by then an Allscripts subsidiary.
  Practice Fusion admitted criminal responsibility for conspiring with Purdue Pharma to violate the
  federal Anti-Kickback Statute — the first-ever criminal case filed against an electronic health
  records company. Visual: "$145 MILLION SETTLEMENT — JANUARY 2020" title card.
- **Segment 12 — The Obstruction Case (12:30–13:30):** separately, Steven Mack, Practice Fusion's
  former Director of National Accounts and the company's point of contact with Purdue on this deal,
  wasn't charged with the underlying kickback scheme — he was charged with obstruction, after
  deleting relevant files from his work laptop in late 2019 once he learned about the investigation.
  He pleaded guilty in March 2021 and was sentenced in May 2024 to one year of probation and a
  $20,000 fine. On-screen framing card: "Mack was a sales director — not a founder, not part of the
  executive carve-out described earlier. Keep him separate from both groups." Visual: "GUILTY PLEA —
  2021 / SENTENCED — 2024" title card.

### The Actual Lesson (13:30–14:45)
- Plain-language comparison: a company can raise a lot of money, build a real product, and still exit
  in a way where the people who negotiated protection for themselves get paid, and the people who
  didn't get nothing — regardless of how the company's underlying story ends.
- The core irony, stated directly: the same sale that paid one executive $7 million paid the ordinary
  employees who built the product $0, from the exact same $100 million.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2005) → near-IPO talk (2015) → founder removed (Aug
  2015) → opioid contract signed (Mar 2016) → DOJ investigation opens (Mar 2017) → offer collapses
  ($250M → $100M, 2017-2018) → sale closes (Jan 2018) → payout split reported (Jan 2018) → DOJ
  settlement ($145M, Jan 2020) → obstruction plea (2021) → sentencing (2024).

### Final CTA + Outro (14:45–16:00)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a term sheet's fine print decides who gets paid at an exit long
  before the exit itself happens, and it rarely favors the people without a lawyer in the room
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want the other cap-table-mechanics
  story on this channel, check out package 018 on down rounds"
- Engagement prompt: "If you've ever held startup equity, did you actually understand your liquidation
  preference stack before you needed to? And do you think Ryan Howard deserved to know what happened
  to his own stake? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 018 (Down Rounds) + most recent autopsy + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed cap-table waterfall graphic and the $7M-vs-$0 payout comparison, then
works backward into the founding, the ouster, the opioid deal, and the price collapse. Measured,
graphics-forward, treats the video as a genuine case study in term-sheet mechanics rather than a
company-history piece.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the 2015 IPO talk itself — the $1.5 billion projected valuation, the optimism — before
cutting hard to the "$100 million, employees got $0" reveal, then rewinding to tell the full story in
order. Slower pacing, holds the carve-out reveal until Segment 9, resolving with the DOJ settlement
and obstruction case as the closing beats. Strongest tonal-irony arc of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "how a $100 million sale becomes $0 for some people," leading into rapid-fire beats:
founding, near-IPO, ouster, opioid contract, investigation, price collapse, carve-out, employee
wipeout, DOJ settlement, obstruction case — landing on the waterfall-mechanism lesson as the final,
clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially the $7M-vs-$0 contrast and the
$250M-to-$100M collapse (keep the DOJ/kickback material precise and text-led in any Short that touches
it — no opioid/pill imagery).

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the cap-table waterfall segment (8:30–9:30) if it becomes a dry mechanics
  lecture without a clear visual payoff
- **Fix:** keep the "preferred tiers fill, common stock stays empty" graphic on screen long enough to
  land as a genuine reveal, paired immediately with the concrete $7M/$2.25M/$0 numbers rather than
  abstract percentages
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general EHR-industry background if audience familiarity with
  healthcare software is already low and not essential to the core thesis
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the opioid-kickback subplot more explicitly in the cold
  open if early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 5 (4:30) — most viewers won't expect a
  federal opioid investigation inside a liquidation-preference story
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "$157M raised" vs. "$100M sale" counter visible
  in a corner graphic from Segment 2 onward, so the math gap stays visually present before the
  waterfall segment explains exactly where it went
