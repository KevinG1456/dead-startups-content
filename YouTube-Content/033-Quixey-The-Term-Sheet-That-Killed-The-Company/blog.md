# Blog Article: Quixey Signed a Debt Deal With One Veto Clause in It. That Clause Killed the Company.

*All figures sourced to TechCrunch, CNBC, Bloomberg, Recode, Global Venturing, The Information, and
Mountain View Voice — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. This is a deal-structure and
governance story, not a fraud story — no individual was charged with anything. Co-founder Tomer
Kagan's role is stated precisely throughout: replaced as CEO in April 2016, but remained on the
board — he did not leave the company entirely.*

## Introduction
This channel's title library includes a batch idea about "the company that died of a term sheet" —
toxic terms as a signaling death sentence. Researching a real anchor for that pattern led to Quixey, an
app-search startup whose collapse turns out to be an unusually precise match: not vague "bad terms"
that spooked investors in the abstract, but one literal, contractual veto right, used exactly as
written, to block money every other investor had already agreed to provide.

## The Founding and the Climb
Quixey was founded in Mountain View, California, in 2009, by Tomer Kagan and Liron Shapira. The
company built a "functional" search engine for mobile apps — letting users search by what an app does,
rather than requiring them to already know its name.

Over the next several years, Quixey raised a $400,000 seed round led by Eric Schmidt's Innovation
Endeavors, a $3.8 million Series A in August 2011, a $20 million Series B in June 2012, and a roughly
$50 million Series C in 2013 that brought in Alibaba as an investor for the first time. In March 2015,
Quixey raised $60 million more, from Goldman Sachs, GGV Capital, Alibaba, and SoftBank, at a reported
valuation near $600 million — the company's peak.

## The Warning Signs
By early 2016, the trajectory had reversed. In February, several executives — reportedly including the
COO and CTO — left the company as it missed revenue targets. On April 8, 2016, co-founder and CEO
Tomer Kagan was replaced by Mark Lazar, a turnaround-focused executive brought in from outside; Kagan
remained on Quixey's board. In November 2016, Lazar was fired after roughly seven months, and John
Foster — previously CEO of Zed USA — became Quixey's third CEO in about a year.

## The Deal
In the middle of that leadership churn, on August 4, 2016, Quixey announced $30 million in new debt
financing. The company's public disclosure at the time did not detail the full terms. Later reporting
filled in the picture: the financing was actually a combined package from Alibaba — a $10 million
payment plus $30 million in debt, roughly $40 million total. That debt carried two specific terms: an
18-month repayment deadline, and a veto right giving Alibaba the power to block any future equity
financing round Quixey tried to raise.

This is the mechanism at the center of this story, and it's worth stating precisely: this wasn't a
case of one investor's terms being so aggressive that other investors quietly declined to
participate later. It was a literal, contractual right — held by one party — to stop a deal that
everyone else had already agreed to.

## The Rescue Round
With the 18-month clock running, Quixey began raising a new round in November 2016. By February 2017,
it had succeeded, at least on paper: existing investors, including Atlantic Bridge Capital, had agreed
to provide $10 million in rescue financing.

## The Veto
Alibaba exercised its contractual veto right and blocked the deal. Every other investor involved had
already said yes. That agreement wasn't enough. Days later, Quixey laid off approximately 50 employees
and permanently closed its Mountain View office. Alibaba's public statement on the closure: "Unfortunately,
the development of the company did not meet expectations and the board made a decision to end the
business."

## The Actual Lesson
Quixey's ending complicates a comforting assumption many founders make: that if you can get other
investors to agree to fund you, you're safe. That assumption breaks the moment an earlier, unrelated
deal has given someone else a veto over the whole decision. The same investor whose capital helped
Quixey reach a $600 million valuation was also the one whose contractual right ended it — not because
the company's other investors lost confidence, but because one investor's earlier terms made their
confidence irrelevant.

This channel has told two other cap-table mechanics stories: package 018 (Down Rounds: The Silent
Killer) and package 032 (Practice Fusion's liquidation-preference waterfall). Quixey is a third,
distinct mechanism — not a down round diluting existing holders, not a preference stack consuming
exit proceeds, but a veto right blocking a deal before it can even happen.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **A veto right in a debt deal isn't the same as a strong lead investor** — it's a specific,
  enforceable power that can override every other investor's agreement, not just influence it
- **An 18-month maturity paired with a veto right is a compounding risk** — it creates a hard deadline
  while simultaneously limiting the company's ability to meet it on its own terms
- **Leadership churn is a visible symptom, not the disease** — Quixey's three CEOs in a year were a
  warning sign, but the actual mechanism that ended the company was already signed into a contract
  months before the final CEO took over
- **"Other investors agreed" is not the same as "the deal is done"** — always check whether any party
  holds a consent or veto right that could override that agreement
- **A company's peak valuation says nothing about its deal-structure risk** — Quixey reached $600
  million and still had a single clause capable of ending it

## The Lesson
Quixey is a precise, real-world illustration of a risk that's easy to describe in the abstract and
easy to underestimate in practice: a veto right, signed into one deal, can silently determine the
outcome of every deal that comes after it — no matter how many other people say yes.
