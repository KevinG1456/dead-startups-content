# Script Package

Anchored on Quixey — full citations in `sources-log.txt`. THE SINGLE MOST IMPORTANT RULE FOR THIS
SCRIPT: state the mechanism precisely — Alibaba's August 2016 debt deal included a specific,
contractual veto right over future equity financing, and Alibaba used exactly that veto in February
2017 to block a rescue round every other investor had already agreed to. This is not a vague "toxic
terms scared investors away" story — be specific about the mechanism throughout. On Tomer Kagan: state
precisely that he was replaced as CEO in April 2016 but remained on the board — do not imply he left
the company entirely. No fraud or criminal allegations apply to anyone in this story; keep the tone
analytical, not accusatory.

## Complete Viral Script (Main Cut)

**Target length:** 12–15 minutes (founding, the rise, the leadership crisis, the debt deal, the
blocked rescue round, and the shutdown all covered in full)

### Cold Open (0:00–0:15)
- VO: "Every other investor in this company said yes to a rescue round. One investor's veto said no.
  The company was gone within days. Here's exactly how one line in a contract did that."
- Visual: scattered green checkmarks, hard cut to a large red X stamped over all of them
- Pattern Interrupt: hard cut to silence, white text on black: "This isn't a metaphor. One investor
  had a literal, contractual veto — and used it."

### Hook (0:15–1:00)
- VO: "This is Quixey — a 'functional' app-search company founded in 2009 by Tomer Kagan and Liron
  Shapira, letting users search for apps by what they do instead of what they're called. It raised
  roughly $173 million, peaking at a $600 million valuation in 2015. In February 2017, it shut down."
- Open loop: "By the end of this video, you'll understand the exact mechanism that ended this company
  — not a vague story about running out of money, but one specific clause, signed six months earlier,
  that gave a single investor the power to block every other investor's money."

### Introduction (1:00–1:40)
- Method statement: reconstructed from TechCrunch, CNBC, Bloomberg, Recode, Global Venturing, The
  Information, and Mountain View Voice — sourced on screen throughout
- Explicit framing, stated early and plainly: "This company had money on the table from other
  investors. It didn't matter. One earlier deal gave one investor a veto over that money — and that
  investor used it."

### Main Content — The Rise (1:40–3:30)
- **Segment 1 — The Founding (1:40–2:15):** Quixey founded 2009 in Mountain View by Tomer Kagan and
  Liron Shapira. Visual: generic startup-office establishing shot, no real branding.
- **Segment 2 — The Climb (2:15–3:30):** funding progression — $400K seed, $3.8M Series A (2011), $20M
  Series B (2012), ~$50M Series C (2013, first Alibaba investment), $60M round in 2015 at a ~$600
  million valuation, backed by Goldman Sachs, GGV Capital, Alibaba, and SoftBank. Visual: funding
  ladder graphic climbing to "$600M VALUATION — 2015."

### Main Content — The Warning Signs (3:30–5:15)
- **Segment 3 — Missed Targets (3:30–4:00):** February 2016, several executives — reportedly including
  the COO and CTO — leave as the company misses revenue targets. Visual: "EXECUTIVES DEPART — FEBRUARY
  2016" title card.
- **Segment 4 — Three CEOs (4:00–5:15):** April 8, 2016, co-founder and CEO Tomer Kagan is replaced by
  Mark Lazar, a turnaround CEO — Kagan stays on the board. November 2016, Lazar is fired after about
  seven months; John Foster becomes CEO — Quixey's third CEO in roughly a year. On-screen framing
  card: "Kagan stayed on the board. This isn't a story about a founder who left — it's a story about a
  contract term nobody outside the company could see." Visual: three-name CEO-succession graphic.

### Main Content — The Deal (5:15–8:00)
- **Segment 5 — The Debt Deal (5:15–7:00):** August 4, 2016, Quixey announces $30 million in debt
  financing. Reporting later revealed the fuller package: a $10 million payment plus $30 million in
  debt from Alibaba — roughly $40 million total — carrying two critical terms: an 18-month repayment
  deadline, AND a veto right giving Alibaba the power to block any future equity round Quixey tried to
  raise. On-screen framing card, held for a beat: "This is the mechanism at the center of this video.
  Not vague 'bad terms' — a specific, contractual veto." Visual: document graphic with one line
  highlighted in red: "VETO RIGHT — FUTURE EQUITY FINANCING."
- **Segment 6 — Racing the Clock (7:00–8:00):** November 2016, with the 18-month debt clock running,
  Quixey begins raising a new round to survive. Visual: countdown-style timeline graphic.

### Main Content — The Veto (8:00–10:30)
- **Segment 7 — The Rescue Round (8:00–9:00):** by February 2017, existing investors — including
  Atlantic Bridge Capital — had agreed to provide $10 million in rescue financing. Visual: green
  checkmark graphic building as each investor "agrees."
- **Segment 8 — The Veto (9:00–10:00):** Alibaba exercised its contractual veto right and blocked the
  agreed rescue round. On-screen framing card: "Every other investor said yes. This is the single
  clause that made their agreement not enough." Visual: large red X stamped over the checkmark
  graphic.
- **Segment 9 — The Shutdown (10:00–10:30):** forced out of options, Quixey laid off roughly 50
  employees and permanently closed its Mountain View office. Alibaba's public statement: "Unfortunately,
  the development of the company did not meet expectations and the board made a decision to end the
  business." Visual: "SHUTDOWN — FEBRUARY 2017" title card, Alibaba's quote card.
- Mid-video CTA (~10:15): "This channel has covered cap-table mechanics before — down rounds in
  package 018, liquidation preferences in package 032. This is a third mechanism: the investor veto.
  Subscribe for a new autopsy every week."

### The Actual Lesson (10:30–12:00)
- Plain-language comparison: a company can have real investor demand for a rescue round and still die,
  if an earlier, unrelated deal gave one party the contractual power to say no to everyone else.
- The core irony, stated directly: the same investor whose money helped the company reach a $600
  million valuation was also the one whose veto ended it.
- Visual: full-arc timeline graphic — founding (2009) → funding climb (2011-2015) → peak valuation
  (2015) → executive departures (Feb 2016) → CEO changes (Apr/Nov 2016) → debt deal with veto right
  (Aug 2016) → rescue round agreed (Feb 2017) → veto exercised (Feb 2017) → shutdown (Feb 2017).

### Final CTA + Outro (12:00–13:15)
- VO: recap the one-sentence lesson — a veto right buried in an earlier deal can override every other
  investor's agreement, no matter how much demand exists to save a company
- "Subscribe — I'm building a full library of these, and if you want the other cap-table mechanics
  stories on this channel, check out package 018 on down rounds and package 032 on liquidation
  preferences"
- Engagement prompt: "If you're evaluating a term sheet right now, does it give any single party a
  veto over your future financing? And do you think Quixey's board should have seen this coming when
  they signed the August 2016 deal? Tell me in the comments."
- End screen: package 032 (Practice Fusion) + package 018 (Down Rounds) + subscribe

## Alternative Scripts

### Script Version A — Data-First / Analyst Style
Opens directly on the completed funding ladder and valuation-to-shutdown graphic, then works backward
into the founding, the leadership crisis, and the veto mechanism. Measured, graphics-forward, treats
the video as a genuine case study in deal-structure risk rather than a company-history piece.

### Script Version B — Narrative / Documentary Style
Opens on the February 2017 rescue-round agreement itself — the relief, the sense that the company was
saved — before cutting hard to the veto and the shutdown days later, then rewinding to tell the full
story in order. Slower pacing, holds the veto-clause reveal until Segment 5, resolving with the actual
shutdown as the closing beat. Strongest tonal-irony arc of the three versions.

### Script Version C — Fast-Cut / Listicle Style
Structured as "the six months that ended this company," leading into rapid-fire beats: founding,
funding climb, executive departures, CEO churn, debt deal, rescue round, veto, shutdown — landing on
the veto-mechanism lesson as the final, clarifying beat. Strong Shorts-extraction potential, especially
the "everyone said yes, one said no" contrast.

## Audience Retention Optimization
- **Drop-off risk:** the leadership-churn segment (3:30–5:15) if the three-CEO sequence feels like a
  tangent before the main mechanism is revealed
- **Fix:** frame the CEO churn explicitly as a visible warning sign the audience can "see coming,"
  building anticipation for the deal-terms reveal in Segment 5 rather than treating it as a detour
- **Faster pacing opportunity:** trim general 2015-era funding-climate context if audience familiarity
  with startup fundraising basics is already high
- **Curiosity increase opportunity:** tease the veto mechanism more explicitly in the cold open if
  early-retention data shows drop-off before Segment 5 (5:15) — most viewers won't expect a single
  contract clause to be this decisive
- **Recommended visual change:** keep a small running "18-month clock" corner graphic visible from
  Segment 5 onward, counting down toward the February 2017 veto, so the mechanism's urgency stays
  visually present
