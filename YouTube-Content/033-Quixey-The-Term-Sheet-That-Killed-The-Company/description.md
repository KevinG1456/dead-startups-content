## Long Description
Every week on this channel, we reconstruct startup outcomes using primary sources. This week: Quixey —
a real, fully-sourced case study in what a veto right buried in a term sheet can actually do to a
company.

Quixey, a "functional" app-search engine that let users search by what an app does rather than its
name, was founded in 2009 in Mountain View by Tomer Kagan and Liron Shapira. The company raised
roughly $173.8 million over its life: a $400,000 seed round, a $3.8 million Series A (2011), a $20
million Series B (2012), a roughly $50 million Series C (2013, Alibaba's first investment), and a $60
million round in 2015 — backed by Goldman Sachs, GGV Capital, Alibaba, and SoftBank — at a peak
valuation near $600 million.

By early 2016, missed revenue targets triggered a leadership crisis. Several executives, reportedly
including the COO and CTO, left in February. On April 8, 2016, co-founder and CEO Tomer Kagan was
replaced by turnaround CEO Mark Lazar — Kagan stayed on the board. In November 2016, Lazar was fired
after about seven months, and John Foster became Quixey's third CEO in roughly a year.

In August 2016, in the middle of this leadership churn, Quixey took an Alibaba-led financing package:
a $10 million payment combined with $30 million in debt. That debt carried two critical terms — an
18-month repayment deadline, and a veto right giving Alibaba the power to block any future equity
financing round Quixey tried to raise.

By November 2016, racing that 18-month clock, Quixey began raising a new round to survive. By February
2017, other investors — including Atlantic Bridge Capital — had agreed to provide $10 million in
rescue financing. Alibaba exercised its contractual veto and blocked the deal. Days later, Quixey laid
off roughly 50 employees and permanently closed its Mountain View office. Alibaba's public statement:
"Unfortunately, the development of the company did not meet expectations and the board made a decision
to end the business."

This channel has covered cap-table mechanics before — down rounds in package 018, liquidation
preferences in package 032. This is a third, distinct mechanism: the investor veto right.
