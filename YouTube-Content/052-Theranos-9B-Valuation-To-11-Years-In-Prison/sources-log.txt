FOUNDING
- Theranos was founded in 2003 by Elizabeth Holmes, then 19 years old, who
  dropped out of Stanford's chemical engineering program to start the company.
- Original concept was a wearable patch that could administer medication and
  monitor blood; the company pivoted to a blood-testing device (branded
  "Edison," later "miniLab") claiming to run hundreds of standard lab tests
  from a few drops of finger-stick blood, without a traditional venous draw.
Source: Wikipedia ("Theranos," "Elizabeth Holmes"); Britannica; multiple
retrospective profiles (Forbes, Al Jazeera).

FUNDING AND VALUATION
- The SEC's own March 2018 complaint states Theranos raised more than $700
  million from investors. *** Use "$700 million+" as the safe, primary-source
  figure. Some aggregator/press retrospectives cite higher lifetime totals
  (up to ~$945 million cited in some secondary sources) — this could not be
  independently reconciled to a single primary-source figure in this
  research pass. [VERIFY] if a higher number is needed; default to the SEC's
  "$700 million+" for any on-screen claim. ***
- Peak valuation reached approximately $9 billion in 2013–2014, following a
  major funding round. At that valuation, Holmes — who held roughly half the
  company — was named by Forbes as the youngest self-made female billionaire
  on paper; Forbes revised her estimated net worth to $0 in 2016 once the
  fraud became public.
- Notable investors/board members: media mogul Rupert Murdoch invested $125
  million (the single largest individual investor, and the largest named
  recipient in the later criminal restitution order); the board included
  high-profile statesmen rather than scientists or clinicians, including
  Henry Kissinger, George Shultz, retired general James Mattis, and former
  senator Sam Nunn — a recurring point of criticism in retrospective coverage
  (notably John Carreyrou's book "Bad Blood").
Source: SEC Press Release 2018-41 and Litigation Release LR-24069 (sec.gov);
Forbes net-worth coverage; Wikipedia; CB Insights/biography retrospectives.

THE TECHNOLOGY AND THE CORE DECEPTION
- Theranos publicly claimed its proprietary "Edison" devices could run over
  200 different blood tests reliably from a few drops of finger-stick blood.
- In reality, per the Wall Street Journal's reporting (corroborated at trial
  by former lab employees), the Edison devices could reliably perform only a
  small fraction of the tests the company offered. The large majority of
  patient tests run through Theranos's Walgreens "Wellness Centers" were
  actually processed on modified, diluted samples run through conventional,
  commercially available third-party lab analyzers (including machines made
  by Siemens) — while Theranos represented the results to patients, doctors,
  and partners as coming from its own revolutionary technology.
Source: Wall Street Journal (John Carreyrou, Oct 15, 2015 and subsequent
reporting); trial testimony summarized by DOJ/court reporting (Courthouse
News, NPR, CBS News coverage of the 2021-2022 trial).

PARTNERSHIPS AND THEIR COLLAPSE
- Walgreens: partnership announced 2013; Theranos "Wellness Centers" were
  rolled out inside roughly 40 Walgreens stores, concentrated in the Phoenix,
  Arizona area, starting in 2013. Walgreens froze and then formally ended the
  relationship in 2016, after the WSJ reporting and a federal regulatory
  sanction (below). Walgreens was later named in the criminal restitution
  order as owed $40 million.
- Safeway: a 2010 deal under which Safeway spent approximately $350 million
  building in-store clinics across more than 800 stores to offer Theranos
  blood tests. The tests never launched at scale — Theranos repeatedly missed
  rollout deadlines — and Safeway quietly severed the partnership in November
  2015 without the clinics ever going fully live.
Source: Fierce Biotech, BioSpace, and CNBC reporting on the Safeway deal
collapse; TechCrunch and trial-testimony summaries (Marketplace, MassDevice)
on the Walgreens partnership; restitution figures per the May 2023 court
order (below).

THE EXPOSÉ (October 2015)
- October 15, 2015: Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published the
  first investigative story questioning Theranos's technology, after roughly
  ten months of reporting. The story drew on former employees, including lab
  director/pathologist sources, and revealed that Theranos was not primarily
  using its own proprietary devices to run patient tests, contrary to its
  public claims to investors, partners, and regulators.
- The story is widely credited as the beginning of Theranos's public
  unraveling; internal reaction inside the company was reportedly hostile to
  Carreyrou.
Source: Wall Street Journal (Carreyrou, Oct 15, 2015); Fast Company
retrospective on Theranos's response; The Ringer / Dark Daily interviews with
Carreyrou on sourcing.

REGULATORY ACTION
- Following inspections, the U.S. Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services
  (CMS) found deficiencies at Theranos's California lab that it determined
  posed "immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety." CMS revoked
  Theranos's CLIA laboratory certificate and banned Holmes from owning,
  operating, or directing a lab for two years (2016).
Source: CMS sanction widely reported contemporaneously (2016) and
subsequently cited in DOJ/SEC case materials and retrospective coverage.

THE SEC CIVIL CASE (March 14, 2018) — separate from the later criminal case
- The SEC charged Theranos, Holmes, and former president/COO Ramesh "Sunny"
  Balwani with what the agency called an "elaborate, years-long fraud,"
  alleging the company raised more than $700 million from investors through
  false and exaggerated statements about its technology, business, and
  financial performance.
- Holmes settled with the SEC without admitting or denying the allegations:
  she agreed to pay a $500,000 civil penalty, agreed to be barred from
  serving as an officer or director of any public company for 10 years,
  agreed to return the remaining 18.9 million shares she had obtained during
  the fraud, and agreed to relinquish voting control of Theranos by
  converting her super-majority Class B shares into ordinary Class A shares.
- The SEC's case against Balwani was NOT settled at this time and proceeded
  separately in litigation (he was later also criminally prosecuted — see
  below).
- *** This SEC settlement is a CIVIL matter, entirely separate from Holmes's
  later CRIMINAL conviction — keep the two distinct on screen: the 2018 SEC
  case ended in a civil settlement (no admission of wrongdoing, a fine and
  officer/director bar); the criminal case (below) ended in an actual jury
  conviction. ***
Source: SEC Press Release 2018-41 (sec.gov); SEC Litigation Release LR-24069
(sec.gov); contemporaneous coverage (WLRN/NPR affiliate, The D&O Diary).

DISSOLUTION — SEPTEMBER 2018 — NOT A BANKRUPTCY FILING
- Theranos defaulted on a $65 million loan from Fortress Investment Group
  after breaching a minimum-cash-level covenant.
- September 5, 2018: general counsel and CEO David Taylor emailed
  shareholders that the company would dissolve. *** Theranos did NOT file for
  Chapter 7 or Chapter 11 bankruptcy — it wound down through an "assignment
  for the benefit of creditors," an out-of-court alternative in which the
  company's non-IP assets were assigned to a third party in trust for
  creditors. State this precisely — "dissolved via an out-of-court wind-down,
  not a bankruptcy filing" — rather than defaulting to "filed for bankruptcy,"
  which would be inaccurate. ***
- Taylor's letter stated the company's remaining cash was "not nearly
  sufficient to pay all of the creditors in full" and that there would be "no
  distribution to shareholders" — shareholders received $0.
- Unsecured creditors were owed at least $60 million against roughly $5
  million in remaining cash; as part of the wind-down, Fortress obtained
  rights to Theranos's patent portfolio.
Source: CNN Business (Sept 5, 2018); Fortune (Sept 5, 2018, "Theranos Default
Dissolution"); NPR; TechCrunch.

THE CRIMINAL CASE
- Holmes and Balwani were indicted in June 2018 on federal wire fraud and
  conspiracy to commit wire fraud charges. Their trials were later severed
  (separated) after Holmes's legal team indicated she intended to present
  evidence that Balwani had been psychologically, emotionally, and (per her
  later trial testimony) sexually abusive and controlling toward her during
  their relationship and the company's operation. *** Balwani has denied
  these allegations. This is Holmes's own defense-trial account, not an
  independently adjudicated finding — attribute it as her testimony/claim if
  used, not as settled fact. ***
- Holmes's trial ran September 2021 to January 2022. On January 3, 2022, a
  federal jury convicted Holmes on four counts: one count of conspiracy to
  commit wire fraud against investors, and three individual counts of wire
  fraud against specific investors. The jury ACQUITTED Holmes on all four
  patient-related fraud counts (one conspiracy count and three individual
  counts related to defrauding patients). The jury could not reach a verdict
  on three additional investor-fraud counts (a partial mistrial on those
  counts; prosecutors later moved to dismiss them rather than retry).
- Balwani was tried separately, after Holmes. He was convicted on all 12
  counts he faced — *** unlike Holmes, Balwani's convictions covered BOTH
  investor fraud AND patient fraud counts; his conviction is broader than
  hers. Keep this distinction explicit whenever both are discussed. ***
- November 18, 2022: Holmes was sentenced to 135 months (11 years, 3 months)
  in federal prison, plus 3 years of supervised release.
- December 2022: Balwani was sentenced to 155 months (approximately 12 years,
  11 months) in federal prison — a longer sentence than Holmes's, consistent
  with his broader conviction.
- May 2023: U.S. District Judge Edward Davila ordered Holmes and Balwani to
  pay $452 million in restitution, jointly and severally liable, to victims
  of the fraud. Individual amounts named in reporting include $125 million to
  Rupert Murdoch and $40 million to Walgreens, among other investors.
- Holmes exhausted efforts to remain free pending appeal (the Ninth Circuit
  denied her bid) and reported to Federal Prison Camp Bryan, in Bryan, Texas,
  on May 30, 2023, after an earlier surrender date was pushed back.
- Balwani reported to begin his sentence around April 20, 2023.
- Subsequent Bureau of Prisons sentence-computation updates (reported by NBC
  News, CBS News, and others in 2023) reflected credit for good conduct and
  program completion, shortening Holmes's practical time to be served — her
  projected release date has been reported as on or around December 29,
  2032, which is roughly two years earlier than her original 11-year-3-month
  term would imply if served in full. *** This is a BOP administrative
  credit/computation matter, NOT a judicial resentencing or a clemency
  action — state it as "her projected release date, per Bureau of Prisons
  sentence computation, is now around Dec 2032," never as "her sentence was
  reduced by a judge" or conflated with the clemency petition below. ***
Source: U.S. Department of Justice, Northern District of California press
releases (justice.gov/usao-ndca — "Elizabeth Holmes Sentenced To More Than 11
Years..."); DOJ case page "U.S. v. Elizabeth Holmes, et al."; CBS News, NBC
News, ABC News, Fortune, and Washington Post sentencing/surrender coverage
(2022-2023); CBS News/NBC News/Scripps News (2023, sentence-computation/
release-date reduction reporting); CBS News San Francisco (May 2023,
$452 million restitution order).
