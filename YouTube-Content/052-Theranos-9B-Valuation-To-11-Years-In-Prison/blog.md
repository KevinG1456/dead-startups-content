## Introduction
Most people know the outline of the Theranos story: a young founder, a $9 billion valuation, a
blood-testing device that didn't work, a fall from grace. Far fewer know the actual legal mechanics —
that the SEC's 2018 case and the 2022 criminal case are two entirely different proceedings, that
Elizabeth Holmes was acquitted of every patient-related fraud charge in the same trial that
convicted her, or that her co-founder was convicted more broadly than she was. This article follows
the complete, documented sequence: the founding, the funding and board, the technology gap, the
partnerships, the exposé, the SEC case, the dissolution, the criminal case and its split verdict, the
sentencing, and the current, unresolved clemency petition — each fact sourced, and the most
legally and politically sensitive fact in the story handled with the same neutral, dated treatment as
every other fact in this piece.

## The Founding
Theranos was founded in 2003 by Elizabeth Holmes, then 19 years old, who dropped out of Stanford's
chemical engineering program to start the company. The original idea was a wearable patch that could
administer medication and monitor blood; the company pivoted to a blood-testing device — eventually
branded "Edison," later "miniLab" — built around one extraordinary claim: it could run more than 200
standard lab tests from just a few drops of finger-stick blood, without a traditional venous draw.

## The Money and the Board
Theranos raised more than $700 million from investors — the figure stated in the SEC's own 2018
complaint. The company's valuation peaked at approximately $9 billion in 2013-2014. At that
valuation, Holmes — who held roughly half the company — was named on paper the youngest self-made
female billionaire; Forbes later revised that estimate to $0. The board included Henry Kissinger,
George Shultz, retired four-star general James Mattis, and former senator Sam Nunn — a board of
statesmen, not scientists or clinicians, a detail frequently cited in retrospective analysis of how
the fraud went unchecked for so long. Rupert Murdoch alone invested $125 million, the single largest
individual investment in the company, and later the largest named recipient in the criminal
restitution order.

## What the Technology Actually Did
The claim was that Edison devices could run more than 200 tests from a finger-prick. The reality, per
Wall Street Journal reporting later corroborated by trial testimony from former lab employees, was
that the devices could reliably run only a small fraction of the tests Theranos offered. The large
majority of patient tests processed through the company's Walgreens "Wellness Centers" were actually
run on modified, diluted samples through conventional, commercially available third-party lab
machines — while Theranos represented the results to patients, doctors, partners, and regulators as
coming from its own proprietary technology.

## The Partnerships
Safeway signed a deal in 2010 and spent approximately $350 million building in-store clinics across
more than 800 stores. The blood-testing rollout never launched at scale — Theranos repeatedly missed
deadlines — and Safeway quietly ended the partnership in November 2015. Walgreens rolled Theranos
"Wellness Centers" into roughly 40 stores, concentrated around Phoenix, Arizona, starting in 2013;
Walgreens froze and then formally ended the relationship in 2016, after federal inspectors found
deficiencies posing "immediate jeopardy to patient health and safety" and Theranos's lab
certification was revoked.

## The Exposé
On October 15, 2015, Wall Street Journal reporter John Carreyrou published the first investigative
story questioning Theranos's technology, after roughly ten months of reporting drawing on former
employees. The story revealed that Theranos was not primarily using its own proprietary devices to
run patient tests, contrary to its public claims — widely credited as the beginning of the company's
public unraveling.

## The SEC's Civil Case
On March 14, 2018, the SEC charged Theranos, Holmes, and former president Ramesh "Sunny" Balwani
with an "elaborate, years-long fraud," alleging the company raised more than $700 million through
false and exaggerated statements. Holmes settled — without admitting or denying the allegations — for
a $500,000 civil penalty, a 10-year bar from serving as an officer or director of a public company,
the return of 18.9 million shares, and the surrender of her voting control. This is a CIVIL matter,
entirely separate from the CRIMINAL case that followed — this article keeps the two explicitly
distinct throughout, the same way it should always be kept distinct in any retelling of this story.
Balwani's SEC case was not settled at this point and proceeded separately.

## The Company Ends — Without Bankruptcy
Theranos defaulted on a $65 million loan from Fortress Investment Group after breaching a
minimum-cash covenant. On September 5, 2018, general counsel David Taylor emailed shareholders that
the company would dissolve. This was NOT a bankruptcy filing — Theranos wound down through an
out-of-court "assignment for the benefit of creditors," with non-IP assets assigned to a third party
in trust for creditors. Shareholders received $0; unsecured creditors were owed at least $60 million
against roughly $5 million in remaining cash; Fortress obtained rights to Theranos's patents as part
of the wind-down.

## The Criminal Case and the Split Verdict
Holmes and Balwani were indicted in June 2018 on federal wire fraud and conspiracy charges; their
trials were later severed. Holmes's defense indicated she would present evidence that Balwani had
been psychologically, emotionally, and — per her own trial testimony — sexually abusive and
controlling toward her; this is her account and testimony, not an independently adjudicated fact, and
Balwani has denied it. Holmes's trial ran September 2021 to January 2022. On January 3, 2022, the
jury convicted her on one count of conspiracy to commit wire fraud against investors and three
individual investor wire-fraud counts — and ACQUITTED her on all four patient-related fraud counts.
The jury hung on three additional investor counts, later dismissed by prosecutors rather than
retried. Balwani, tried separately, was convicted on all 12 counts he faced — both investor and
patient-related counts, a broader conviction than Holmes's.

## Sentencing and Restitution
On November 18, 2022, Holmes was sentenced to 135 months (11 years, 3 months) in federal prison, plus
3 years of supervised release. Balwani was sentenced in December 2022 to 155 months (nearly 13
years) — longer than Holmes's, consistent with his broader conviction. In May 2023, a federal judge
ordered $452 million in restitution, Holmes and Balwani jointly and severally liable, including $125
million to Rupert Murdoch and $40 million to Walgreens among other named investors.

## Prison, and What's Happening Right Now
Holmes reported to Federal Prison Camp Bryan, Texas, on May 30, 2023, after exhausting efforts to
remain free during appeal. Bureau of Prisons sentence-computation credit later shortened her
practical time to be served — her projected release date has been reported around December 2032, an
administrative BOP matter, not a resentencing. In 2025, Holmes's team filed a formal clemency petition
with the Trump administration; as of the most recent reporting found for this article (January 2026),
the Office of the Pardon Attorney lists the petition as PENDING. No decision — grant, denial, or
otherwise — has been reported. This article states only that: a petition was filed, in 2025, and it
remains undecided as of the most recent available reporting. It does not speculate on what will
happen, and it does not frame this as a statement about the broader justice system.

## The Actual Lesson
HeadSpin (package 005), Ozy Media (package 029), and Theranos are this channel's three real,
adjudicated startup-fraud convictions. Manish Lachwani pleaded guilty and served his sentence in
full. Carlos Watson was convicted by a jury and had his sentence commuted before serving a day.
Elizabeth Holmes was convicted by a jury, is currently serving her sentence, and has an undecided
clemency petition pending. Same standard of proof across all three — a real conviction, not an
allegation — three different points in three different outcomes. A board full of famous names is not
due diligence, and a company can reach a $9 billion valuation on a technology that mostly doesn't do
what it claims — right up until someone actually checks.

## What Founders and Investors Should Take From This
- **A prestigious board is not technical due diligence** — Theranos's board included two former
  Secretaries of State and a four-star general, and none of them caught what junior lab employees and
  a skeptical reporter eventually did
- **A civil settlement and a criminal conviction are not the same event** — the SEC's 2018 case with
  Holmes ended in a fine and a bar, with no admission of wrongdoing; the 2022 criminal case ended in
  an actual jury conviction; conflating the two misrepresents both
- **A split verdict is still a verdict worth understanding in full** — Holmes was convicted on
  investor-related counts and acquitted on patient-related counts in the same trial; reducing this to
  a single "guilty" or "not guilty" flattens a real, documented legal outcome
- **A company's ending isn't always a bankruptcy filing** — Theranos dissolved through an
  out-of-court wind-down, a distinction worth knowing when evaluating what "the company died" actually
  means in any given case

## The Lesson
Elizabeth Holmes was criminally charged, tried, and convicted by a federal jury of wire fraud and
conspiracy to commit wire fraud against investors — a fact, not an allegation. She was also acquitted
on every patient-related count in that same trial — an equally real, equally documented fact that
most retellings of this story leave out. Both are true. Both are stated here plainly, sourced, and
without editorializing — along with the honest, current status of her still-pending clemency
petition.
