# Blog Article: HeadSpin — The Unicorn That Faked Its Revenue 4x

*Anchored to a legally resolved case: Manish Lachwani pleaded guilty April 23, 2023 (N.D. Cal.) to two counts of wire fraud and one count of securities fraud; the SEC filed civil charges August 26, 2021. Figures sourced to the SEC litigation release, DOJ press releases, and contemporaneous reporting citing those filings.*

## Introduction
Most startup failures in this series come down to bad unit economics, legal risk, or a strategy that didn't adapt in time. This one is different: HeadSpin, a real, working SaaS company, raised $117 million and reached a $1.1 billion valuation on annual recurring revenue figures its founder later admitted, in a guilty plea, were fabricated. This article reconstructs the real numbers, based on the SEC complaint and DOJ court filings.

## What Was Claimed
HeadSpin — founded in 2015, providing mobile app testing infrastructure on real physical devices across real carrier networks — told investors during 2019 fundraising that it had approximately $54 million in annual recurring revenue. That figure was central to the growth story behind the company's eventual ~$1.1 billion valuation in a 2020 round involving ICONIQ Capital and Dell Technologies Capital, part of $117 million raised in total since founding ($100 million-plus of it between April 2017 and April 2020, per the DOJ).

## What the Record Shows Actually Happened
According to the SEC's complaint, HeadSpin's board later determined the company's actual year-end 2019 ARR was roughly $10 million — a gap of roughly $44 million versus what was claimed. Reporting on the broader 2018–2020 scheme describes the inflation as running to nearly fourfold. The SEC complaint describes the mechanism directly: founder Manish Lachwani created fake invoices and altered real ones to show higher billed amounts, counted revenue from prospective customers who had never actually agreed to pay, continued counting revenue from customers who had already churned, and falsely told investors that firms including Apple and American Express were HeadSpin customers.

## How It Passed Diligence
HeadSpin had no CFO, no HR department, and was never audited during the period the fraud occurred. When investor ICONIQ Capital pushed for stronger financial controls, reporting describes a resulting rift — Lachwani reportedly considered returning ICONIQ's investment rather than accept the oversight. In the absence of an independent audit or finance function, the company's own founder-supplied figures went largely unverified by any internal check, and investor diligence relied significantly on data the company itself provided.

## How It Was Discovered
An employee whistleblower raised concerns directly to HeadSpin's board in early 2020. The board launched an internal investigation, Lachwani stepped down as CEO, and the investigation's findings — including the revised, far lower revenue figures — led the company to mark its own internal valuation down from $1.1 billion to $300 million.

## The Legal Outcome
The SEC filed civil securities fraud charges against Lachwani on August 26, 2021 (the SEC's own release describes an "$80 million fraud," referring to the investor funding obtained through the scheme — a figure distinct from the ARR numbers above). Lachwani pleaded guilty on April 23, 2023, in the Northern District of California, before Judge Charles Breyer, to two counts of wire fraud and one count of securities fraud. He was sentenced in April 2024 to 18 months in prison, three years of supervised release, a $1,000,000 fine, and restitution reported at $8.6 million. Notably, HeadSpin the company was not penalized by the SEC — its cooperation after discovering the fraud helped it avoid company-level penalties; this was prosecuted as an individual founder's crime, not a company-wide conspiracy.

## What Happened to the Company
HeadSpin kept operating under new leadership, with real — if far smaller — revenue: $21 million in 2023, and roughly $5 million in the first quarter of 2024. In July 2024, the company was sold to Canadian private equity firm PartnerOne for a reported $28.2 million, about 1.4 times revenue and roughly 2.5% of its $1.1 billion peak valuation. Most former HeadSpin employees received nothing for their stock options, vested or unvested.

## The Lesson
This case is a reminder that revenue figures provided directly by a company — however credible the narrative, however sophisticated the investors — are not automatically verified facts. HeadSpin's product was real and functional; the fraud was entirely in the reporting layer around it, which is arguably a harder pattern to catch than a fake product, because everything an outside observer can see and test still works. For investors, the practical lesson is to build independent verification (bank statements, customer reference calls, contract sampling, a real audit requirement) into diligence rather than relying solely on company-provided reporting — especially at a company with no CFO and no audit function. For founders, it's a reminder that the consequences of fabricating growth metrics extend far beyond a failed round — they can be criminal, individually, even when the underlying business survives.

**6. How did investors miss this?**

Investors including Google Ventures, ICONIQ Capital, Dell Technologies Capital, Battery Ventures, Felicis, and Tiger Global collectively put in $117 million; the company was later sold for $28.2 million — a near-total loss relative to the peak $1.1 billion valuation.

**12. Is this an isolated case, or does this happen often?**
Startup revenue fraud cases are relatively rare in absolute terms but recur periodically; this case is notable for its scale ($1.1B peak valuation) and for the unusually clear, well-documented mechanism.

**13. What is ARR?**
Annual Recurring Revenue — the predictable, subscription-based revenue a company expects over a 12-month period.

**14. How can investors verify ARR claims independently?**
Common methods include reviewing bank statements, contacting a sample of customers directly, requesting underlying contracts, and using third-party audit or verification services.

**15. Is revenue fraud a criminal offense?**
It can be, particularly when it involves securities fraud — misrepresenting material financial information to investors in connection with the sale of securities, as in this case.

**16. What agency investigated this case?**
Both the SEC (civil charges) and the DOJ (criminal charges) were involved.

**17. What's the difference between an aggressive projection and fraud?**
A projection is a forward-looking estimate presented as such; fraud involves misrepresenting current or historical facts as true when they are known to be false — as with HeadSpin's fabricated invoices and phantom customer deals.

**18. Could this have been caught earlier?**
An earlier audit requirement or a CFO function likely would have surfaced the discrepancy sooner — HeadSpin had neither during the period the fraud occurred.

**19. Did HeadSpin's board know about the fraud?**
No — reporting indicates the board discovered the issue only after an employee whistleblower raised concerns in early 2020, after which the board launched its own investigation.
