# Blog Article: Color Labs Raised $41 Million Before Launch. It Sold for $7 Million, for Its Patents, Not Its Product.

## Introduction
"Build it and they will come" rarely works, and "build it for everyone" almost never does. Color Labs
is one of the clearest, most expensive real illustrations of that second failure mode: a well-funded,
well-staffed team that built a genuinely novel product, launched it to real initial excitement, and
never once had a clear answer to the single question every product actually needs answered — who,
specifically, is this for?

## The Pre-Launch Funding
Color Labs was founded by Bill Nguyen — a serial entrepreneur whose previous company, Lala, had been
acquired by Apple in 2009 — alongside co-founder Peter Pham. Before Color's product ever launched, the
company raised $41 million: $25 million from Sequoia Capital, $9 million from Bain Capital, and $7
million in venture debt from Silicon Valley Bank. That's a striking amount of capital committed to an
entirely unproven, unlaunched concept.

## The Product
Color's core idea was "elastic," proximity-based photo sharing. Open the app, and you'd automatically
see photos taken by any other Color user physically near you — no friend requests, no follows, no
specified relationship required at all. It was a genuinely novel technical concept. It was also, by
design, built for "everyone nearby" — which is not a customer segment, a use case, or a reason to open
the app twice.

## The Launch and the Collapse
Color launched publicly in March 2011 and drove over a million downloads in its first weeks. It also
drove immediate, widespread confusion: users didn't understand what problem the app solved for them,
or why they'd want to keep using it. By September 2011 — six months after launch — monthly active
users had fallen below 100,000. Co-founder Peter Pham had already left the company in June, less than
three months after the product went live.

## "Thrilled"
On September 18, 2011, at TechCrunch Disrupt, Sequoia partner and Color board member Doug Leone told
the audience Sequoia was "thrilled" to be an investor in Color, and teased "stay tuned" for a pivot
already underway internally, codenamed "Blue." The comment is worth sitting with: it was made in
public, on the record, while the first product's user base was already collapsing.

## The Pivot
That pivot became "Color for Facebook" — a mobile app for broadcasting live 30-second video clips
directly to a user's Facebook Wall. It's a different product in a different format, but it carried the
exact same underlying flaw: no specific, defined answer to who it was for, or why they'd choose it over
any alternative. It also failed to gain a significant, retained audience.

## The Acqui-Hire and the Shutdown
In October 2012, reports surfaced that Apple was acquiring Color Labs. The deal was confirmed in
November: an "acqui-hire" reported at approximately $7 million — roughly 17% of the $41 million Color
had raised. Apple's stated interest was the company's engineering team and its patent portfolio, which
covered device-to-device content sharing and an HD video file format, reportedly intended to strengthen
Apple's own FaceTime product. Not Color's actual app, which was officially shut down on December 31,
2012.

## The Actual Lesson
Color Labs had real money, real engineering talent, and — at least for a few weeks in March 2011 —
real curiosity from a real audience. What it never had, in either version of the product, was a
specific person it was built for. "Everyone nearby" describes a technical mechanism, not a customer.
And the pivot didn't fix that; it just relocated the same unanswered question into a new format,
proving the problem was never really about photos or video specifically — it was about never defining
who the product was for in the first place.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **"Everyone" is not a customer segment** — a product built for the widest possible audience often
  ends up mattering to none of them specifically
- **Pre-launch funding doesn't buy you a customer, only time to find one** — Color's $41 million
  bought a launch and a pivot, not an audience
- **A pivot fixes a positioning problem only if it actually changes the answer to "who is this for"**
  — Color's pivot changed the format without changing the answer
- **Real initial downloads can mask a real positioning problem for a little while** — Color's million-
  download launch created a false signal of demand that six months of retention data corrected
- **An acquisition isn't always validation of the product** — Apple's acqui-hire was explicitly for
  talent and patents, with the actual product discontinued, not continued

## The Lesson
Color Labs is a genuinely rare thing: a startup failure with a clean, precise, dollar-figure-backed
answer to "what went wrong." It wasn't underfunded. It wasn't understaffed. It never solved the one
problem that actually mattered — deciding, specifically, who it was building for — and no amount of
capital or engineering talent substituted for that decision, twice.
