# Blog Article: Google Erased $1.2 Billion In One Afternoon. A Startup Died Slower — To Google and Two Others.

*All figures sourced to public reporting — see `sources-log.txt` for the full citation map. Where a source could not be independently verified (part of Picturelife's causal narrative), it's flagged inline rather than stated as confirmed fact.*

## Introduction
This video started as a plan for five case studies of "platform risk" — moments when a much larger platform launches a free version of something a smaller company charges for. Real sourcing only supported two. The other three candidate categories (paid web analytics vs. free Google Analytics, paid cloud storage vs. Google Drive's pricing moves, email productivity tools vs. native Gmail features) turned up no named company whose death is reliably and directly attributed to a specific Google decision, once checked against real reporting rather than general "the market got harder" narratives. That's disclosed here rather than hidden, because forcing a fifth or third weak example would undermine exactly the kind of rigor this channel is supposed to model. What's left are two real, well-documented cases that illustrate platform risk at two different speeds.

## Case One: Garmin and TomTom — The Fast Price

### The Industry
Through the 2000s, standalone GPS navigation devices were a real, profitable hardware category. Garmin and TomTom were its two biggest publicly-traded names, both built substantially around dedicated hardware that customers bought once, typically for $200 or more.

### The Announcement
On October 28, 2009, Google announced Google Maps Navigation — free, built directly into Android phones, offering spoken turn-by-turn directions that matched or beat what a dedicated device did. There was no new hardware to buy, no separate purchase; if you had an Android phone, you had it.

### The Same Day
The market didn't wait to see how this would play out. The same trading day, TomTom's stock fell roughly 20-21%, and Garmin's fell roughly 16% — wiping out approximately $1.2 billion of Garmin's market capitalization in a single session.

**An important disclosure:** Garmin and TomTom are publicly-traded hardware companies, not VC-funded startups. This is a deliberate exception to how this channel usually works, included because the causal mechanism here — a platform launches a free version, and the market reprices an entire category in hours — is the single cleanest, most quantifiable example of the video's core lesson anywhere in this research, even though the company type doesn't match the channel's usual subject.

## Case Two: Picturelife — The Slow Price

### The Startup
Picturelife was a real, funded startup: a cloud photo backup and organization app founded by Charles Forman (also the founder of OMGPOP), which raised $4.6 million from investors including Spark Capital, SV Angel, Chris Dixon, and Founder Collective. It was acquired by Streamnation in early 2015.

### Free, Everywhere, At Once
Starting around 2015, cloud photo and file storage went free or nearly free across several major platforms at roughly the same time: Google Photos launched free unlimited storage in May 2015, Apple expanded iCloud's tiers, and Amazon offered aggressive storage pricing of its own. Unlike Garmin and TomTom's single-day shock, this was a gradual, multi-front erosion of the reason a customer would pay for a dedicated photo-backup product.

### The Shutdown
Picturelife shut down on August 22, 2016. Its own stated reason, per contemporaneous coverage, was "a challenging economic environment and a decreasing quality of service" — vague corporate language rather than a specific attribution to any one competitor.

**An honest complication:** this article does not claim "Google killed Picturelife." The available reporting on the specific causal mechanism — that Picturelife struggled to convert free users to paying customers while Google, Apple, and Amazon all offered free alternatives — comes from a secondary retrospective source that could not be fully independently verified in this research pass. The funding, acquisition, and shutdown facts above are confirmed by multiple sources; the precise causal story is the weaker-sourced part, and is presented as the most plausible available account, not as settled fact.

## What This Research Also Ruled Out
Getting to these two cases required checking and rejecting several other candidates:
- **Loom.com** (an old photo/video backup app, unrelated to the current screen-recording company of the same name) and **Everpix** both shut down before Google Photos' 2015 free-unlimited launch — they can't have been caused by an event that hadn't happened yet.
- **ClickTracks**, a paid web analytics tool, merged into Lyris in 2006 and continued operating for years afterward — not a Google-caused death, despite the free Google Analytics launch happening around the same era.
- **Bitcasa**, a cloud storage startup, died from its own "unlimited" plan being abused by a small number of extremely heavy users, not from Google Drive's pricing — its own reporting is explicit about this.
- **Dash Navigation**, a VC-funded GPS startup that would have been a much better niche fit than Garmin and TomTom, was acquired by RIM in June 2009 — four months before Google's announcement. Its decline predates the event it might otherwise illustrate.
- **Mailbox**, an email productivity app owned by Dropbox, was shut down for Dropbox's own stated strategic reasons (refocusing on Dropbox Paper), not because of any specific Gmail feature.

## The Actual Lesson: Platform Risk Has Two Speeds
Garmin and TomTom show what happens when a platform's decision reprices an entire category in hours — the market doesn't wait for anyone to adapt, and the cost is immediate and legible: you can point to the exact day and the exact number. Picturelife shows the slower version: several platforms independently deciding roughly the same thing over a longer window, with no single moment to point to, just an eroding reason for a customer to keep paying.

The practical difference matters for anyone building near a larger platform's roadmap today. The fast version is at least visible in hindsight — a specific announcement, a specific day. The slow version is harder to fight precisely because there's no single villain and no single moment of decision; by the time the pattern is obvious, the market has usually already moved.

## What Founders Should Take From This
- **Identify what you're actually charging for** that a larger platform could plausibly give away — that's the specific risk, not "competing with big companies" generally
- **Watch for simultaneous moves across multiple platforms**, not just one — Picturelife's story shows that the slow version of this risk often comes from several directions at once, which makes it easy to rationalize any single one as survivable
- **Don't expect a single moment of clarity** for the slow version the way Garmin and TomTom got one on October 28, 2009 — plan for erosion, not just for a single shock

## The Lesson
Whether it happens in an afternoon or over several years, the mechanism is the same: charging for something a larger platform can plausibly give away is a real, structural risk, not a hypothetical one. Garmin and TomTom's $1.2 billion single-day loss and Picturelife's slower, multi-platform decline are two real, differently-paced proofs of the same underlying fact.

**21. What should founders take from the Garmin/TomTom story specifically?**
